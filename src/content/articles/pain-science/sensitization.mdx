<Lede>After an injury the nervous system turns up its own gain, so ordinary inputs produce more pain and the painful area spreads. That is normal and protective in the short term. When it outlasts healing it becomes the main problem, and it is why pain can persist long after tissue has recovered.</Lede>

<Callout type="rose" title="This describes a mechanism, not your diagnosis">
Sensitization is a general property of nervous systems. Whether it is the dominant driver of any particular person's pain is a clinical judgement that no article can make, and persistent pain can also have tissue causes that need assessment. The [plan](/plan/) lists the red flags that end self-direction regardless of how well a mechanism seems to fit; read them first.
</Callout>

## The gain control

The [pain vs damage article](/learn/pain-science/pain-vs-damage/) made the case that pain is a protective output computed from many inputs. Sensitization is what happens when the computation changes its own settings. In the hours and days after a tissue is injured, the nerve endings in it become more responsive, and the circuits in the spinal cord and brain that handle their signals become easier to trigger and harder to quieten. The result is a system in which a given input now produces a larger output <Source id="moseley-butler-explain-pain" />.

You have felt this. Sunburned skin hurts under a warm shower that would normally feel pleasant; a sprained ankle hurts to a touch that would not register on the other foot. Those are the two signatures of sensitization: things that used to hurt now hurt more, and things that did not hurt now do. A third is that the painful area grows beyond the injured tissue, because the circuits being amplified handle input from a wider territory than the injury itself.

In the short term this is good design. A system that over-protects a healing tissue for a couple of weeks costs little and prevents re-injury. The gain is supposed to come back down as healing proceeds and the evidence of threat fades.

## When the gain stays up

Sometimes it does not come down. The injury heals on the timescale tissues heal on, weeks for most, but the amplified processing persists, and now the same loading that would be harmless in an unsensitized system continues to produce pain. The Explain Pain authors describe this as the system having learned pain: the circuits have been rehearsed, the protective meaning has been reinforced, and the output no longer tracks the tissue <Source id="moseley-butler-explain-pain" />.

What keeps the gain up is not mysterious, and it is mostly the other inputs from the previous article. Fear of movement means the movement is rarely tried, so the system never collects evidence that it is safe. Beliefs about a damaged or unstable spine keep the threat evaluation high. Poor sleep, stress and low mood each lower the threshold at which the system decides protection is warranted. Repeated flares, each interpreted as further damage, rehearse the association between the movement and the alarm. None of these are tissue, and all of them feed back into the pain.

This is one reason why persistent low back pain is so poorly served by treatments aimed at the tissue. The Lancet series documents how much imaging, injection and surgery is delivered for non-specific back pain and how little of it the guidelines support <Source id="lancet-2018-lbp-series" />. If the dominant driver is an amplified nervous system rather than a damaged disc, repairing the disc was never going to help, and telling the patient their disc is the problem may make the sensitization worse.

## What turns the gain back down

The inputs that keep the gain up are also the levers for bringing it down, which is why treatment for persistent pain looks so different from treatment for an acute injury.

**Reconceptualising the pain.** Teaching the sensitization model directly, so that the person stops reading each twinge as damage, lowers the threat input. The pain neuroscience education trials reviewed by Louw and colleagues measured exactly this: reductions in catastrophising and fear of movement, with smaller effects on pain intensity and the best results when education was combined with movement <Source id="louw-2016-pne" />.

**Graded re-exposure to feared movement.** Sensitized circuits are unlearned the way they were learned, through repeated experience, but this time of the movement being tolerated. Loads and ranges that were avoided are reintroduced in steps small enough to be completed, so the system collects safety evidence instead of threat evidence. The [graded exposure article](/learn/pain-science/graded-exposure/) covers how this is dosed.

**Addressing the amplifiers.** Sleep, stress and mood are treated as part of the pain problem rather than as separate issues, because they are inputs.

Cognitive functional therapy puts these three together in one package, and the RESTORE trial is the strongest single test of the approach: a 492-person randomised trial in people with chronic, disabling back pain, with large and durable reductions in activity limitation at one year compared with usual care <Source id="kent-2023-restore" />. The rehab track returns to this trial, and to what it does and does not show, in its guidelines articles.

## Reading your own flares with this in mind

For a lifter in rehab, sensitization explains several things that are otherwise confusing.

It explains why a flare after a set is not proof the set caused damage. A sensitized system will produce pain in response to loads well inside what the tissue tolerates, and the pain can arrive hours later as the system reviews the day. The self-coaching track asks for the flare to be logged with sleep, stress and the previous days' load alongside the set, because any of those could be the input that tipped the evaluation.

It explains why a slow, consistent return to loading tends to work and a cautious, inconsistent one tends not to. Each completed session is safety evidence; each session skipped out of fear is a missed opportunity to collect it, and each session abandoned at the first twinge rehearses the alarm.

And it explains why the [plan](/plan/) treats pain-free completion as a gate rather than treating pain as a stop signal. A gate asks whether the system tolerated the load, which is the question that matters. A stop signal asks whether anything hurt, which a sensitized system will answer yes to for reasons unconnected to the tissue.

None of this means a flare is always sensitization. It means a flare is ambiguous, that the ambiguity is the normal condition for persistent pain, and that the red flags exist precisely to catch the cases where it is not ambiguous at all.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Sensitization is the nervous system raising its own gain.** After injury, nerve endings and central circuits become easier to trigger, so ordinary inputs produce more pain.
2. **Its signatures are predictable.** Things that hurt now hurt more, things that did not hurt now do, and the painful area spreads beyond the injury.
3. **Short-term sensitization is protective.** It over-guards a healing tissue for a few weeks and is supposed to resolve with healing.
4. **Persistent sensitization outlasts the tissue.** Fear, beliefs, poor sleep, stress and rehearsed flares keep the gain up long after healing.
5. **The levers are the same inputs.** Reconceptualising pain, graded re-exposure and treating the amplifiers; cognitive functional therapy packages all three.
6. **A flare is ambiguous, not a verdict.** Log it with its context, keep collecting safety evidence, and use the red flags to catch the cases that are not ambiguous.

</KeyList>

<Fragment slot="answer">
Pain is computed from inputs through a system whose sensitivity is adjustable, and sensitization raises that sensitivity so the identical input produces a larger output. Fear, beliefs, sleep, stress and rehearsed flares can raise the gain with no tissue change at all. Why it matters: it means a flare is evidence about the state of the whole system, not a measurement of the tissue, and should be logged and interpreted that way.

Why would a protective mechanism that is useful after an injury become the main problem later? Explain what has to go wrong for that to happen.
<Fragment slot="answer">
Short-term sensitization is cheap insurance while tissue heals, and it is supposed to resolve as the threat evidence fades. It becomes the problem when other inputs keep the threat evaluation high after healing: movements are avoided so no safety evidence arrives, beliefs about a fragile spine persist, and each flare is read as damage and rehearses the alarm. Why it matters: the thing to treat is then the amplification and its inputs, which is why tissue-directed treatments for persistent non-specific back pain perform poorly.
