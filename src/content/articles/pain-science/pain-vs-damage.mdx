<Lede>Pain is an output the nervous system produces when it concludes the body needs protecting. Tissue state is one input among many, which is why pain and damage can come apart in both directions. Understanding that is itself a treatment with measurable, if modest, effects.</Lede>

<Callout type="rose" title="General claim, not a verdict on your back">
This article explains how pain works in general. It cannot tell you whether your own pain is or is not coming from damaged tissue; nobody can from a web page. If you have any of the red flags listed in the [plan](/plan/), such as numbness in the saddle area, worsening leg weakness, or a change in bladder or bowel control, stop reading and see a clinician the same day.
</Callout>

## The intuitive model and where it fails

Most people carry an intuitive model of pain that works like a smoke alarm wired to the tissue: damage happens, a signal travels up the wire, the brain reads the signal off a dial. More damage, more pain. Less pain, less damage. The model is wrong in a way that matters, and the clearest way to see it is to look at the cases where it fails.

It fails in one direction when there is tissue change and no pain. The [asymptomatic degeneration article](/learn/pathology/degeneration-and-asymptomatic-findings/) covers the imaging data: disc degeneration, bulges and even protrusions are common findings in people who have never had back pain, and they get more common with every decade of age <Source id="brinjikji-2015-meta" />. Whatever those findings are, they are not a reliable pain source, or everybody who has them would hurt.

It fails in the other direction when there is pain and no evident tissue cause. Most low back pain is non-specific, meaning no structure can be confidently blamed for it even after careful examination, and the Lancet series describes it as the leading cause of disability in the world <Source id="lancet-2018-lbp-series" />. Phantom limb pain is the extreme case: the limb that hurts is not there.

A smoke alarm reading a dial cannot produce either of these. Whatever pain is, it is not a damage meter.

## What pain is instead

The alternative model, developed over the last few decades and popularised as "Explain Pain", treats pain as a protective output rather than a damage readout <Source id="moseley-butler-explain-pain" />. The nervous system continuously weighs evidence about whether the body is in danger. Signals from the tissue, called nociception, are one line of evidence, and often an important one. But they are evaluated alongside everything else the system knows: what happened last time this movement was tried, what a clinician or a website said, how threatening the situation feels, how tired or stressed or afraid the person is, and what they believe the sensation means. Pain is produced when the overall judgement is that protection is needed. It is felt in the body because that is where protection has to act.

Two consequences follow directly from this.

First, nociception is neither necessary nor sufficient for pain. A tissue can send danger signals that never become pain because the context says there is nothing to fear; soldiers and athletes finishing a task with injuries they only notice afterwards are the classic examples. And pain can be produced with little or no nociception when the context is threatening enough.

Second, pain is real regardless of its cause. This is the point the model is most often misread on. Saying pain is produced by the nervous system does not mean it is imagined or "in the head" in the dismissive sense. All pain is produced by the nervous system, including the pain of a broken bone. The question is never whether the pain is real but what the system is responding to, and tissue damage is only one of the candidates.

## Why the distinction is treatment, not philosophy

If pain depends partly on what the system believes, then changing beliefs should change pain, and it does, modestly. Pain neuroscience education, which teaches exactly the model above, has been trialled on its own and alongside exercise. A systematic review of thirteen randomised trials found improvements in pain, disability, catastrophising and fear of movement, with the largest changes in beliefs and function rather than in pain intensity, and the clearest effects when education was paired with movement <Source id="louw-2016-pne" />.

<Evidence id="louw-2016-pne" />

The effect sizes are small to moderate and the trials are heterogeneous, so this is not a cure. What it is, is a lever. Someone who believes each twinge is further damage will protect, brace, avoid and rest, and each of those feeds the threat evaluation that produces more pain. Someone who understands that pain can outrun damage, and that most back pain is not a sign of a fragile spine, has a reason to keep moving. The [graded exposure article](/learn/pain-science/graded-exposure/) builds on that reason. The [sensitization article](/learn/pain-science/sensitization/) explains why the system can stay in protective mode long after any tissue has healed.

## Holding the model honestly

The model has an obvious failure mode of its own: concluding that because pain is not a damage meter, pain never means damage. That is as wrong as the smoke-alarm model. Sometimes nociception is the dominant input and the tissue is the problem, and the whole point of the [red flags](/learn/pathology/red-flags/) is that certain presentations are too dangerous to reason about from first principles. The honest position is that pain is evidence about threat, that tissue state is one part of the threat, and that the weight of the other parts is larger than intuition suggests.

In practice, this site uses the model in three places. The [plan](/plan/) keys its progression on load, range and preparedness relative to your history rather than on "this movement is dangerous", because a movement that is safe but feared is a sensitization problem rather than a tissue problem. The self-coaching track treats a flare as data about the whole system, including sleep and stress, not only about the last set. And the pathology track keeps repeating that imaging findings are common in people without pain, because the single most reliable way to make a back hurt more is to convince its owner it is broken.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Pain is a protective output, not a damage readout.** The nervous system produces it when it judges that the body needs protecting.
2. **Nociception is one input among many.** It is neither necessary nor sufficient for pain; context, belief, memory and mood all weigh in.
3. **Pain and damage come apart in both directions.** Common imaging findings in people without pain; disabling pain with no identifiable structure.
4. **All pain is real.** Saying it is produced by the nervous system is a statement about mechanism, not about legitimacy.
5. **Teaching this model is a modest treatment in itself.** Trials of pain neuroscience education show small to moderate improvements, largest when paired with movement.
6. **It is not a licence to ignore pain.** Red flags end self-direction and go to a clinician; the model describes how pain works, not whether yours is tissue-driven.

</KeyList>

<Check id="pain-vs-damage-3" kind="concept">
Why would simply teaching someone this model change their pain, even slightly? Explain the mechanism you think is doing the work.
<Fragment slot="answer">
If pain is produced from a threat evaluation, then the belief that each twinge is damage is itself an input that raises the evaluation, and it also drives protection, avoidance and rest that keep the system in a guarded state. Replacing that belief lowers the threat input and gives the person a reason to move, and movement adds its own safety evidence; that is why the trials show the largest effects when education is paired with exercise. Why it matters: it explains why education works best as a foundation for graded activity rather than as a standalone fix.
