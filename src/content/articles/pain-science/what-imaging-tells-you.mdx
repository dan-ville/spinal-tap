<Callout type="rose" title="This is about reading reports, not about whether to get one">
Whether imaging is warranted is a clinical question with its own article, [when imaging is warranted](/learn/pathology/when-imaging-is-warranted/), and some presentations need it urgently. This article is about what to do with a report once it exists. If you have any of the [red flags](/learn/pathology/red-flags/), the report is a conversation to have with a clinician, not with a website.
</Callout>

## Three things a scan cannot show

**It cannot show pain.** Pain is a protective output of the nervous system, as the [pain vs damage article](/learn/pain-science/pain-vs-damage/) sets out; it is not a property of tissue and so it does not appear on a picture of tissue <Source id="moseley-butler-explain-pain" />. A radiologist reporting a protrusion is describing a shape. The word "pain" in the report, if it appears at all, was copied from the referral.

**It cannot show the past.** Almost nobody has a baseline scan from before their pain started. A finding on today's scan might have appeared last month, or it might have been there for fifteen years. The asymptomatic-prevalence review makes the second possibility the default rather than the exception: in people with no back pain, disc degeneration was found in about 37% of 20-year-olds and about 96% of 80-year-olds, and disc bulge in about 30% rising to 84% <Source id="brinjikji-2015-degeneration" />. A finding that is present in most pain-free people of your age was, by the numbers, probably present before the pain.

**It cannot show cause.** A scan can show a finding and a person can have pain, and the two facts sitting side by side on the same page do not make one the cause of the other. Establishing cause needs the finding to match the symptoms, the examination to agree, and nothing else to explain them, which is clinical work that happens outside the scanner.

## The two Brinjikji reviews, read together

The asymptomatic review is often quoted alone, and alone it can be made to say that imaging findings are meaningless. The same group published a second review in the same year that keeps that from being true. The meta-analysis compared MRI findings in adults with low back pain against pain-free controls and found that, mostly in people under 50, bulge, protrusion, extrusion and Modic type 1 change were each more prevalent in the group with pain. The odds ratio for bulge was about 7.5, for extrusion about 4.4, and for protrusion about 2.6 <Source id="brinjikji-2015-meta" />.

<Evidence id="brinjikji-2015-meta" />

Both results are true at once, and they only seem to conflict if a finding is expected to be either cause or noise. The resolution is that imaging findings move probability. A finding that is common in pain-free people has a weak claim to be the cause; a finding that is more common in people with pain has a stronger claim; a finding that is both, which describes most of them, has a claim that depends on everything else. For a 25-year-old with leg pain in a nerve-root pattern and an extrusion at the matching level, the finding carries real weight. For a 55-year-old with central back pain and a bulge, it carries almost none, because bulges are nearly universal at that age and the symptoms do not match a bulge in any case.

The pathology track's [terminology article](/learn/pathology/bulge-protrusion-extrusion/) defines the words the reports use, and that vocabulary is exactly what the two reviews are counting. A bulge is a generalised extension round more than a quarter of the circumference. A protrusion is focal, with a base wider than the piece outside. An extrusion has a head wider than its neck or has moved up or down. Knowing which one the report says matters, because the two reviews give each a different prevalence in pain-free people and a different association with pain.

## Why reports can make pain worse

A report is also an input to the nervous system that reads it. The words "degeneration", "tear" and "disease" describe age-related change in the same neutral sense that grey hair does, but they land as threat, and threat is the input a sensitized system over-weights. The Lancet series and the NICE guideline both discourage routine imaging for non-specific low back pain partly for this reason: imaging without a clinical indication tends to produce findings that do not change management but do change what the person believes about their back <Source id="lancet-2018-lbp-series" /> <Source id="nice-ng59" />.

This is not an argument that imaging is harmful or that findings should be hidden. It is an argument for reading a report with the base rates in hand. "Degenerative disc disease at L4/5 and L5/S1" in a 50-year-old describes the spine of most 50-year-olds. It is a finding. It is not a verdict.

## Reading your own report

The practical sequence, once a report exists and the red flags have been cleared, is:

1. **Find the nouns.** Bulge, protrusion, extrusion, sequestration, Modic change, stenosis. Look each one up in the [terminology article](/learn/pathology/bulge-protrusion-extrusion/) rather than in the adjectives around it.
2. **Check the base rate for your age.** Use the asymptomatic prevalence figures. If most pain-free people your age have the finding, it was probably there before the pain.
3. **Check whether the symptoms match.** A finding on one side at one level that produces pain and altered sensation in that root's distribution is a match. Central back pain and a bilateral bulge is not a match for anything specific.
4. **Ask what would change.** If no treatment decision turns on the finding, the finding is context, not a target.
5. **Then take it to the clinician.** The report is one input to a clinical judgement, and the same base-rate reasoning is what a good clinician does with it.

None of this tells you your pain is or is not tissue-driven. It tells you how much a single picture can carry, which is less than it looks.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **A scan shows structure, not pain, not the past, and not cause.** It is a picture of tissue at one moment.
2. **Most findings are common in pain-free people.** Degeneration and bulge are present in a large majority of asymptomatic adults by middle age.
3. **Some findings are more common in people with pain.** Bulge, protrusion, extrusion and Modic 1 change carry higher odds in adults under 50.
4. **Read the two reviews together.** A finding shifts probability; it does not settle the question on its own.
5. **Reports are an input to the nervous system too.** Threatening language without base rates can worsen a sensitized system; that is one reason guidelines discourage routine imaging.
6. **Match the words to the definitions.** Bulge, protrusion and extrusion each have a different base rate and a different association with pain.

</KeyList>

<Fragment slot="answer">
Report A describes a bulge: generalised extension round more than a quarter of the circumference, with multi-level degeneration. Both are near-universal in pain-free people in their late forties, and central pain without leg symptoms does not match any specific lesion, so the finding carries little weight beyond context. Report B describes an extrusion: focal, with the outside piece wider than its base and migrating below the disc level. Extrusion carries a higher odds ratio in people under 50 with pain, and the symptoms match a nerve root on the same side at the same level, so this finding carries real weight. <Check kind="concept">
In your own words, explain how the Brinjikji asymptomatic-prevalence review and the Brinjikji meta-analysis can both be correct, and what that means for how much a single imaging finding can tell you.
<Fragment slot="answer">
The first shows that most degenerative findings are common in people without pain, so having one does not mean it causes pain. The second shows that some findings are more common in people with pain, so having one is not noise either. Both are true because a finding changes probability rather than giving a yes or no: its weight depends on the finding, the person's age, and whether the symptoms match. Why it matters: reading only the first review leads to dismissing every finding, reading only the second leads to blaming every finding, and both errors lead to wrong decisions about treatment and about how fragile to believe the back is.
</Fragment>

<Fragment slot="answer">

Why it matters: each of the three is a common misreading of a report, and each one pushes the reader toward over-protecting a back that is probably ordinary for its age.
