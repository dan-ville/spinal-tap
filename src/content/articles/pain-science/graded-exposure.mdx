<Callout type="rose" title="Exposure assumes the red flags have been cleared">
Graded exposure is the right tool for pain that has outlasted tissue healing and for movements that are avoided out of fear. It is the wrong tool for a presentation that has not been screened. Check the [red flags](/learn/pathology/red-flags/) first; any of them ends self-direction and goes to a clinician via the [plan](/plan/).
</Callout>

## The problem it solves

The [sensitization article](/learn/pain-science/sensitization/) ends with a system that has learned, through repetition, that a particular movement is dangerous. Every avoided rep confirmed the lesson, and every guarded, braced attempt that hurt confirmed it again. By the time the tissue has recovered, the movement itself is the trigger, and the pain on performing it is produced by the threat evaluation rather than by what the movement does to the disc or the facet <Source id="moseley-butler-explain-pain" />.

A lesson learned by repetition is unlearned the same way. The system needs a stack of experiences in which the movement was performed and nothing bad followed. Graded exposure is the method for collecting those experiences on purpose: start where the movement is already tolerable, repeat it until it is boring, move the dial a little, repeat. The RESTORE trial, in which cognitive functional therapy built exactly this kind of individualised re-exposure on top of pain education, produced large and durable reductions in activity limitation in people with chronic, disabling low back pain <Source id="kent-2023-restore" />.

## There is no list of bad movements

The first instinct, when planning exposure, is to sort movements into safe and unsafe and work through the safe pile. This site deliberately does not do that, for two reasons.

The first is that the sorting is not supported. A spine that flexes under load is doing something spines do all day; the [flexion-compression article](/learn/biomechanics/flexion-compression/) explains why the cadaveric failure data describe a mechanism and a dose, not a forbidden movement. Any movement can be loaded past what a given person is prepared for, and any movement can be loaded within it. The movement is not the variable. The gap between what it demands and what the person is currently prepared to do is the variable.

The second reason is that labels teach the wrong lesson. Telling a sensitized system "never round your back" is a threat message, and threat messages are the input the system is already over-weighting. The education trials show that beliefs about fragility are themselves a target of treatment <Source id="louw-2016-pne" />. A plan built on forbidden movements undermines that, because the person leaves with a longer list of things to fear.

So the question is never "is this movement bad?" It is "how far is this from what I have recently done, and is that gap one step or five?"

## The four dials

Exposure is dosed along the same dimensions as any other training. Each is read against your own recent history, which means the first job is to know that history; the self-coaching track covers keeping the log that makes it readable.

**Load.** The weight on the bar, or the equivalent for bodyweight work. The reference is not a chart; it is the heaviest load you have handled in that movement in the last few weeks without a flare. A step is a small fraction of that, in the range you would use for ordinary progression, which the [RPE and RIR article](/learn/training-science/rpe-and-rir/) frames as leaving reps in reserve rather than grinding.

**Volume.** Sets and reps across the session and the week. A movement that has been avoided for months has an effective volume history of zero, and the sensible first step from zero is a handful of easy reps, not a working set. Dose-response work on exercise for back pain shows benefit across a wide range of volumes, with no evidence that more is better early on <Source id="hayden-2021-cochrane" />.

**Range.** How far into the movement you go. Range is the dial most often ignored, and it is the one that most directly maps to the feared position. A hinge to mid-shin is a different exposure from a hinge to the floor, and a squat to a box is a different exposure from a squat to depth. Range can be shortened to find a tolerable start and lengthened a little at a time.

**Preparedness.** Everything that sets the system's current threshold: sleep, stress, time since the last flare, time of day, warm-up, and how the previous session went. Preparedness decides which of the other three dials you move today, and by how much. On a poor night's sleep after a stressful week, the right step may be to repeat last session rather than progress it. That is still exposure. Completing the same dose again, calmly, is a safety experience.

## Choosing the step

The rule for setting a step is that it must be completable. Pain during the set is not the failure signal; failing to finish, or finishing braced and frightened, is. A step the system can complete while staying relatively calm is evidence that the movement is tolerable. A step that triggers guarding is evidence for the old lesson, and it costs ground.

In practice that means one dial at a time, and a small move on it. If load goes up, range and volume hold. If range opens, load drops. If the week's volume is going up, each session stays easy. The [RPE and RIR](/learn/training-science/rpe-and-rir/) scale gives a vocabulary for "easy" that does not depend on pain: a set at two or three reps in reserve is the usual ceiling during exposure <Source id="helms-rpe-rir" />.

Some pain during exposure is expected and is not a reason to back off, provided it settles by the next day and the trend across weeks is downward. The [pain vs damage article](/learn/pain-science/pain-vs-damage/) is the reason this is defensible: a system that has been over-protecting will produce pain on ordinary inputs for a while, and that pain is the thing being treated, not a sign the treatment is harming tissue. The self-coaching track turns this into a daily decision rule.

## What exposure is not

It is not pushing through. A session that ends with the person white-knuckling a load they are not ready for teaches the system that the movement is exactly as dangerous as it feared.

It is not a replacement for screening. Exposure is applied to a presentation that has been cleared of red flags and in which the pain has outlasted any plausible tissue timeline. New leg symptoms, progressive weakness or any of the [red flags](/learn/pathology/red-flags/) change the problem from a sensitization problem to a clinical one, and the [plan](/plan/) routes them out of self-direction.

It is not movement-specific. The goal is a system that treats loaded spinal movement, in general, as ordinary. A person who has exposed themselves to the deadlift but still flinches when picking a child off the floor has transferred the lesson to one lift, not to their back.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Exposure unlearns a sensitized response by repetition.** Repeated completed exposures to the feared movement replace threat evidence with safety evidence.
2. **There is no list of bad movements.** The variable is the gap between what a movement demands and what you have recently done, not the movement itself.
3. **Four dials set the step: load, volume, range, preparedness.** Each is read against your own history, and preparedness decides how far to move the others today.
4. **A step must be completable, calmly.** Pain during the set is tolerable if it settles by the next day; guarding and failing to finish are the real failure signals.
5. **One dial at a time, two to three reps in reserve.** Progression during exposure is deliberately slower than ordinary training progression.
6. **Red flags end exposure.** New neurological symptoms or any listed red flag reclassify the problem and go to a clinician.

</KeyList>

<Check kind="apply">
A lifter has avoided the deadlift for four months after a flare and is now pain-free day to day. Before the flare they pulled 140 kg for sets of five to the floor. Describe a first session of exposure using the four dials, and say which dial you would move first in week two and why.
<Fragment slot="answer">
Load is read against the last few weeks, not against the 140 kg from before the flare, so the start is light: an empty bar or a load that feels trivial. Volume starts from an effective history of zero, so a few sets of three to five easy reps. Range is the dial most likely to be feared, so start from blocks or mid-shin rather than the floor. Preparedness is checked on the day: a well-slept, calm session is the one to use for a first exposure. In week two, if the sessions were completed calmly and any pain settled overnight, open range first, because the feared position is the thing the system needs evidence about, and hold load where it is. Why it matters: moving one dial at a time keeps each step completable, which is what makes it a safety experience rather than another confirmation of threat.
</Fragment>

What distinguishes a tolerable amount of pain during exposure from a signal to back off?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Pain during the set that settles by the next day and trends downward across weeks is tolerable; guarding, failing to finish the set, pain that does not settle overnight, or any new neurological symptom or red flag is the signal to back off or to leave self-direction. Why it matters: the pain being treated in exposure is produced by the over-protective system, so its presence is expected, but the system only collects safety evidence from steps it can complete calmly.
