<Lede>The paired facet joints at the back of each segment guide motion and share load. Their orientation decides what a lumbar segment can and cannot do.</Lede>

## Two small joints per segment

Each spinal segment has three joints: the disc in front and two <Structure>facet joints</Structure> behind. A facet joint, properly the zygapophysial joint, forms where the inferior articular process of one vertebra meets the superior articular process of the vertebra below. Unlike the disc, the facet is a true synovial joint: hyaline cartilage on both surfaces, a fibrous capsule, synovial fluid and a nerve supply from the medial branch of the dorsal ramus at that level and the level above <Source id="openstax-ap-2e">ch. 9</Source>.

The capsule is loose enough to allow gliding and tight enough to stop the surfaces separating. Small fat pads and synovial folds sit inside the joint margins. Because the facet is synovial, it can be a source of pain in its own right and it can develop osteoarthritis, which the disc cannot.

## Orientation sets the motion

In the lumbar spine the facet surfaces face mainly medially and laterally, close to the sagittal plane, and curve into a J shape. The superior facets are concave and face inward; the inferior facets are convex and face outward, so the inferior process of the upper vertebra sits inside the superior process of the lower one <Source id="grays-1918" />.

That geometry explains lumbar movement:

- **Flexion and extension** are free, because the surfaces slide along each other in the sagittal plane.
- **Lateral bending** is moderate, limited partly by the facets and partly by the disc.
- **Rotation** is small, a degree or two per segment, because the sagittal surfaces collide almost immediately. Most lumbar "rotation" during a twist is actually happening in the thoracic spine and hips.

The L5–S1 facets are the exception. They are angled more toward the coronal plane than the facets above, which resists the forward slide of L5 on the sloping top of the <Structure>sacrum</Structure>. That is why L5–S1 has more rotation and more shear protection than L4–L5.

## Load sharing with the disc

In an upright neutral posture the facets carry a modest share of compression, with the disc taking most of it. Move into extension and the facets take more; move into flexion and the load shifts forward to the disc. The facets also carry a large part of any anterior shear force, the tendency of one vertebra to slide forward on the one below. When the disc narrows with age, the facets are loaded more and respond like any synovial joint under extra load: thicker capsule, bony overgrowth and sometimes narrowing of the foramen next to them.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Three joints per segment.** One disc in front, two facet joints behind.
2. **Facets are synovial.** Cartilage, capsule, fluid and a nerve supply, so they can hurt and they can develop arthritis.
3. **Sagittal orientation.** Lumbar facets allow flexion and extension, limit side bending and nearly block rotation.
4. **Load shifts with posture.** Extension loads the facets; flexion loads the disc; facets resist anterior shear.

</KeyList>
