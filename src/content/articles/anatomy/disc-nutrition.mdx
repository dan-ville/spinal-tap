<Lede>The adult disc has no blood supply of its own. Its cells live on nutrients that diffuse through the endplates, and the loading rhythm of the day is part of the pump.</Lede>

## The largest avascular structure in the body

Most tissues are threaded with capillaries. The <Structure id="FMA13894">intervertebral disc</Structure> is not: in the adult, blood vessels reach only the outer few millimetres of the <Structure>annulus fibrosus</Structure> and the bone on the far side of the <Structure>vertebral endplate</Structure>. The <Structure>nucleus pulposus</Structure> at the centre may sit 8 mm or more from the nearest capillary <Source id="grays-1918" />. For the cells living there, every molecule of glucose and oxygen has to diffuse in from the edge, and every waste product has to diffuse out the same way.

Diffusion over that distance is slow. The result is a tissue with a low cell density, a low metabolic rate and a very slow turnover of its structural proteins. The collagen and proteoglycans a disc is built from are replaced over years to decades, not weeks. That is the single most important fact for understanding why disc injury heals slowly and why disc adaptation to training is measured in years.

## Two routes in

Nutrients reach the disc by two routes, and the endplate route matters most.

- **Through the endplates.** The vertebral endplate is a thin plate of hyaline cartilage sitting on a layer of porous bone. Capillary buds in that bone end against the cartilage, and solutes cross the cartilage into the nucleus and inner annulus. Because the nucleus has no other supply, the endplate is its lifeline <Source id="openstax-ap-2e">ch. 9</Source>.
- **Through the outer annulus.** Vessels in the tissue around the disc feed the outermost rings of the annulus directly. This route supplies only the periphery.

Anything that reduces the permeability of the endplate therefore starves the centre of the disc. Endplate calcification with age, endplate fractures from a heavy compressive overload, and the sclerotic (hardened) bone that forms next to a degenerating disc all reduce the flow, which is one reason degeneration tends to be self-reinforcing once it starts <Source id="adams-dolan" />.

## Loading is part of the pump

Diffusion is passive, but the disc is not a static sponge. Compressive load squeezes water out of the nucleus; unloading lets it draw water back in. Across a normal day the disc loses height under the load of standing and lifting, and recovers it overnight lying down. Small solutes such as glucose and oxygen move mainly by diffusion regardless of load, but larger molecules are carried along with that fluid flow, and the daily loading cycle keeps the fluid moving and the cells supplied with fresh medium <Source id="adams-dolan" />.

The practical implication is that a disc is fed by being used. Prolonged complete unloading (bed rest, or weightlessness in spaceflight studies) and prolonged constant loading both reduce the exchange. Alternating load and rest, which is what a training day and a night's sleep provide, is the condition the disc is built for.

## Does training change the disc?

The best human evidence is cross-sectional: a 2017 MRI study compared the lumbar discs of long-term runners and joggers with those of non-athletes and found the runners' discs were thicker and better hydrated, with the association strongest at moderate running speeds <Source id="belavy-2017" />. A cross-sectional study cannot show cause. It is possible that people with healthier discs are more likely to keep running, rather than running making the discs healthier. No trial has yet loaded people for years and measured the change.

What the study does establish is that disc quality is associated with a long history of repetitive, moderate loading rather than with avoiding load. That fits the diffusion mechanism above, and it is the reason this site treats the disc as a tissue that adapts, slowly, to what it is asked to do.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Avascular centre.** Vessels stop at the outer annulus and the bone beside the endplate; the nucleus lives entirely on diffusion.
2. **The endplate is the gateway.** Most of the nucleus's supply crosses the cartilage endplate, so endplate damage or calcification starves the disc.
3. **Load drives fluid exchange.** Daily compression and overnight recovery pump water in and out; both constant load and complete unloading reduce the exchange.
4. **Slow tissue, slow adaptation.** Structural proteins turn over across years, so healing and adaptation are measured in years. Runners' discs look healthier, but that evidence is cross-sectional.

</KeyList>

<Check kind="concept">
What would you expect to happen to the centre of a disc if the endplates above and below it became calcified and far less permeable?
<Fragment slot="answer">
The nucleus has no supply except what crosses the endplates, so its cells would be starved of glucose and oxygen and unable to clear waste. Cell death and loss of proteoglycan would follow, the nucleus would hold less water and the disc would lose height and pressure. Why it matters: it explains why endplate damage and degeneration reinforce each other, and why the disc cannot be rescued quickly once that cycle has started.
</Fragment>

Why does the site treat the runners' MRI study as support for loading the disc, but not as proof that running makes discs healthier?

That shows an association, but people with healthier discs may simply be the ones who keep running. Why it matters: the finding fits the diffusion mechanism and argues against avoiding load, but the strength of the claim has to match the design that produced it.
