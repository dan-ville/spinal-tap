<Lede>The hip is a deep ball-and-socket joint driven by the gluteals, hamstrings and hip flexors. How much it bends and extends decides how much the lumbar spine has to.</Lede>

## The joint

The hip joint is the head of the <Structure>femur</Structure> sitting in the acetabulum of the <Structure>hip bone</Structure>. The socket is deepened by a fibrocartilage labrum and the whole joint is wrapped in a thick capsule reinforced by three ligaments. The iliofemoral ligament at the front is the strongest in the body and is what stops the pelvis tipping backward on the femur in standing <Source id="openstax-ap-2e">ch. 9</Source>.

Because the socket is deep, the hip trades range for stability compared with the shoulder. It still gives roughly 120 degrees of flexion with the knee bent, 10 to 20 degrees of extension, and moderate abduction and rotation. Bony shape varies a lot between people: a deeper socket or a more retroverted neck means less flexion before the femoral neck meets the rim, which is why squat depth and stance width are individual rather than prescribed.

## The movers

**Extensors.** <Structure id="FMA22314">Gluteus maximus</Structure> is the largest muscle in the body. It runs from the ilium, sacrum and sacrotuberous ligament to the gluteal tuberosity and iliotibial tract. It is a powerful extensor and external rotator, most active in climbing, sprinting and rising from a deep squat rather than in level walking <Source id="grays-1918" />. The <Structure id="FMA22357">hamstring group</Structure>, semitendinosus, <Structure id="FMA22438">semimembranosus</Structure> and the long head of <Structure id="FMA45887">biceps femoris</Structure>, arises from the ischial tuberosity and both extends the hip and flexes the knee. In a hinge the hamstrings do the hip extension work with the knee nearly straight; in a squat the gluteals take over as the knee bends.

**Flexors.** <Structure>Psoas major</Structure> and <Structure>iliacus</Structure> share a tendon onto the lesser trochanter. Psoas also pulls on the lumbar spine, so a tight or hard-working psoas loads the lumbar discs in compression. Rectus femoris and sartorius assist.

**Abductors and rotators.** <Structure>Gluteus medius</Structure> and <Structure>gluteus minimus</Structure> keep the pelvis level on one leg. The deep rotators (piriformis, obturators, gemelli, <Structure>quadratus femoris</Structure>) steer the femoral head in the socket and act as a rotator cuff for the hip.

**Adductors.** The adductor group on the inside of the thigh is a large mass that contributes to hip extension from a flexed position, which is why the adductor magnus works hard in a wide-stance squat.

## The hip-spine relationship

The pelvis is the link between hip and spine, so any hip motion that the joint cannot provide gets made up by the lumbar spine. When a lifter runs out of hip flexion at the bottom of a squat the pelvis rotates backward and the lumbar spine flexes, the "butt wink". When hip extension is limited at the top of a deadlift the lumbar spine extends to finish the lift. Neither is dangerous in itself, but each shifts load from a joint built to take it to segments that get less of a say.

The reverse also holds. Strong, mobile hips let the trunk stay close to neutral through a lift, and the hip extensors are the engine that an intact spine transmits force through. Most of the "back strength" in a deadlift is hip extension torque carried up through a stiff trunk.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Deep socket, strong capsule.** The hip is stable first; range is individual and set partly by bone shape.
2. **Gluteus maximus and hamstrings extend the hip.** Hamstrings dominate the hinge, gluteals the deep squat.
3. **Psoas flexes the hip and loads the spine.** It compresses the lumbar discs rather than moving the vertebrae.
4. **What the hip cannot do, the lumbar spine does.** Limited hip range turns into lumbar flexion at the bottom of a squat or lumbar extension at the lockout.

</KeyList>

<Check id="hip-complex-1" kind="recall">
Which muscles extend the hip, and how does their share of the work change between a hinge and a squat?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Gluteus maximus, the hamstrings and the adductor magnus. The hamstrings dominate a hinge with a near-straight knee; the gluteals take over as the knee bends in a squat. Why it matters: it tells you which lift to pick when you want to load a particular extensor.
</Fragment>
</Check>

<Check id="hip-complex-2" kind="apply">
A lifter's lumbar spine flexes sharply at the bottom of every squat. Where would you look first, and why?
<Fragment slot="answer">
At hip flexion range and bony hip shape. When the hip runs out of flexion the pelvis rotates backward and the lumbar spine flexes to make up the difference. Why it matters: changing stance, depth or foot angle may fix it, whereas cueing "keep the back flat" cannot create range the hip does not have.
</Fragment>
</Check>
