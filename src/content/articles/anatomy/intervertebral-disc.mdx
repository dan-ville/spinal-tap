<Lede>The disc is a pressurised cushion between vertebral bodies. A gel core, a fibre wall and two cartilage plates share every load the spine carries.</Lede>

## Where it sits

An <Structure id="FMA13894">intervertebral disc</Structure> lies between every pair of lumbar vertebral bodies, from L1–L2 down to L5–S1. Together the lumbar discs make up roughly a quarter of the height of the lumbar column, which is why a single lumbar disc is thicker than any disc above it. Each disc is a symphysis: a fibrocartilaginous joint with no joint cavity, built to bear load and permit small movement in every direction rather than large movement in one <Source id="openstax-ap-2e">ch. 9</Source>.

## Three parts, one job

The disc has three tissues, and each one fails in a recognisable way when it is overloaded.

- The **<Structure>nucleus pulposus</Structure>** is the gel core. It is mostly water held by proteoglycans, so it behaves like a pressurised fluid: squeeze it from above and it pushes outward in every direction.
- The **<Structure>annulus fibrosus</Structure>** is the wall around the nucleus. It is built from concentric rings (lamellae) of collagen, with the fibres of each ring angled about 30° to the horizontal and alternating direction ring to ring. That criss-cross lets the wall resist the nucleus pushing out, and also resist bending and twisting <Source id="grays-1918" />.
- The **<Structure>vertebral endplate</Structure>** is the thin plate of hyaline cartilage that caps the top and bottom of the disc and bonds it to the bony surface of the vertebral body. The endplate is the route by which nutrients diffuse into the disc, and it is the layer that cracks first under a sudden heavy compression.

When the spine is loaded in compression, the nucleus pressurises, the annulus is tensioned like a barrel's hoops, and the endplates spread the load across the vertebral bodies above and below. Load the segment in flexion and the front of the disc is squeezed while the back of the annulus is stretched. Repeat that enough times under enough compression and the nucleus can work its way through the posterior annulus; this is the mechanism of a flexion-related herniation, demonstrated in cadaveric and animal spines rather than in living lifters <Source id="adams-dolan" />.

## It is alive, and it adapts

The adult disc has almost no blood supply, so its cells live on what diffuses through the endplates. That makes the disc slow to adapt and slow to repair. It does adapt, though: a cross-sectional MRI study of long-term runners found thicker, better-hydrated lumbar discs than in non-athletes, with the association strongest for moderate running speeds <Source id="belavy-2017" />. The disc is not inert packing material. It responds to the loads it is given over years, not weeks.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Three tissues.** Nucleus pulposus (gel core), annulus fibrosus (criss-cross fibre wall), vertebral endplates (cartilage caps that bond the disc to bone).
2. **It works as a pressure vessel.** Compression pressurises the nucleus; the annulus holds it in; the endplates spread the load.
3. **Flexion plus compression stresses the back wall.** Repeated flexion under load is the laboratory mechanism for posterior herniation.
4. **Avascular but adaptable.** Nutrition is by diffusion through the endplates, so adaptation is slow, but loading over years is associated with healthier discs.

</KeyList>

<Check kind="recall">
Name the three tissues of the intervertebral disc and give each one's role under compression.
<Fragment slot="answer">
The nucleus pulposus pressurises like a fluid; the annulus fibrosus holds that pressure in with its angled collagen rings; the vertebral endplates spread the load into the vertebral bodies and let nutrients diffuse in. Why it matters: every load the spine carries passes through all three, so each has its own failure mode.
</Fragment>
</Check>

<Check kind="concept">
Why does the annulus fibrosus use alternating fibre directions in its rings?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Each ring's fibres run about 30° to the horizontal, in opposite directions ring to ring. One direction resists twisting one way, the next resists twisting the other, and together they hoop the nucleus against outward pressure. Why it matters: it explains why the disc tolerates compression well but is more vulnerable to combined bending and twisting.
</Fragment>
</Check>
