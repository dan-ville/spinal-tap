<Lede>The five lumbar vertebrae are the largest in the column. Their shape is a direct answer to the loads they carry.</Lede>

## The body carries the load

The vertebral body is the thick, kidney-shaped block at the front of each vertebra. In the lumbar spine it is wider than it is deep and wider than it is tall, because the lumbar bodies carry more of the trunk's weight than any segment above them. The body's top and bottom surfaces are flat, slightly roughened plates called endplates. The intervertebral disc attaches to them, so the compressive load from the body above passes through the disc and into the endplates of the body below <Source id="openstax-ap-2e">ch. 7</Source>.

Inside, the body is mostly cancellous (spongy) bone wrapped in a thin cortical shell. This matters for loading: the spongy interior is what deforms first under compression, and it is where most load-related adaptation happens.

## The arch protects the nerves

Behind the body sits the vertebral arch, made of two pedicles and two laminae. Body and arch together enclose the vertebral foramen. Stacked, the foramina form the vertebral canal that houses the spinal cord above L1–L2 and the cauda equina below it. The lumbar foramen is triangular and roomier than the thoracic one, which is one reason lumbar canal stenosis takes years of narrowing to become symptomatic.

Between adjacent vertebrae, notches in the pedicles form the intervertebral foramina. Each spinal nerve root exits through one of these. A disc, a facet joint and a ligament all border every foramen, so changes in any of them can narrow the exit.

## Processes are lever arms

Seven processes project from the arch. They are attachment and leverage points, not load bearers.

- **Spinous process** (one): short, thick and almost horizontal in the lumbar spine, pointing straight back. <Structure>Multifidus</Structure>, the thoracolumbar fascia and the interspinous ligaments attach here.
- **Transverse processes** (two): long, thin and lateral. Quadratus lumborum and psoas major attach to them, and the deep rotators run between them.
- **Articular processes** (four, two superior and two inferior): each carries a facet. The inferior facets of one vertebra meet the superior facets of the vertebra below to form the facet (zygapophysial) joints. In the lumbar spine the facets face roughly medially and laterally, in the sagittal plane, which lets the segment flex and extend freely but strongly limits rotation <Source id="grays-1918" />.

## Lumbar vertebrae differ from the rest

Compared with cervical and thoracic vertebrae, a lumbar vertebra has no transverse foramen, no costal facets for ribs, a much larger body, a short blunt spinous process and sagittally oriented facets. The <Structure id="FMA13076">fifth lumbar vertebra</Structure> is the largest and most wedge-shaped, taller at the front than the back, which produces the lumbosacral angle where L5 meets the <Structure>sacrum</Structure>.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **The body bears compression.** Lumbar bodies are the largest in the column; load passes disc → endplate → cancellous bone.
2. **The arch guards the canal and the exits.** Pedicles and laminae form the vertebral foramen; pedicle notches form the intervertebral foramina the nerve roots leave through.
3. **Processes are levers.** Spinous and transverse processes are muscle and ligament anchors; articular processes form the facet joints.
4. **Facet orientation sets motion.** Sagittal lumbar facets allow flexion and extension and block most rotation.

</KeyList>

<Check kind="recall">
Which part of a lumbar vertebra carries most of the compressive load, and what does that load pass through to reach it?
<Fragment slot="answer">
The vertebral body. Load passes from the body above through the intervertebral disc and the endplate into the cancellous bone of the body below.
</Fragment>
</Check>

<Check kind="concept">
Why does the lumbar spine flex and extend freely but rotate very little?
<Fragment slot="answer">
The lumbar facet joints face medially and laterally, in the sagittal plane. That orientation lets the articular processes glide for flexion and extension but makes them collide under rotation.
</Fragment>
