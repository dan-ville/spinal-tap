<Lede>The spinal cord ends at L1–L2; below that, the lumbar and sacral roots run as the cauda equina and exit one level at a time. Each root has a skin territory and a muscle, and the pattern of symptoms tells you which root is involved.</Lede>

## The cord stops early

In an adult the <Structure>lumbar spinal cord</Structure> does not run the length of the lumbar canal. The cord ends at about the L1–L2 disc as the <Structure>conus medullaris</Structure>; the vertebral column grew faster than the cord during development, so the lower segments of cord sit well above the vertebrae that share their names <Source id="openstax-ap-2e">ch. 13</Source>. Below the conus, the canal holds no cord at all, only a bundle of descending <Structure>lumbar nerve roots</Structure> and sacral roots called the <Structure>cauda equina</Structure> (the "horse's tail"), floating in cerebrospinal fluid <Source id="grays-1918" />.

That arrangement has two consequences. A lumbar disc never presses on the spinal cord, because there is no cord at that level; it presses on roots. And because the roots run downward for several levels before exiting, a problem at one disc can affect a root that exits lower down.

## How a root exits

Each pair of roots leaves the canal through the intervertebral foramen between two pedicles, the gap bordered by a disc in front and a facet joint behind. In the lumbar spine the root is named for the vertebra *above* the foramen: the L4 root exits below L4, between L4 and L5.

Which root a disc bulge hits depends on where the bulge sits. A posterolateral bulge at L4–L5, the common pattern, usually catches the L5 root, which is still travelling down to its own foramen one level below. A bulge that pushes far out into the foramen itself catches the root exiting at that level, L4. The level of the disc and the level of the root are therefore not the same thing, and a scan report that names a disc has not yet told you which root is involved.

## Three roots to know

The roots that matter most for lifters are L4, L5 and S1, because they share the L4–L5 and L5–S1 discs, which carry the most load and herniate most often. Each root supplies a strip of skin (a dermatome) and a group of muscles (a myotome), and each has a reflex that a clinician can tap <Source id="openstax-ap-2e">ch. 13</Source>.

| Root | Skin (dermatome) | Muscles (myotome) | Reflex |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **L4** | Inner shin and inner ankle | Knee extension (quadriceps), ankle dorsiflexion (tibialis anterior) | Knee jerk |
| **L5** | Outer shin, top of the foot, big toe | Big-toe extension, ankle dorsiflexion, hip abduction (gluteus medius) | None reliable |
| **S1** | Outer foot, sole, little toe, back of the calf | Ankle plantarflexion (calf), hip extension (gluteus maximus) | Ankle jerk |

Dermatome maps vary between people and between textbooks; the borders overlap, and a single root rarely produces a clean strip of altered sensation. Treat the table as the classic pattern, not a precise map.

## From root to leg

Below the pelvis the L4–S3 roots join into the <Structure>sciatic nerve</Structure>, the largest nerve in the body, which runs through the buttock and down the back of the thigh before splitting behind the knee <Source id="grays-1918" />. This is why root irritation in the lower lumbar spine is felt in the leg rather than the back: the root's territory is the leg. Pain that follows a root down the leg is called radicular pain, and the word "sciatica" is the everyday name for it, whatever the cause.

## Why below the knee is a boundary

Back pain that spreads into the buttock or thigh is common and usually comes from the back itself, referred from a disc, a facet or a muscle. Pain, numbness, tingling or weakness that travels *below the knee* is different: the only structures with a skin territory below the knee are the roots in the table above, so symptoms there point to a nerve root rather than to the back alone. That is why "symptoms below the knee" sits at the top of the red-flag list on the [plan page](/plan/). It does not mean an emergency on its own, but it moves you out of self-directed training and into an assessment, because what you are managing has changed from a back to a nerve <Source id="nice-ng59" />.

Two more items on that list come straight from this anatomy. **Saddle anaesthesia** (numbness around the perineum and inner thighs) and **bladder or bowel change** are the sacral roots of the cauda equina, S2–S4, and a large central disc herniation can compress many of those roots at once. That combination is cauda equina syndrome, which is a same-day emergency because the roots do not recover if decompression is delayed <Source id="nice-ng59" />.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **The cord ends at L1–L2.** Below the conus medullaris the canal holds only the cauda equina, a bundle of roots, so lumbar discs press on roots, not the cord.
2. **Roots exit below the vertebra they are named for**, and travel down before they exit, so a disc level and the root it affects are usually one level apart.
3. **L4, L5 and S1 have distinct territories.** L4 is the inner shin and knee jerk; L5 is the top of the foot and big toe; S1 is the outer foot, calf and ankle jerk.
4. **Below the knee means a root.** Only nerve roots have skin territory there, so leg symptoms below the knee leave the self-directed plan. Saddle numbness or bladder change means the sacral roots and is an emergency.

</KeyList>

<Check kind="recall">
Which root is most often affected by a posterolateral disc bulge at L4–L5, and where would that root's symptoms be felt?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Usually the L5 root, because it is still descending past the L4–L5 disc on its way to the L5–S1 foramen. Its territory is the outer shin, the top of the foot and the big toe, with weakness of big-toe extension and sometimes hip abduction. Why it matters: the disc level on a scan and the symptomatic root are often one level apart, so the leg pattern, not the report, tells you what is involved.
</Fragment>
</Check>

Why does the plan treat symptoms below the knee differently from pain in the buttock or thigh?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Buttock and thigh pain can be referred from the back itself (disc, facet, muscle), but the only structures with skin territory below the knee are the L4, L5 and S1 roots carried in the sciatic nerve. Symptoms there point to a nerve root rather than to the back. Why it matters: a nerve root problem needs assessment rather than self-directed loading, which is why it is a red flag on the plan page.
