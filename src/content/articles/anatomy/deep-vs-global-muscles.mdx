<Lede>The trunk muscles split into short, segmental muscles that stiffen the spine and long, multi-joint muscles that move it. Both matter, and the popular idea that one "switches off" after back pain is contested.</Lede>

## Two layers, two jobs

The muscles around the lumbar spine can be sorted by what they attach to. **Deep (local) muscles** span one or a few segments and attach directly to the vertebrae. **Global muscles** span many segments, often from pelvis to rib cage, and attach to the vertebrae only indirectly or not at all. The short muscles are well placed to control the position of one vertebra on the next; the long muscles produce large torques and move the whole trunk <Source id="openstax-ap-2e">ch. 11</Source>.

The division is useful but not a rule of nature. Most muscles do some of both, and a heavy lift recruits every layer at once.

## The deep layer

- <Structure>Multifidus</Structure> is the most medial of the deep back muscles. Its fascicles run from a spinous process down and out to the mammillary processes and sacrum two to five segments below. It is thickest in the lumbar spine and is the main segmental extensor and stiffener <Source id="grays-1918" />.
- <Structure>Quadratus lumborum</Structure> runs from the iliac crest to the twelfth rib and lumbar transverse processes. It side-bends the trunk, fixes the last rib, and stiffens the spine laterally in a carry.
- <Structure>Transversus abdominis</Structure> is the deepest abdominal muscle. Its horizontal fibres wrap the abdomen and pull on the thoracolumbar fascia, so it tensions the fascia and raises intra-abdominal pressure rather than flexing the trunk.
- The rotatores and intertransversarii are tiny and are richly supplied with muscle spindles. They are probably more sensor than motor.

## The global layer

- The erector spinae group is the long paravertebral mass. <Structure id="FMA22702">Iliocostalis lumborum</Structure> is lateral and <Structure id="FMA22709">longissimus thoracis</Structure> is medial to it. Both extend the trunk and, because their lumbar fibres run slightly backward and down, also resist the forward shear of the upper vertebra on the lower one <Source id="grays-1918" />.
- <Structure>Internal oblique</Structure> and the external oblique flex, side-bend and rotate the trunk and compress the abdomen. Their layered fibre directions let them work like a woven belt.
- <Structure>Latissimus dorsi</Structure> is the big sheet from the humerus to the thoracolumbar fascia, lower ribs and iliac crest. It is an arm muscle that happens to be a trunk stiffener; in a deadlift it holds the bar close and tensions the fascia.
- <Structure>Psoas major</Structure> runs from the lumbar vertebral bodies and discs to the lesser trochanter. It is a hip flexor first and a lumbar stabiliser by its compressive line of pull, not an extensor or flexor of the lumbar spine in any useful sense.

## The timing debate

A widely repeated claim is that transversus abdominis and multifidus fire late or shrink after an episode of back pain, and that targeted "core activation" exercises are needed to restore them. The early studies were small, used fine-wire electromyography in laboratory tasks, and have not been consistently replicated; trials of specific stabilisation exercise do not outperform general exercise for pain or function. Treat any statement that a deep muscle "switches off" or that a timing deficit causes back pain as `[contested]`.

What is better supported is capacity, not timing. Trunk muscle endurance can be measured with simple timed holds, and the ratios between flexor, extensor and side-bridge times give a useful picture of balance <Source id="mcgill-1999-endurance-norms" />. Low endurance is a trainable finding; "poor activation" is not a measurable one outside a laboratory.

## What this means in the gym

Think of the deep layer as providing segmental stiffness and the global layer as providing torque. Carries, bracing and anti-rotation work load the deep layer without needing to isolate it; squats, hinges and rows load the global layer. You do not need to feel a specific muscle to train it.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Deep = segmental, global = multi-segment.** Multifidus, quadratus lumborum and transversus abdominis control position; erector spinae, obliques and latissimus produce torque.
2. **Psoas is a hip muscle.** Its lumbar attachments compress and stabilise rather than move the spine.
3. **Timing deficits are contested.** Claims that a deep muscle switches off after pain lack consistent replication; measure endurance instead.
4. **Compound lifts train both layers.** Isolation is not required.

</KeyList>

<Check id="deep-vs-global-muscles-1" kind="recall">
Name two deep (segmental) lumbar muscles and two global trunk muscles, and state the main job of each layer.
<Fragment slot="answer">
Deep: multifidus, quadratus lumborum or transversus abdominis, which control segmental position and stiffness. Global: erector spinae (iliocostalis, longissimus), obliques or latissimus dorsi, which produce large trunk torques. Why it matters: it tells you which layer a given exercise is mainly loading.
