<Lede>Ligaments are the passive restraints of the segment. Each one tightens at a particular end of range, and together they define where motion stops.</Lede>

## Ligaments only pull

A ligament is a band of dense collagen joining bone to bone. It cannot push and it cannot contract. It is slack through the middle of a joint's range and becomes taut as the joint approaches its end of range, so its job is to stop motion, not to produce it. In the spine, each ligament lies at a particular distance in front of or behind the axis of bending, and that position tells you which movement it resists <Source id="openstax-ap-2e">ch. 9</Source>.

## The front and back of the body

- The **<Structure>anterior longitudinal ligament</Structure>** is a broad, strong band running down the front of the vertebral bodies and discs from the skull to the sacrum. It is the main check on extension and the only ligament that is tensioned by backward bending.
- The **<Structure>posterior longitudinal ligament</Structure>** runs down the back of the vertebral bodies, inside the vertebral canal. It is narrower than its anterior counterpart and narrows further in the lumbar spine, where it tapers to a strip over the back of each disc. That taper leaves the posterolateral corner of the disc less covered, which is one reason herniations tend to appear there rather than straight back <Source id="grays-1918" />.

## The arch and the processes

- The **<Structure>ligamentum flavum</Structure>** joins adjacent laminae. It is unusual for a ligament: mostly elastin rather than collagen, which is why it is yellow. The elastin keeps it taut in neutral so it does not buckle into the canal when the spine extends, and lets it stretch considerably in flexion.
- The **<Structure>interspinous ligaments</Structure>** join adjacent spinous processes and the **<Structure>supraspinous ligament</Structure>** runs along their tips. Both are furthest from the axis of bending, so they are the first to tighten in flexion and the ones that take most of the strain at end-range forward bending.
- The **<Structure>intertransverse ligaments</Structure>** are thin bands between transverse processes that resist lateral bending to the opposite side.
- The **<Structure>iliolumbar ligaments</Structure>** run from the transverse processes of L5 (and often L4) to the iliac crest. They anchor the lowest lumbar vertebra to the pelvis and resist L5 sliding forward or bending sideways on the sacrum.

## The thoracolumbar fascia

The **<Structure>thoracolumbar fascia</Structure>** is not a ligament but it behaves like one under load. It is a sheet of dense connective tissue with several layers that wraps the lumbar back muscles and attaches to the spinous processes, the sacrum, the iliac crest and the ribs. Latissimus dorsi, gluteus maximus, transversus abdominis and the internal oblique all pull on it, so it is a tension-transmitting sheet between the arm, the trunk wall and the hip. When the trunk flexes and the abdominal wall braces, the fascia tightens and adds a small extension moment, which is part of how a braced trunk resists forward bending.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Ligaments resist, never produce, motion.** Position relative to the bending axis tells you which movement each one checks.
2. **Anterior longitudinal resists extension; everything behind the bodies resists flexion.** The posterior longitudinal ligament thins over the lumbar discs.
3. **The ligamentum flavum is elastic.** Its elastin keeps it from buckling into the canal in extension.
4. **The thoracolumbar fascia links trunk, arm and hip.** It transmits tension from large muscles and stiffens the lumbar spine when the trunk braces.

</KeyList>

<Check kind="recall">
Which ligament is the main restraint against lumbar extension, and where does it lie?
<Fragment slot="answer">
The anterior longitudinal ligament, running down the front of the vertebral bodies and discs. Why it matters: it is the only ligament in front of the bending axis, so it is the only one tensioned by backward bending.
</Fragment>
</Check>

<Check kind="concept">
Why does the ligamentum flavum contain so much elastin when most ligaments are collagen?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Elastin keeps the ligament under tension in neutral and lets it recoil, so it does not fold into the vertebral canal when the spine extends. Why it matters: a slack collagen ligament in that position would buckle toward the cord and nerve roots.
</Fragment>
