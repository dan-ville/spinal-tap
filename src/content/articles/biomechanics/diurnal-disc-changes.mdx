<Lede>The disc swells with water overnight and loses it under load during the day, so you are measurably taller in the morning and the lumbar discs are stiffer in flexion for the first hour or so after rising. That is the reason the plan schedules the McGill big 3 at least an hour after waking.</Lede>

## A sponge on a daily cycle

The nucleus of the <Structure id="FMA13894">lumbar disc</Structure> is mostly water held by proteoglycans, large molecules that attract and bind it <Source id="openstax-ap-2e">ch. 9</Source>. The disc has no blood supply of its own and exchanges fluid with the vertebral bodies through the endplates, a process the [disc nutrition article](/learn/anatomy/disc-nutrition/) covers. What drives that exchange, more than anything else, is load.

Lying down for eight hours removes most of the compressive load from the spine. With the pressure off, the proteoglycans draw water in and the disc swells. Standing up puts the load back, and over the day the disc slowly expresses water again. The loss is largest in the first hour or two of being upright and tails off after that. Cadaveric loading studies reproduce the same behaviour: sustained compression squeezes fluid out and reduces disc height, and unloading lets it return <Source id="adams-dolan" />.

The outward sign is stature. People are a little taller first thing in the morning than at night, and the difference is mostly disc height <Source id="physiopedia" />.

## Why a swollen disc is stiffer in flexion

A fuller disc is a more pressurised disc. When the nucleus is at its most hydrated it pushes outward harder on the annulus, and the annular fibres are already under tension before any bending starts. Bending the segment forward then has to stretch fibres that are already taut, so the segment resists flexion more and reaches the stiff part of its [neutral zone](/learn/biomechanics/neutral-zone/) curve sooner. In the cadaveric work, the same segment loaded early in its simulated day resisted bending more and sustained higher bending stresses in the posterior annulus than it did after a period of creep loading had expelled fluid <Source id="adams-dolan" />.

In plain terms: in the first hour after rising, a given amount of forward bending puts more stress on the posterior annulus than the same bend does later in the day. The [flexion-compression article](/learn/biomechanics/flexion-compression/) explains why the posterior annulus is the part worth being thoughtful about.

## What that does and does not mean

This is a cadaveric mechanism with a plausible everyday correlate, not an injury rule. Nobody has shown that bending over to tie shoes at 7 a.m. injures people, and most people do it every day of their lives without consequence. What the mechanism supports is a mild scheduling preference. Flexion-heavy work, whether the trunk flexor hold of the McGill battery, a Jefferson curl or a heavy rounded-back event, is marginally kinder to the disc an hour or more after rising than straight out of bed. The size of that advantage is unknown; it costs nothing to take it.

## The plan's rule

The [plan](/plan/) prescribes the McGill big 3 daily as endurance work and asks for it to be done at least an hour after waking. The rule is tagged as a heuristic there, and this article is the reason behind it. The curl-up component of the big 3 loads the trunk flexors with the spine near neutral, but it is still practised repeatedly, and the side bridge and bird dog are long holds. Pushing them an hour past rising lets the discs shed their overnight swelling before the session rather than during it. The same logic applies to the morning stiffness test in the daily check-in: it is useful precisely because the disc is in the same hydrated state every morning, so the test is comparable day to day.

Two practical corollaries:

- **Walk first.** The plan's daily walk is a convenient way to spend the first hour upright under light load, which is exactly the loading that expresses fluid.
- **Do not over-read evening flexibility.** You will bend further and more easily in the evening than in the morning. That is disc hydration, not a sudden change in the back's condition, and it is the same reason a morning stiffness that eases within an hour is ordinary rather than alarming.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **The disc swells overnight and drains during the day.** Water follows load; unloading in bed lets the proteoglycans pull it in.
2. **Most of the daytime loss happens early.** The first hour or two upright accounts for the bulk of it.
3. **A hydrated disc is stiffer in flexion.** Pre-tensioned annular fibres resist bending and carry more stress for a given bend.
4. **This is cadaveric mechanism, not an injury rule.** It supports a scheduling preference, not a prohibition on morning movement.
5. **The plan's one-hour rule follows from it.** McGill big 3 at least an hour after waking lets the discs shed their overnight swelling first.

</KeyList>

What if a lifter notices they are stiff on rising but loosen up within the first hour, and worries it means their back is getting worse? What does the diurnal cycle say?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Morning stiffness that resolves within an hour is the expected behaviour of a hydrated disc losing fluid under load, not a sign of deterioration. The plan's morning stiffness test uses that consistency to make day-to-day comparisons fair. Why it matters: a stiffness that persists or worsens is the thing to log and watch, not one that follows the normal daily cycle.
