<Lede>The load on the lumbar spine in a lift is set less by the weight on the bar than by how far in front of the spine that weight sits. Moment equals force times horizontal distance, and the back muscles pay for every centimetre of that distance with compression.</Lede>

## What a moment is

A force applied at a distance from a pivot tends to rotate the thing it acts on. That rotating tendency is a **moment** (or torque), and it is the product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force's line of action. That distance is the **moment arm** <Source id="biomechanics-human-movement" />. The same force produces twice the moment at twice the distance, which is why a long spanner turns a stiff bolt and why holding a kettlebell at arm's length is harder than holding it against your chest.

In a lift the pivot that matters for this site is the lumbar spine, usually taken as the lower lumbar discs, and the force is gravity acting on the barbell and on the trunk itself. The moment arm is the horizontal distance from the spine to the bar's line of pull. Nothing about the bar's height matters to the moment; only its horizontal offset does <Source id="shelton-exercise-science" />.

Two things fall out of playing with the numbers. Doubling the load doubles the moment. Doubling the moment arm also doubles the moment, with no extra weight on the bar. A lifter who lets the bar drift forward has, in effect, added plates.

## Why the spine pays in compression

The external moment does not disappear. It is balanced by an internal moment from the <Structure id="FMA22702">erector spinae</Structure> and the other extensors, which pull the trunk back toward the pivot. Those muscles run close to the spine, so their moment arm is short, on the order of a few centimetres, while the bar's moment arm is often tens of centimetres. To balance a long external arm with a short internal one, the muscles must produce a force many times the bar's weight <Source id="openstax-ap-2e">ch. 11</Source>. Almost all of that muscle force runs parallel to the spine and compresses it.

That lever disadvantage is why modelled lumbar compression in heavy deadlifts reaches several times the bar load and scales with the horizontal distance between bar and spine <Source id="deadlift-loads-2022" />. The [lift spinal loads article](/learn/biomechanics/) gives the modelled ranges. Here the point is the mechanism: the moment arm is the lever the lifter controls, and the compression is the price of the lever.

## What this means in the gym

- **Bar path is load management.** Keeping the bar over the midfoot and close to the shins shortens the moment arm. The same set, same weight, is a smaller spinal load when the bar does not drift.
- **Trunk angle is a moment arm too.** The trunk's own mass sits in front of the lumbar spine when you hinge. A more horizontal torso lengthens the arm of that mass as well as the bar's, which is one reason a conventional pull and a sumo pull with the same bar load are different spinal loads.
- **Picking things up off the floor badly is not uniquely dangerous.** It is simply a long moment arm. The same arithmetic applies to a stone load, a yoke pick or a bag of compost. Shorten the arm where you can and treat the rest as training load.
- **The number on the bar is not the number on the spine.** When a programme caps "axial load" it is the moment, and the compression that follows from it, that the cap is really about.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Moment = force × perpendicular distance.** The moment arm is the horizontal offset from the pivot to the force, not the height.
2. **The lumbar spine is the pivot.** The bar and the trunk's own weight both act in front of it in a hinge.
3. **Muscles have short arms.** The extensors sit close to the spine, so they generate a force many times the bar's weight to balance it.
4. **That force compresses the spine.** Modelled deadlift compression is several times bar load and scales with bar-to-spine distance.
5. **Bar path is the lever you control.** Keeping the bar close is a reduction in spinal load without a reduction in weight.

</KeyList>

<Check kind="concept">
Two lifters pull the same weight. One keeps the bar against the shins; the other lets it drift a hand's width forward. Why is the second lifter's spine loaded more heavily even though the bar is identical?
<Fragment slot="answer">
The external moment about the lumbar spine is force times horizontal distance. The drift lengthens the moment arm, so the moment rises with no change in load, and the extensors, working through their own short arm, must produce proportionally more force to balance it. That force compresses the spine. Why it matters: bar path changes spinal load as surely as adding plates does, and it is the lever the lifter can change set to set.
</Fragment>
</Check>

<Check kind="apply">
What if a lifter switched from a conventional to a more upright pulling style with the same bar load? Which two moment arms change, and in which direction?
<Fragment slot="answer">
The bar's horizontal distance from the spine typically shortens, and the moment arm of the trunk's own mass shortens as the torso becomes more vertical. Both reduce the external moment the extensors must balance. Why it matters: "the same weight" can be a materially different spinal load depending on the geometry of the lift, which is why load caps in a plan are about moment, not just bar weight.
</Fragment>
</Check>

<Check kind="recall">
Use the calculator above with a 100 kg load. What happens to the moment when you change the arm from 10 cm to 20 cm, and what does that tell you about how the two inputs trade off?
<Fragment slot="answer">
The moment doubles; the two inputs multiply, so each is as powerful as the other. Why it matters: a lifter who cannot reduce the weight can still reduce the spinal load by reducing the distance.
