<Lede>Each spinal segment has a small range near its resting position where it moves with almost no resistance, and a larger range where ligaments and the disc stiffen against further motion. Muscles control the first range; the passive tissues set the limits of the second. "Neutral spine" in lifting is a coaching cue, not a single position.</Lede>

## The load-displacement curve

Take one motion segment, two <Structure id="FMA13075">lumbar vertebrae</Structure> and the <Structure id="FMA13894">disc</Structure> between them, and bend it in a laboratory rig. Plot the force you apply against how far it moves. The curve is not a straight line. Near the resting position the segment moves easily; small forces produce comparatively large motion. Past a certain point the curve steepens sharply as the <Structure>anterior longitudinal ligament</Structure>, the <Structure>posterior longitudinal ligament</Structure>, the facet capsules and the annulus come under tension <Source id="adams-dolan" />.

Panjabi's model names the two parts. The flat part is the **neutral zone**, the range over which the segment is moved by very little force. The steep part is the **elastic zone**, where the passive tissues resist. The whole range of motion is the sum of the two <Source id="physiopedia" />. The model is a description of cadaveric mechanics; its clinical extensions are debated, but the shape of the curve is well established.

## Who controls which zone

In the neutral zone, by definition, the ligaments and disc are slack. Something else has to position the segment, and that something is muscle. The short segmental muscles described in the [anatomy track](/learn/anatomy/deep-vs-global-muscles/), together with the global trunk muscles under a brace, stiffen the segment and hold it where the lifter wants it. In the elastic zone the passive tissues take over and the limit of motion is set by their stiffness and their failure load <Source id="openstax-ap-2e">ch. 9</Source>.

This is the mechanical reason trunk endurance appears in rehabilitation plans. A spine that is positioned by muscle needs muscle that can hold the position for the duration of the set and the session, which is a question of endurance as much as strength <Source id="mcgill-1999-endurance-norms" />.

## What "neutral spine" does and does not mean

Lifting coaches say "neutral" to mean roughly: keep the lumbar segments near the middle of their neutral zones, neither rounded into end-range flexion nor arched into end-range extension, and hold them there under load. Three things follow from the mechanics.

First, neutral is a zone, not a point. Segments move within it by several degrees under almost no load, and a lifter cannot hold a single angle to the degree even if they wanted to. Visible small movement during a heavy pull is not a failure of neutral.

Second, neutral is defined per segment and per person. A lifter whose resting lumbar curve is flatter or deeper than average has a different neutral from the textbook picture, and chasing someone else's shape is chasing the wrong thing.

Third, leaving the zone is not an injury. The cadaveric work that gives the zone its shape also shows what fails at end range under compression, and under repeated cycles of flexion the disc is the structure that gives way in those specimens <Source id="adams-dolan" />. That is a mechanism, found in dead tissue without muscle or adaptation, and the human in-vivo literature does not show that lumbar flexion during lifting predicts injury; the [flexion-compression article](/learn/biomechanics/) takes that question up directly. The claim that any departure from neutral is dangerous is `[contested]`.

## What this means in the gym

- **Brace to position, not to freeze.** The brace's job is to hold the segments in the middle of their zones under load, not to eliminate all motion.
- **Find your own neutral.** Set the spine where the hips can hinge freely and the extensors feel loaded but not pinched, then learn to reproduce that position, not a photograph.
- **End range under heavy load is the thing to train carefully, not to fear.** Loaded flexion exists in strongman and in life. Progress it as you would any other load, which the training-science track covers.
- **Fatigue moves you out of the zone.** Late in a long set the muscles that position the segment tire, and the passive tissues start doing more of the work. That is a reason to cap set length on heavy axial lifts, not a reason to avoid them.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **The curve has two parts.** A neutral zone of low-resistance motion and an elastic zone where ligaments and disc stiffen.
2. **Muscle controls the neutral zone.** The passive tissues are slack there; position is a motor task.
3. **Passive tissue sets the limits.** In the elastic zone stiffness and failure load belong to ligaments, facets and the annulus.
4. **Neutral is a zone, not a point.** It is individual and it includes several degrees of motion.
5. **Leaving the zone is a load, not an injury.** Cadaveric end-range failure is mechanism, not prescription; in-vivo flexion is not shown to predict injury.

</KeyList>

<Check kind="concept">
Why does a cadaveric motion segment move easily near its resting position but stiffen sharply further out, and what does that imply about which tissues position the spine during a lift?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Near rest the ligaments, facet capsules and annulus are slack, so little force moves the segment; as it approaches end range those tissues come under tension and resist. Because nothing passive holds the segment in the neutral zone, muscles must do it. Why it matters: holding a position under load is a trained motor and endurance task, which is why trunk endurance is part of the plan.

<Check kind="apply">
A coach tells a lifter that their lower back "moved" during a heavy deadlift and that this means they lifted dangerously. What does the neutral zone model say about that claim?
<Fragment slot="answer">
The neutral zone includes several degrees of motion under almost no load, so visible small movement is not evidence of leaving neutral. Even leaving the zone is a change in which tissues bear load, not an injury; the cadaveric failure data are a mechanism in dead tissue, and in-vivo flexion during lifting is not shown to predict injury. Why it matters: a cue that treats every millimetre of movement as danger teaches fear rather than skill.

What if a lifter did a long set of heavy axial work to the point of trunk fatigue? Which zone's tissues start doing more of the positioning, and what practical rule follows?
