<Lede>Bracing turns the abdomen into a pressurised cylinder that stiffens the trunk and helps the back muscles hold a heavy load. How much of the benefit comes from pressure itself and how much from the stiffness of the muscles that create it is contested, and belts help with the pressure part.</Lede>

## The cylinder

The abdomen is a fluid-filled cavity with muscular walls. Its roof is the <Structure>diaphragm</Structure>, its floor is the pelvic floor, and its walls are the <Structure>transversus abdominis</Structure>, the obliques and <Structure>rectus abdominis</Structure> in front and the deep back muscles and lumbar spine behind <Source id="openstax-ap-2e">ch. 11</Source>. When the diaphragm descends on a held breath and the abdominal wall contracts against it, the contents cannot escape and the pressure inside rises. That is **intra-abdominal pressure** (IAP), and the deliberate act of raising it before a lift is **bracing**.

A pressurised cylinder resists bending. Push on the side of an inflated ball and it springs back; the same happens to a braced trunk under the forward-bending moment described in the [torque article](/learn/biomechanics/torque-and-moment-arms/).

## Two accounts of how it helps

The textbook account is that IAP pushes up on the diaphragm and down on the pelvic floor, producing an extension moment on the trunk that relieves the back extensors of some of their work <Source id="shelton-exercise-science" />. On that account the pressure itself is doing the lifting.

The second account is that the pressure is mostly a by-product. What stiffens the trunk is the co-contraction of all the trunk muscles at once: the abdominal wall, the extensors, the diaphragm and the pelvic floor pulling in every direction simultaneously turn the lumbar spine from a flexible column into a rigid one. The pressure is what you get when you do that; the stiffness is what matters. The relative size of these two contributions is `[contested]` and this site does not take a side. Both accounts agree that a braced trunk is a stiffer trunk and that a stiffer trunk transmits force from the floor to the bar with less unwanted motion at the spine.

A caution for the reader keen on mechanism. Whatever the pressure does, the muscles that create it also compress the spine, because their lines of pull run along it. Bracing is not free; it is a trade in which more stiffness is bought with more compression. Modelled lumbar compression in heavy pulls already runs to several times the bar's weight, and part of that is the trunk's own co-contraction <Source id="deadlift-loads-2022" />.

## Belts

A lifting belt gives the abdominal wall something to push against. Pressing the abdomen out into a belt raises IAP further than the same effort without one, and most lifters report feeling more stable under heavy loads with a belt on. Beyond that, strong claims thin out quickly. Whether belts reduce injury has not been shown; whether they weaken the trunk if worn habitually has not been shown either. Both common assertions are `[contested]`. The defensible position is that a belt is a tool for raising IAP on the heaviest sets, and that a lifter who cannot brace without a belt has a bracing skill to practise, not a belt to buy.

## Bracing as a skill

Bracing is a motor skill and it can be learned and measured. The pattern that most coaches teach is: breathe into the belly and lower ribs rather than the chest, then tighten the whole circumference of the trunk as if about to take a punch, then hold the breath for the hard part of the lift and exhale through the sticking point or at the top. The version that goes wrong is drawing the stomach in, which lengthens the cylinder and softens the wall instead of stiffening it.

Capacity to hold a brace is trainable. Timed trunk holds, and the ratios between them, give a simple picture of whether the flexors, extensors and side walls are in balance <Source id="mcgill-1999-endurance-norms" />; the plan's Phase 0 uses those ratios as gates, and the McGill calculator on the [plan page](/plan/) computes them.

## Blood pressure and the Valsalva

Holding the breath against a closed glottis is a Valsalva manoeuvre, and it raises blood pressure sharply for the duration of the strain. This is normal in heavy lifting and resolves on exhalation. People with uncontrolled hypertension, a history of aneurysm, retinal problems or recent abdominal or hernia surgery should ask a clinician before training with a maximal brace. This site does not give medical clearance.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **IAP is pressure in a muscular cylinder.** Diaphragm on top, pelvic floor below, abdominal wall and spine around.
2. **A pressurised cylinder resists bending.** Bracing stiffens the trunk against the forward-bending moment of a lift.
3. **The mechanism is contested.** Pressure acting on the diaphragm versus co-contraction stiffness; both agree the trunk gets stiffer.
4. **Stiffness costs compression.** The muscles that brace also load the spine along its axis.
5. **Belts raise IAP; the rest is unproven.** Injury reduction and trunk weakening are both unshown claims.
6. **Bracing is a skill with a measurable capacity.** Timed holds and their ratios are how the plan tracks it.

</KeyList>

<Check kind="concept">
Why does a braced trunk resist bending more than a relaxed one, and why does that not make bracing free?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Bracing pressurises the abdominal cylinder and co-contracts every trunk muscle at once, turning the lumbar spine into a stiffer column that resists the forward moment of the load. The same muscles pull along the spine, so the stiffness is bought with additional compression. Why it matters: bracing is a trade that is worth making on heavy sets, not a safety blanket that removes load from the spine.

<Check kind="apply">
A lifter says they feel unstable in a squat without a belt and asks whether to wear one for every set. What is the defensible answer and why?
<Fragment slot="answer">
A belt raises intra-abdominal pressure by giving the abdominal wall something to push into, so it is a reasonable tool for the heaviest sets. Feeling unable to brace without it points to a skill to practise on lighter sets rather than a reason to belt everything, and claims that belts either prevent injury or weaken the trunk are unproven either way. Why it matters: the decision rests on what is known, and what is known is only that belts raise pressure.
</Fragment>
</Check>

<Check kind="recall">
What if a lifter were told to "suck the stomach in" to protect their back during a deadlift? What is wrong with that cue in terms of the cylinder?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Drawing the abdomen in lengthens and narrows the cylinder and relaxes the wall against the pressure, which is the opposite of what stiffens the trunk; bracing pushes outward around the whole circumference. Why it matters: a plausible-sounding cue can undo the very mechanism it is meant to use.
</Fragment>
</Check>
