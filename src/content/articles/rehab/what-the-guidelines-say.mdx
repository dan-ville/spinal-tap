<Lede>The three major low back pain guidelines of the last decade, from JOSPT, the WHO and NICE, agree on the shape of care. Stay active, exercise, learn what the pain is, and avoid passive-only treatment, routine imaging and most drugs. They disagree on detail and none of them tells you what to lift or how much.</Lede>

## Why read guidelines at all

A guideline is a committee's answer to the question "given every trial we could find, what should a clinician do?" It is not the best evidence for any single claim; the trials and reviews underneath it are. What a guideline adds is breadth and a grading system. It tells you which recommendations rest on many consistent trials and which rest on a handful of weak ones, and it does so across the whole problem rather than one intervention at a time.

That makes guidelines the right place to start for a self-coaching lifter, for a reason that has little to do with their authority. They are the consensus you would have to argue against. If a plan departs from all three, it needs a reason. If it sits inside them, it needs much less defending. The Lancet series that prompted much of the recent rewriting was blunt about the gap between what guidelines recommend and what people with back pain actually receive: imaging, rest, opioids and surgery are overused, and education, activity and exercise are underused <Source id="lancet-2018-lbp-series" />.

## JOSPT 2021: the physical therapy guideline

The 2021 revision of the American physical therapy guideline grades interventions from A to F on the strength and consistency of the trial evidence. For chronic low back pain, exercise that targets trunk strengthening and endurance carries a Grade A, as do general exercise and aerobic or multimodal programmes. Specific-exercise approaches and manual therapy carry weaker grades. The guideline recommends against bed rest and against care that is passive only <Source id="jospt-2021-lbp-cpg" />.

<Evidence id="jospt-2021-lbp-cpg" />

Two things in that card matter more than the grade. First, an A grade reports the consistency of the evidence, not the size of the effect. The trials behind it show modest average benefits, and the differences between exercise modes are small. Second, the recommendation is for a category of exercise, not a dose. "Trunk strengthening and endurance" does not say three sets of ten, and it does not name a lift.

## WHO 2023: the primary-care guideline

The World Health Organization guideline is the newest and the most conservative in its language. It was built with the GRADE method, which forces a panel to state how certain it is about each recommendation. The result is a long list of conditional recommendations in favour of education and advice, structured exercise programmes, and several other therapies, with most of the underlying evidence rated low or very low certainty <Source id="who-2023-clbp" />.

<Evidence id="who-2023-clbp" />

The recommendations against are more useful to a lifter than the recommendations for. The WHO advises against lumbar braces, traction, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation and opioids. If you have been told that a belt, a stretching table or a nerve-stimulation unit is the thing that will fix a chronic back, the most cautious guideline available disagrees.

A conditional recommendation means the panel thought most people would want the intervention but that the evidence could not rule out it being unhelpful for some. Read "structured exercise programme, conditional, low certainty" as "probably helpful, choose one you will keep doing, do not expect it to work like a drug trial".

## NICE NG59: the UK guideline with a caveat

NICE NG59 covers low back pain with or without sciatica in anyone over sixteen. Its first-line advice is self-management and a group exercise programme; manual therapy and psychological therapy are to be offered only as part of a package that includes exercise. It advises against routine imaging in non-specialist settings, and against belts, corsets, traction, acupuncture and most drug classes <Source id="nice-ng59" />.

<Evidence id="nice-ng59" />

NG59 carries a caveat that the other two do not. A surveillance update dated July 2026 reportedly removed the psychological-therapy recommendations. This site has not confirmed that against the published guideline, so the psychological-therapy line above is provisional and should be checked at nice.org.uk before anyone relies on it. The exercise, imaging and drug recommendations are not affected by that update as far as is known, and they are the ones a self-coaching lifter actually uses.

## Where the three agree

Read side by side, the three documents converge on a short list:

| Recommendation | JOSPT 2021 | WHO 2023 | NICE NG59 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stay active, exercise | Grade A | Conditional for | First-line |
| Education about the pain | Supported | Conditional for | Self-management advice |
| Routine imaging | Not addressed | Not addressed | Against |
| Belts, braces, traction | Not addressed | Against | Against |
| Opioids for chronic LBP | Not addressed | Against | Against |
| Passive-only care | Against | Against by omission | Against by omission |

"Not addressed" means the guideline's scope did not include the question, not that it approved the intervention. The JOSPT document is a physical therapy guideline and does not cover prescribing or imaging.

The Cochrane review on exercise for chronic low back pain is the single piece of evidence most of this rests on: across two hundred and forty-nine trials, exercise probably reduces pain and improves function compared with no treatment or usual care, with small to moderate effects and no exercise type standing out <Source id="hayden-2021-cochrane" />. Every guideline cites it or its predecessors.

## Where they differ, and why it does not matter much here

They differ on manual therapy, on needling, on massage and on which psychological approaches to name. Those differences come from different review dates, different scopes and different cost-effectiveness assumptions. For a lifter building a plan around progressive loading, none of those differences changes what to do next week. The things that would change the plan, a dose, a lift selection, a progression rule, are absent from all three.

That absence is the point of the rest of this track. The [exercise modes article](/learn/rehab/exercise-modes-compared/) looks at what the trials say when modes are compared directly. The [cognitive functional therapy article](/learn/rehab/cognitive-functional-therapy/) covers the one approach with a large recent trial behind it. The [McGill approach](/learn/rehab/mcgill-approach-and-critics/), [passive therapies](/learn/rehab/passive-therapies/) and [return-to-sport gates](/learn/rehab/return-to-sport-gates/) articles fill in what the guidelines leave open.

## What a guideline cannot do for you

It cannot clear a symptom. Every guideline in this article scopes itself to non-specific low back pain, which is a diagnosis made after specific causes have been excluded. The red-flag list at the top of this page is the boundary; the [plan](/plan/) routes any of them to a clinician before self-direction starts.

It cannot choose your dose. The [training-science track](/learn/training-science/) covers volume, intensity and progression, and the [self-coaching track](/learn/self-coaching/) turns them into a weekly decision.

It cannot tell you that exercise will work for you. A moderate average effect across a trial population is compatible with a large effect for some people and none for others. The only way to know which group you are in is to run the programme and keep a log.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Three guidelines, one shape of care.** JOSPT 2021, WHO 2023 and NICE NG59 all put activity, exercise and education first, and all advise against passive-only care.
2. **Grades measure consistency, not effect size.** JOSPT's Grade A for trunk strengthening means the trials agree, not that the benefit is large.
3. **The recommendations against are the useful ones.** Belts, traction, TENS, routine imaging and opioids are advised against by the guidelines that address them.
4. **NG59 carries an unverified caveat.** A July 2026 surveillance update reportedly removed the psychological-therapy recommendations; treat that line as provisional until checked.
5. **No guideline gives a dose or names a lift.** That gap is what the rest of the rehab and training-science tracks are for.
6. **Guidelines apply after screening.** They cover non-specific low back pain; red flags leave their scope and go to a clinician.

</KeyList>

Name one intervention that both the WHO 2023 guideline and NICE NG59 recommend against, and state what a JOSPT Grade A recommendation does and does not tell you.
<Fragment slot="answer">
Both the WHO and NICE advise against lumbar belts or braces, traction and opioids for chronic low back pain. A JOSPT Grade A tells you the trial evidence for that intervention is consistent and of good quality; it does not tell you the effect is large, which exercises to do, or at what dose. Why it matters: reading a grade as an effect size, or a recommendation as a prescription, is the commonest way to over-read a guideline.

<Check id="what-the-guidelines-say-2" kind="concept">
In your own words, explain why a guideline recommending "structured exercise" with a conditional, low-certainty rating is still useful to someone planning a lifting programme.
<Fragment slot="answer">
The rating means the panel thought exercise is probably helpful but could not be sure from the trials that it helps everyone; it does not mean exercise is doubtful. For a lifter the useful content is the direction (active, not passive), the things advised against (belts, traction, rest), and the honest admission that the evidence does not pick a mode or dose, which leaves those choices to the training-science principles and a log. Why it matters: the guideline is the consensus a plan has to sit inside or argue against, and it tells you which parts of the plan are supported and which are your own judgement.
