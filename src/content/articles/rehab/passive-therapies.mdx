<Lede>Manual therapy, massage, acupuncture and dry needling, electrical stimulation, traction and braces are the treatments done to you rather than by you. The WHO 2023 guideline conditionally recommends some of them and recommends against others; NICE NG59 allows manual therapy only inside a package that includes exercise and advises against the rest. The Cochrane reviews underneath find small, short-lived effects at low to moderate certainty. None of them is a programme. Each may be an adjunct to one.</Lede>

## What "passive" means and why the word is loaded

A passive therapy is one where the person receiving it is still. Someone else applies the force, the needle, the current or the heat. Spinal manipulation and mobilisation, massage, acupuncture and dry needling, TENS and other electrotherapies, ultrasound, traction, belts and corsets all sit in this group. Exercise, education and graded exposure sit in the other group, the active one, because the person does the work and carries what they learned home.

The word is loaded because every guideline in the [guidelines article](/learn/rehab/what-the-guidelines-say/) warns against care that is passive only. That is not the same as warning against passive care. The distinction is the whole subject of this article, and getting it right stops two mistakes: dismissing a treatment that could make the next six weeks of training more tolerable, and paying for a treatment as if it were the training.

## What the three guidelines say

**WHO 2023.** The World Health Organization guideline, built with GRADE, gives conditional recommendations in favour of needling therapies, spinal manipulative therapy and massage for chronic primary low back pain in adults, alongside its recommendations for education and structured exercise. It recommends against lumbar braces and belts, traction and TENS. Almost all of the underlying evidence was rated low or very low certainty, and a conditional recommendation means the panel judged that most people would want the option but many would not <Source id="who-2023-clbp" />.

<Evidence id="who-2023-clbp" />

**NICE NG59.** The English guideline is stricter. Manual therapy, meaning manipulation, mobilisation or soft-tissue techniques such as massage, is to be considered only as part of a treatment package that includes exercise, with or without psychological therapy. On its own, it is not recommended. Acupuncture, TENS, ultrasound, interferential therapy, belts, corsets, foot orthotics and traction are recommended against. The guideline's reasoning rests on moderate-certainty evidence and on cost-effectiveness analysis for the NHS, which may not transfer elsewhere <Source id="nice-ng59" />.

**JOSPT 2021.** The American physical therapy guideline grades manual therapy lower than exercise for chronic pain and recommends against passive-only care; it is included here for completeness because it is the one most physiotherapists will cite <Source id="jospt-2021-lbp-cpg" />.

The two newest guidelines therefore disagree on acupuncture and on whether manipulation and massage can stand alone. Both agree they should not be the only thing happening. Both agree against traction, braces and TENS.

## What the Cochrane reviews find

Guidelines are built on systematic reviews, and for passive therapies the Cochrane reviews are the ones to read. They are older than the WHO guideline and their abstracts and plain-language summaries are free.

**Spinal manipulation.** The Cochrane review of spinal manipulative therapy for chronic low back pain found high-quality evidence that manipulation has a small, statistically significant but not clinically relevant short-term effect on pain and function compared with other interventions, and very low quality evidence against sham. In plain words: it is about as good as the other things it was compared with, and not better <Source id="rubinstein-2011-cochrane-smt" />.

**Massage.** The Cochrane review of massage for low back pain found low to very low quality evidence of short-term improvement in pain for both acute and chronic pain, with little effect on function and no evidence of lasting benefit. Adverse events were minor, mostly soreness <Source id="furlan-2015-cochrane-massage" />.

**TENS.** The Cochrane review of transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation against placebo for chronic low back pain found conflicting evidence and could not conclude that TENS reduces pain or improves function; the trials were few and small <Source id="khadilkar-2008-cochrane-tens" />.

Set these against the exercise review from the same organisation: modest but consistent benefit on pain and function that persists, at least in part, at long-term follow-up <Source id="hayden-2021-cochrane" />. The pattern is clear enough to build a decision on. Passive therapies, where they work, work briefly. Exercise works modestly and keeps working.

## The honest use case for a lifter

Short-term pain relief is not nothing. A lifter in a flare who cannot tolerate the first week of a [graded-exposure block](/learn/pain-science/graded-exposure/) might tolerate it after a session of manual therapy or a massage, and that week of training is the thing that produces the durable change. Used that way, a passive therapy is a window-opener for the active work, and the guidelines allow exactly this. NICE's "as part of a package that includes exercise" is the precise formulation.

The failure mode is the standing weekly appointment that becomes the treatment. The relief wears off in days, the appointment is rebooked, the training block never starts, and after six months the lifter has spent a great deal of money on a treatment the Cochrane reviews say has no lasting effect while skipping the one that does. The guidelines' warning against passive-only care is aimed at this exact pattern.

A simple rule from this evidence: if a passive therapy is in your plan, write down what active work it is meant to unlock and when you expect to stop needing it. If you cannot answer the first question, the therapy has no job. If the answer to the second keeps moving, the therapy has become the plan.

## What this article deliberately does not do

It does not describe how any of these techniques are performed, what a session should contain, or which practitioner to see. Those are clinical skills and the sources that teach them are not open licence, so this site links them rather than reproducing them. Physiopedia carries practitioner-level pages on each modality with their own evidence summaries <Source id="physiopedia" />. Barbell Medicine has written at length, from a lifting perspective, on why passive care should stay subordinate to loading <Source id="barbell-medicine" />, and E3 Rehab has reviewed the manual therapy and dry needling evidence for a lay audience <Source id="e3-rehab" />. These are Tier C sources: useful, not primary, and the reason to go to them is to see the argument made in full rather than to take their conclusion.

Braces and belts deserve one extra sentence because lifters own them. The guidelines' recommendation against belts is a recommendation against wearing a brace to manage daily back pain. A lifting belt worn for heavy top sets is a different use with a different purpose, discussed in the [bracing article](/learn/biomechanics/intra-abdominal-pressure-and-bracing/), and the evidence against braces for pain does not speak to it.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Passive means done to you.** Manipulation, massage, needling, TENS, ultrasound, traction and braces. Active means exercise, education and exposure.
2. **WHO 2023 conditionally recommends needling, manipulation and massage; against braces, traction and TENS.** Low to very low certainty throughout.
3. **NICE NG59 allows manual therapy only inside a package that includes exercise.** It recommends against acupuncture, TENS, ultrasound, belts, corsets and traction.
4. **Cochrane finds small, short-term effects.** Manipulation no better than alternatives, massage short-term pain relief only, TENS inconclusive. Exercise's benefit is modest and lasts.
5. **The use case is a window-opener.** Relief that lets the next training block start is legitimate; a standing appointment that replaces the block is the failure mode every guideline warns about.
6. **Technique belongs to Tier C sources.** This site links them and does not copy them. A lifting belt for heavy sets is not the brace the guidelines advise against.

</KeyList>

On which passive therapies do WHO 2023 and NICE NG59 agree, and where do they disagree?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Both recommend against traction, braces and belts for pain, and TENS. Both say passive care should not be the only treatment. They disagree on acupuncture and needling, which WHO conditionally recommends and NICE recommends against, and on whether manipulation and massage can be offered alone, which WHO conditionally allows and NICE permits only inside a package that includes exercise. Why it matters: where guidelines disagree the evidence is weak enough that the lifter's own log of whether a therapy unlocked training is the better guide.

<Check id="passive-therapies-2" kind="concept">
In your own words, explain the difference between a guideline recommending against passive-only care and recommending against passive care.
<Fragment slot="answer">
Against passive-only care means the guideline objects to a plan in which treatments done to the person are the whole plan, because the reviews show their effects are short-lived and exercise is what produces lasting change. It does not forbid a passive therapy inside a plan built around exercise, and WHO and NICE both allow at least some of them in that role. Why it matters: the distinction lets a lifter use a session of manual therapy to start a training block without mistaking the session for the block.

<Check id="passive-therapies-3" kind="apply">
A lifter has had weekly spinal manipulation for four months. Each session helps for two or three days. They have not started the Phase 1 loading in the plan because they are "waiting until the back settles". What does the evidence in this article say about this situation, and what is the one question to ask?
<Fragment slot="answer">
The pattern matches the Cochrane finding exactly: manipulation produces a small short-term effect and no lasting one, so the relief wearing off in days is the expected result, not a sign that more sessions are needed. The guidelines' warning against passive-only care describes this situation. The one question is what active work the manipulation is meant to unlock and when, and the honest answer here is that it has become the plan rather than a window into one, so the Phase 1 loading should start, with the manipulation kept only if it makes the training days more tolerable. Why it matters: waiting for pain to settle before loading is the loop that passive care alone cannot close.
