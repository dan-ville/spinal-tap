<Lede>A gate is a measurable condition that has to be true before the next phase of loading starts. Gates replace the calendar, because tissues and nervous systems do not heal on a schedule and the elite-athlete literature shows the same return rates whether treatment was surgical or not. This article reproduces the plan's gates with their basis on each, computes the ratio and load gates rather than hand-reporting them, and explains what to do when a gate is failed.</Lede>

## Why gates and not dates

The instinct after a back episode is to ask how long. Six weeks? Three months? The question has no good answer because the thing that limits return is not a tissue timeline. Most disc herniations shrink on their own over months, as the [natural history article](/learn/pathology/natural-history-and-resorption/) covers, and most people with pain have imaging findings that match pain-free people, as the [pathology track](/learn/pathology/degeneration-and-asymptomatic-findings/) shows. What limits return is capacity and tolerance, and those are measurable now rather than predictable in advance.

The elite-athlete literature makes the point from the top end. In athletes with symptomatic lumbar disc herniation, about four in five returned to play whether they were treated with surgery or without it, and the time to return was similar in both arms <Source id="sedrak-2021" />. After microdiscectomy specifically, return rates were in the same range and later careers were not consistently shorter <Source id="overley-2016" />. These are observational, low-certainty findings about people with resources most lifters do not have, and the cards say so. What they establish is that return to heavy sport is the normal outcome, and that the treatment chosen did not change the rate. What changed it, in every one of those programmes, was a graded return with criteria.

<Evidence id="sedrak-2021" />

A gate turns "criteria" into something a self-coaching lifter can actually run. It is a condition that is either true or false today, that is checked the same way every time, and that is logged. The [plan](/plan/) on this site is built from fourteen of them. Your gate state stays in your browser; nothing is sent anywhere.

## What a gate must be

Three properties, in order of importance.

**Measurable without judgement.** A gate that reads "the back feels ready" is not a gate; it is the feeling that gates are meant to replace. Seconds held, kilograms lifted, consecutive weeks logged and yes-or-no answers to red-flag questions all qualify.

**Repeatable.** The same test, performed the same way, at the same time of day. The McGill battery is used in the plan precisely because its day-to-day reliability was measured <Source id="mcgill-1999-endurance-norms" />. A retest performed with different form or at a different hour is a different test.

**Labelled with its basis.** Every gate below carries one of three labels. A source id means the number comes from a cited paper. `[heuristic]` means it is an opening position that the plan's author chose and that your own log should replace. The plan page labels its load gates "proposed", which is the same thing said differently: a labelled guess, not a finding.

## The gates, with their basis

**Phase 0 exit.** Fourteen consecutive daily check-ins logged. `[heuristic]`. The number is arbitrary; the property it tests, that you can keep a log, is not.

**Phase 1 exit.** Three endurance-ratio gates from the McGill norms, three from the plan.

|---|---|
| Flexor/extensor ratio below 1.0 | mcgill-1999-endurance-norms |
| Side bridge/extensor ratio about 0.75 | mcgill-1999-endurance-norms |
| Left and right side bridge within 5% | mcgill-1999-endurance-norms |
| Four consecutive weeks without a bad next-day verdict | `[heuristic]` |
| Pain-free RDL at 1.0× bodyweight for 8 | `[heuristic]` |
| Suitcase carry at 0.5× bodyweight per hand for 30 m | `[heuristic]` |

The [McGill article](/learn/rehab/mcgill-approach-and-critics/) explains why those ratios are low certainty as predictors and still worth using as a retest. The three heuristic gates are the ones your log should eventually overwrite: a lifter who trains clean for four weeks at an RDL well below bodyweight has still met the gate that matters, which is tolerance, and may reasonably argue the load numbers down.

**Phase 2 exit.** Two load gates, one clean-weeks gate, one retest.

| Gate | Basis |
|---|---|
| Deadlift from the floor at 1.5× bodyweight for 5 at RPE ≤ 8, no yellow verdict | `[heuristic]` |
| Squat at 1.5× bodyweight for 5 | `[heuristic]` |
| Eight consecutive clean weeks | `[heuristic]` |
| Endurance ratios held at the monthly retest | mcgill-1999-endurance-norms |

The RPE cap is doing real work in the first gate. Repetitions in reserve tracks actual proximity to failure well in trained lifters and poorly in untrained ones <Source id="helms-rpe-rir" />, so "RPE 8" means two reps in the tank, honestly rated, and the [training-science track](/learn/training-science/) covers how to calibrate it. The bodyweight multiples are opening positions. **Phase 3 exit.** First competition entered and completed; three consecutive injury-free blocks. Both `[heuristic]`. The DOTS marker toward elite, around 450 points, is likewise a heuristic from competition data, flagged as approximate and multi-year on the plan page.

**Phase 4.** No exit. The monthly red-flag screen and McGill retest continue `[heuristic]`, because the rules never retire.

## What a failed gate means

A failed gate is information, not a verdict on you. It says the next phase's loading is not yet supported by the measurement you chose, which is exactly what you set it up to say. The response depends on which kind of gate failed.

A failed **ratio gate** says the endurance profile has not changed enough. Keep the daily endurance work, keep the current loading, retest in a month. It does not say stop lifting, and the evidence does not support the ratio as a danger signal.

A failed **load gate** says a working set at that load is not yet tolerable or not yet there. Progress the lift with double progression and retest. If the limit is tolerance rather than strength, the plan's regression rule applies: drop a rung on range, then moment arm, then load, never the movement's identity.

A failed **clean-weeks gate** is the one to take most seriously, because it says the current loading is producing bad next-day verdicts. The plan's rule is that two bad days in a week cut that week's axial hard sets by forty percent while intensity is held, `[heuristic]`, and the streak restarts. If the streak keeps restarting at the same loading, the loading is the problem and the phase has to be rebuilt lower.

None of this overrides a red flag. A red flag is not a gate; it is a boundary, and it sends you to a clinician regardless of what the other gates say. The [red-flags article](/learn/pathology/red-flags/) lists them.

## Why the gates are deliberately modest

The exercise evidence that the whole track rests on finds modest average benefit and no superior mode <Source id="hayden-2021-cochrane" />, and the newest guideline rates almost all of its recommendations as conditional on low-certainty evidence <Source id="who-2023-clbp" />. A gate system that claimed precision would be claiming more than the evidence has. The gates are modest because they are placeholders for your own numbers, and the plan says so on every line. The [self-coaching track](/learn/self-coaching/) covers how the log replaces them over time.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Gates replace calendars.** Return is limited by capacity and tolerance, which are measurable today, not by a tissue timeline.
2. **Elite athletes return at the same rate with or without surgery.** About four in five, low certainty, with graded criteria doing the work in both arms.
3. **A gate is measurable, repeatable and labelled.** Source id or `[heuristic]` on every one; the plan's "proposed" label means the same as heuristic.
4. **Compute the ratio and load gates, never hand-report them.** The McGill and e1rm calculators give the same verdict every time.
5. **A failed gate names the next action.** Ratio: keep training, retest. Load: progress or regress a rung. Clean weeks: cut volume forty percent and rebuild.
6. **Red flags are boundaries, not gates.** They override the whole system and route to a clinician.

</KeyList>

A lifter in Phase 2 has pulled 150 kg for 5 at RPE 8 at a bodyweight of 95 kg, has nine clean weeks, and at the monthly retest their flexor/extensor ratio has risen from 0.8 to 1.1. Which gates pass, which fail, and what is the next step?
<Fragment slot="answer">
The deadlift gate at 1.5× bodyweight is 142.5 kg for 5, so 150 for 5 at RPE 8 passes. Nine clean weeks passes the eight-week gate. The ratio gate fails, because 1.1 is above 1.0 and the ratios were not held. The next step is not to stop pulling, since the ratio is a low-certainty predictor and the load and tolerance gates are both met; it is to add extensor endurance work, hold the current loading rather than advancing to Phase 3, and retest in a month. If the squat gate is also unmet it is the same answer. Why it matters: gates are checked one at a time and each failed gate names its own action, so a single failure delays the phase change without undoing the training that passed.
