<Lede>Stuart McGill's approach to low back pain rests on three ideas, that repeated flexion under load damages discs, that trunk endurance matters more than trunk strength, and that spine-sparing movement plus a small set of endurance exercises builds a stable base. The endurance norms give a repeatable baseline worth measuring. The flexion-avoidance claims come from cadaver and pig-spine work and are contested as a rule for living humans. This article separates what is measurable from what is argued.</Lede>

## Why this approach has a whole article

No other single researcher has shaped how lifters think about their backs as much as Stuart McGill. The "big three" of the curl-up, side bridge and bird dog, the "neutral spine" instruction, the hip hinge taught as a way to keep the lumbar spine out of flexion, the suspicion of sit-ups and of heavy flexion-based training: all of these trace back to his lab at the University of Waterloo and his books. Much of the [plan](/plan/) on this site borrows from him too. Phase 0 asks you to measure the McGill endurance battery, and Phase 1 uses the big three as daily endurance work with the ratios as exit gates.

That makes it important to be clear about which parts of the approach are measurement, which are mechanism, and which are prescription. They do not carry the same weight, and the critics mostly aim at the third.

## Part one: the endurance norms

In 1999 McGill's group published hold times for three isometric tests in seventy-five healthy university students: a trunk flexor hold at a fixed angle, the Biering-Sørensen extensor hold with the upper body unsupported off a bench, and the side bridge on each side. They then proposed ratios that a balanced profile should show: flexor time divided by extensor time below 1.0, the two side bridges within about five percent of each other, and side bridge time at about three quarters of extensor time <Source id="mcgill-1999-endurance-norms" />.

<Evidence id="mcgill-1999-endurance-norms" />

Read the card's caveats before the numbers. The sample was small, young and pain-free. The ratios are proposed targets, not thresholds validated against injury or recovery, and later studies have found the link between endurance scores and back pain to be weak and inconsistent. What the paper does establish is that the tests are reliable from day to day, which is the property a self-coaching lifter needs most. A measurement you can repeat monthly and compare against your own earlier numbers is useful even if its population norms are shaky.

That is exactly how the plan uses them. The Phase 1 gates built from these ratios carry the source id so you can see where they came from, and the calculator below does the arithmetic so the ratio is never hand-computed.

<Calc kind="mcgill" />

The ratios are the point, not the raw seconds. A lifter whose extensor hold is three minutes but whose flexor hold is four has a profile the approach would call imbalanced, even though both numbers are far above the student norms. Whether the imbalance predicts anything is `[contested]`; whether it has changed since last month is a fact the log can hold.

## Part two: the flexion-intolerance mechanism

The second pillar is mechanical. McGill's lab, with Callaghan, loaded pig cervical spine segments in cycles of flexion and extension under compression and produced posterior disc herniations in a dose-dependent way: more cycles and more compression meant more herniation <Source id="callaghan-mcgill" />. Adams and Dolan's cadaveric work on human lumbar segments points the same direction: combined flexion and compression, especially repeated or sustained, is the loading that moves nucleus material through the posterior annulus <Source id="adams-dolan" />.

The [flexion-compression article](/learn/biomechanics/flexion-compression/) in the biomechanics track covers this in detail. The short version matters here: the mechanism is real in the tissue, and the dose that produced it in the laboratory was thousands of cycles in dead or animal tissue with no healing, no adaptation and no nervous system. The jump from "this loading damages discs on a bench" to "lifters must avoid lumbar flexion" is the jump the critics object to, and it is `[contested]`.

Two lines of evidence push against the strong form. Running, which loads the spine cyclically, is associated with better disc hydration and height in a cross-sectional study, which is the opposite of what a pure wear model predicts for a living tissue. And the asymptomatic-imaging literature in the [pathology track](/learn/pathology/degeneration-and-asymptomatic-findings/) shows disc changes in large numbers of people with no pain at all, which makes a mechanical damage model a poor predictor of who hurts.

The weak form survives the criticism: for a lifter who is currently flexion-intolerant, meaning that loaded flexion reliably provokes them, a period of hinging with the lumbar spine held near neutral is a sensible way to keep training while the sensitivity settles. That is a graded-exposure decision about a provocative movement, not a universal law, and the [neutral zone article](/learn/biomechanics/neutral-zone/) explains why the spine's "neutral" is a range rather than a point.

## Part three: endurance over strength

The third pillar is the claim that trunk muscle endurance protects the back better than trunk strength does, and that training the trunk with low loads for long holds is therefore preferable to loading it heavily. This is the pillar that sits least comfortably with the rest of the evidence on this site.

Steele and colleagues reviewed the deconditioning hypothesis and found that people with chronic low back pain commonly show reduced lumbar extensor strength, endurance and multifidus size, and that isolated lumbar extension training reverses those changes alongside reductions in pain and disability <Source id="steele-2014-lumbar-extension" />. That is a strength-training argument, from authors with a declared interest in isolated-extension machines, and it is low certainty. But it is not alone. When exercise modes are compared head to head, resistance training and motor-control training both come out well and neither beats the other <Source id="owen-2020-bjsm-nma" />, and posterior-chain resistance training does at least as well as general exercise for pain and disability <Source id="tataryn-2021" />. Across all exercise, the Cochrane review finds modest benefit with no mode superior <Source id="hayden-2021-cochrane" />.

<Evidence id="steele-2014-lumbar-extension" />

So the honest statement is that "endurance beats strength" is `[contested]`, and the trial evidence does not support one over the other. Endurance work and heavy loading both help, in the same modest way, and for a lifter the choice between them is a programming choice, not a safety one. The [exercise modes article](/learn/rehab/exercise-modes-compared/) lays that out. The plan keeps the big three as daily low-cost endurance work because they are cheap and because they are the same movements the baseline tests use, not because the evidence says they are better than a deadlift.

## What the critics get right, and what they overreach on

The critics are right that the approach is often taught as settled when its mechanism evidence is cadaveric and its clinical evidence is thin. They are right that "never flex your spine" has frightened a lot of people out of ordinary movement, and that fear of movement is itself a driver of disability, as the [pain-science track](/learn/pain-science/pain-vs-damage/) explains. They are right that the ratios have never been shown to predict anything.

The critics overreach when they conclude that the approach has nothing to offer. The endurance tests are reliable and cheap. Spine-sparing technique is a reasonable short-term regression for someone who is currently provoked by loaded flexion. The hip hinge is a good lift to teach regardless of what one believes about discs. The approach's content survives better than its certainty does.

## How this site uses it

Measurement, yes: the battery and ratios are a Phase 0 baseline and a monthly retest, with source ids on every gate. Regression tool, yes: when a movement provokes, the plan drops a rung on range or moment arm before it drops the movement, which is spine-sparing in the McGill sense without making it a permanent rule. Universal flexion ban, no. Endurance instead of strength, no. The [return-to-sport gates article](/learn/rehab/return-to-sport-gates/) shows how the ratios sit alongside load and symptom gates rather than above them.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Three pillars, three evidence grades.** Endurance norms are a reliable measurement in a small healthy sample; flexion intolerance is a cadaver and animal mechanism; endurance-over-strength is a prescription the trials do not support.
2. **Use the ratios as your own baseline.** Flexor/extensor below 1.0, bridge/extensor about 0.75, sides within 5%: repeatable, low certainty as predictors, useful as a monthly retest against yourself.
3. **Flexion avoidance as a universal rule is `[contested]`.** The tissue mechanism is real on the bench; the jump to living, adapting, pain-free lifters is not established.
4. **Endurance versus strength is `[contested]` and probably a false choice.** Resistance training and motor-control work both help, modestly, and neither beats the other.
5. **Spine-sparing technique is a regression tool, not a law.** Hinge near neutral while loaded flexion provokes you; return range as it settles.
6. **The plan borrows the tests and the big three, not the certainty.** Every gate built from this work carries its source id.

</KeyList>

<Check id="mcgill-approach-and-critics-1" kind="concept">
In your own words, explain why the McGill endurance ratios are worth measuring monthly even though the card rates the evidence behind them low certainty.
<Fragment slot="answer">
The low certainty attaches to the ratios as predictors: they were proposed from seventy-five pain-free students and have not been shown to predict injury or recovery. The tests themselves are reliable from day to day, which is a separate property, and reliability is what makes a repeated measurement informative when compared against your own earlier numbers rather than against a population norm. Why it matters: a self-coaching lifter needs repeatable measurements to see change, and a reliable test with weak norms still does that job.

<Check id="mcgill-approach-and-critics-2" kind="recall">
What kind of tissue and what kind of loading produced the herniations in the Callaghan and McGill study, and why does that limit what the study can say about lifting?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Pig cervical spine segments loaded through thousands of flexion-extension cycles under compression, with herniation increasing with cycles and compression. Dead animal tissue cannot adapt, heal or feel pain, and the dose was far beyond what a training session delivers, so the study demonstrates a mechanism rather than a rule for living lifters. Why it matters: the flexion-avoidance claim is tagged contested because its strongest evidence is this mechanism, not a trial in people.

<Check id="mcgill-approach-and-critics-3" kind="apply">
A lifter has an extensor hold of 160 s, a flexor hold of 190 s, and side bridges of 95 s and 80 s. Using the ratios in this article, which gates do they meet and which do they miss, and what should they do about it?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Flexor over extensor is 190/160, which is above 1.0, so that gate is missed. Average bridge is 87.5 s and 87.5/160 is about 0.55, well below the 0.75 target, so that gate is missed too. The side-to-side difference is 15 s on 95 s, about 16%, so the symmetry gate is missed as well. The sensible response is not alarm, since the ratios are unvalidated as predictors, but to log these as the baseline, add side bridge and extensor endurance work in Phase 1, and retest in a month against these numbers. Why it matters: the ratios tell you what to train and when it has changed, not whether you are in danger.
