<Lede>When exercise modes for chronic low back pain are compared directly, resistance training, motor-control work, Pilates and aerobic exercise all beat doing nothing and none of them clearly beats the others. Posterior-chain loading does at least as well as general exercise. The honest conclusion is that the mode you will keep doing is the right mode, and that heavy hinging is a legitimate choice rather than a risky one.</Lede>

## The question people actually ask

The [guidelines article](/learn/rehab/what-the-guidelines-say/) ends with the admission that no guideline names a mode. Every one of them says "exercise" and stops. The person reading them wants the next sentence: which exercise? Core stability or deadlifts? Pilates or walking? The machine at the physio or the barbell at the gym?

There are three bodies of evidence that bear on that. A Cochrane review that pools everything, a network meta-analysis that ranks modes against each other, and a smaller meta-analysis that isolates the one mode lifters care most about. They are read together here, because each on its own invites over-reading.

## The pooled picture: exercise works, modestly

The Cochrane review on exercise therapy for chronic low back pain is the largest synthesis available. Across two hundred and forty-nine trials, exercise reduced pain by about fifteen points on a hundred-point scale and improved function by about seven points compared with no treatment, usual care or placebo. The effects persisted at long-term follow-up, smaller. Exercise was also modestly better than other conservative treatments <Source id="hayden-2021-cochrane" />.

The review does not identify a superior type. It could not, because the trials it pooled used wildly different programmes, doses and populations. What it establishes is the floor: exercise, in general, is better than not exercising, by an amount that is real and that is not dramatic at the group level.

The group-level qualification matters. A fifteen-point average is compatible with some people improving by forty and others by nothing. That is one reason this site insists on a log; the trial cannot tell you which of those people you are.

## The ranked picture: several modes, no winner

The network meta-analysis by Owen and colleagues is the study to read if you want modes compared. It took eighty-nine trials and used network methods to estimate how each mode performed against every other, including modes that had never been directly compared in a single trial <Source id="owen-2020-bjsm-nma" />.

<Evidence id="owen-2020-bjsm-nma" />

Pilates, stabilisation or motor-control exercise, resistance training and aerobic training all outperformed true control for pain and function. Resistance training and stabilisation also showed gains in mental health. Stretching and McKenzie-style approaches ranked lower, with wide confidence intervals. The rankings between the effective modes overlapped so much that the authors declined to crown any of them.

The authors rate the overall quality of the evidence as low. Most trials were small, short and at risk of bias, and the rankings move depending on which trials are included. The result does not say "resistance training is best". It says "resistance training is one of several modes that work, and nothing in the trials tells you to prefer another one over it".

That is not a disappointing result. For a lifter it is the permission slip. The mode you already know, already enjoy and already have the equipment for is one of the modes that works, and no trial evidence says you should swap it for something you will do for six weeks and abandon.

## The lifter's question: is heavy hinging safe and useful?

The remaining worry, for someone whose training is built around the deadlift and the squat, is whether loading the posterior chain is a reasonable mode for a painful back or a reckless one. The Tataryn meta-analysis addresses that directly <Source id="tataryn-2021" />.

<Evidence id="tataryn-2021" />

Across eight trials, posterior-chain resistance training, meaning hip-hinge, deadlift and back-extension variants, produced greater improvements in pain, disability and strength than general exercise or walking programmes. The differences were modest and the trial count was small, which is why the card rates certainty low. The finding is not that loaded hinging is required. It is that it is a legitimate option that did at least as well as the comparators, and somewhat better on the pooled numbers.

Two things the review cannot tell you. It cannot tell you the dose, because the programmes were heterogeneous. And it cannot tell you about a lifter who is already strong, because the trial populations were drawn from the general public with chronic pain, most of whom had never deadlifted. The [training-science track](/learn/training-science/) covers the dose question; the [graded exposure article](/learn/pain-science/graded-exposure/) covers how a trained lifter steps back into a loaded hinge after a flare.

There is a plausible mechanism behind the posterior-chain result, which is worth knowing as long as it is held loosely. People with chronic low back pain often show reduced lumbar extensor strength, endurance and muscle size, and training that targets those extensors reverses the deficits alongside symptom improvement <Source id="steele-2014-lumbar-extension" />. Whether the deconditioning causes the pain or follows it is unresolved, and pain improvements do not always track strength improvements, so this is a reason to think extensor loading is sensible rather than a proof that weak extensors were the problem.

## How the guidelines fit this

The JOSPT guideline's Grade A for trunk strengthening and endurance sits comfortably with all of this. It is the broadest category that includes posterior-chain loading, motor-control work and the general trunk programmes in the Cochrane pool <Source id="jospt-2021-lbp-cpg" />. The guideline's own caveat, that between-mode differences in trials are small, is exactly what the network meta-analysis found.

## Reading the three together

| Question | Best source | Answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does exercise help at all? | Cochrane 2021 | Yes, small to moderate effect, lasting |
| Which mode is best? | Owen 2020 NMA | Several work; no clear winner; low-certainty evidence |
| Is loaded hinging a reasonable mode? | Tataryn 2021 | Yes; at least as good as general exercise; few trials |
| What dose? | None of these | Not answered; see training science |

The honest summary is three sentences. Exercise beats not exercising. The mode matters less than the trials' designers hoped. Choose the one you will keep doing, and if that is a barbell, the evidence does not stand in your way.

## What this does not license

It does not license loading a presentation that has not been screened. The red flags at the top of this page leave the scope of every study above; the [plan](/plan/) routes them out.

It does not license ignoring the modes that ranked alongside resistance training. Motor-control work and aerobic exercise each earned their place in the network, and a lifter with a long history of only lifting heavy may find the missing piece is the easy aerobic volume they never did.

It does not license a dose. "Posterior-chain resistance training works" says nothing about three sets or ten, twice a week or four times, or how fast to add weight. The [McGill approach](/learn/rehab/) and the [training-science track](/learn/training-science/) take that up.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Exercise beats not exercising, modestly.** Cochrane pooled two hundred and forty-nine trials and found small to moderate, lasting improvements in pain and function.
2. **No mode wins.** The Owen network meta-analysis found Pilates, motor-control, resistance and aerobic training all effective, with overlapping rankings and low-certainty evidence.
3. **Posterior-chain loading is a legitimate mode.** Tataryn found hinge and extension training at least as good as general exercise, across a small number of trials.
4. **Group averages hide individual spread.** A fifteen-point mean is compatible with a large gain for some and none for others; the log is how you find out which you are.
5. **Extensor deconditioning is a plausible mechanism, not a proof.** Chronic back pain often comes with weaker extensors, and training them helps, but cause and effect are not settled.
6. **Choose the mode you will keep doing.** The trial evidence supports consistency over selection, and it never answers the dose question.

</KeyList>

<Check kind="concept">
In your own words, explain why "resistance training ranked among the most effective modes" in a network meta-analysis does not mean "resistance training is the best mode for low back pain".
<Fragment slot="answer">
The rankings of the effective modes overlapped widely, the underlying trials were small and at risk of bias, and the authors rated the evidence low certainty, so the order of the modes is not stable. The result establishes that resistance training works, alongside several others, and gives no reason to prefer another mode over it, which is different from a hierarchy. Why it matters: reading the ranking as a podium leads people to abandon a mode they would have kept doing for one the evidence does not actually prefer.
