## What "degeneration" means on a scan

Disc degeneration is a radiology term for a cluster of changes: loss of water signal in the <Structure>nucleus pulposus</Structure>, loss of disc height, fissures in the <Structure>annulus fibrosus</Structure>, and changes in the bone next to the <Structure>vertebral endplate</Structure>. Mechanically it reflects the slow processes described in the anatomy track: a nucleus that holds less water carries less of the compressive load, the annulus and endplates carry more, and the <Structure id="FMA13894">intervertebral disc</Structure> as a whole loses height and spreads outward <Source id="adams-dolan" />. None of those changes is visible from the outside, and none of them is a measurement of pain.

## Finding one: degeneration is normal with age

In 2015 Brinjikji and colleagues pooled 33 imaging studies covering 3110 people who had no back pain at the time of their scan, and estimated how common each finding was by decade of age <Source id="brinjikji-2015-degeneration" />.

| Finding in pain-free people | Age 20 | Age 80 |
|---|---|---|
| Disc degeneration | 37% | 96% |
| Disc bulge | 30% | 84% |
| Disc protrusion | 29% | 43% |
| Annular fissure | 19% | 29% |

Read the first row slowly. More than a third of pain-free 20-year-olds already have a disc that a radiologist would call degenerate, and by 80 almost everyone does. Degeneration behaves like grey hair: a change that tracks age so closely that its presence, on its own, carries very little information about why someone hurts. The review's authors drew exactly that conclusion: degenerative findings are part of normal ageing and are not themselves associated with pain.

## Finding two: some findings are more common in people who hurt

The same year, the same group asked a different question. Instead of pooling pain-free people alone, they found 14 studies that had scanned both people with low back pain and matched controls without it, 3097 people in total, and compared the two groups <Source id="brinjikji-2015-meta" />. In adults aged 50 and under, several findings were clearly more prevalent in the pain group: disc bulge (odds ratio about 7.5), extrusion (about 4.4), protrusion (about 2.6), Modic type 1 endplate change (about 4) and disc degeneration itself (about 2.2).

So, under 50, a scan showing a bulge or protrusion is *not* equally likely in someone with and without pain. The finding shifts the odds. In older adults the association largely disappears, because the findings have become near-universal in both groups and can no longer discriminate.

## Holding both at once

It is tempting to pick a side. One reading says "scans are meaningless, everyone has these findings"; the other says "the bulge explains the pain". Neither is what the two reviews, read together, support.

- The prevalence review tells you the **base rate**: how common a finding is in people like you who do not hurt. For a 30-year-old with a bulge, that base rate is already around a third.
- The case-control review tells you the **likelihood shift**: how much more common the finding is when pain is present. Under 50, a bulge is several times more common in people with pain.

A finding that is common without pain *and* more common with pain is an imperfect signal. It raises the probability that the disc is involved without establishing it, and it says nothing about which direction the causation runs or whether the finding will still be there in a year. The companion article on [disc terminology](/learn/pathology/bulge-protrusion-extrusion/) covers what the specific words mean; the following articles cover how often herniations shrink on their own and what makes imaging worth doing in the first place.

## What this means for a lifter reading their own report

Three things follow, and none of them is a diagnosis.

1. **A degenerative finding is expected, not alarming.** At any adult age a large share of pain-free peers would show the same.
2. **A finding is not an instruction.** Nothing in either review links a specific scan finding to a specific exercise being safe or unsafe. Decisions about load belong in the rehab and training tracks and in the [plan](/plan/), and are keyed to your own symptoms and capacity, not to a scan word.
3. **Age matters to the reading.** The same finding carries more information under 50 than over it, because over 50 it no longer separates the groups.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Degeneration tracks age.** About 37% of pain-free 20-year-olds and 96% of pain-free 80-year-olds show it on imaging.
2. **Bulge and protrusion are common without pain** (30% and 29% at 20), and both rise with age.
3. **Under 50, the same findings are more prevalent in people with pain**, with bulge showing the largest shift.
4. **Base rate plus likelihood shift, not a verdict.** A finding raises or lowers probability; neither review lets a scan settle whether a disc is the source of symptoms.
5. **Scan words are not load prescriptions.** Training decisions key on symptoms and capacity, not on the report.

</KeyList>

A 28-year-old lifter reads "disc bulge at L5/S1" on their report and concludes that this must be the cause of their pain. Using both Brinjikji reviews, what is the strongest honest statement you can make?
<Fragment slot="answer">
The bulge is more common in people under 50 who have back pain than in matched controls, so it raises the probability the disc is involved. But roughly 30% of pain-free people their age also have a bulge, so its presence cannot establish that it is the cause. The honest statement is "this makes disc involvement more likely, not certain, and a clinician who examined you is the person to weigh it". Why it matters: both over-reading and dismissing the finding ignore one of the two reviews.

<Check id="degeneration-and-asymptomatic-findings-3" kind="concept">
Why does the association between imaging findings and pain largely disappear in people over 50, even though the findings themselves become more common?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Because a finding can only discriminate between groups if it is present in one and absent in the other. Over 50, degeneration and bulges approach universal prevalence in both the pain and pain-free groups, so the odds ratio collapses toward 1 even though the absolute prevalence is at its highest. Why it matters: the same scan word carries different information at different ages, and a report should be read with the reader's age in mind.
