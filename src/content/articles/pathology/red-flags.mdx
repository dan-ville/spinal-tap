<Lede>Eight symptoms end self-directed training and go to a clinician the same day. They are rare, they are not a diagnosis, and the rule exists because the small number of serious causes of back pain cannot be told apart from ordinary back pain by how it feels.</Lede>

<Callout type="rose" title="The rule, in one sentence">
Any of the symptoms below ends self-direction. Stop following the [plan](/plan/), do not reason about it from this site, and see a clinician the same day. Treat saddle numbness, bladder or bowel change, or weakness that is getting worse as an emergency.
</Callout>

## Why a short list exists

The great majority of low back pain, including the kind that stops a lifter mid-block, is not caused by anything dangerous. The Lancet series puts specific causes such as fracture, infection, cancer and inflammatory disease at a small minority of presentations, and guidelines are built around that fact <Source id="lancet-2018-lbp-series" />. But those causes exist, and the problem with them is that early on they can feel like any other back pain. Pain intensity does not separate them. Location does not separate them. What does is a small set of accompanying features, and clinicians screen for them at every first visit <Source id="nice-ng59" />.

A "red flag" is one of those features. It is not a diagnosis. It is a reason to stop self-managing and hand the question to someone who can examine you, order tests, and act. The list below is the one this site keeps everywhere, and it matches the [plan](/plan/).

## The list

<Callout type="rose" title="Same-day clinician, every one of these">

1. **Symptoms below the knee.** Pain, tingling or numbness that travels past the knee suggests a nerve root is involved rather than the back alone. See the [radiculopathy article](/learn/pathology/radiculopathy/) for why that changes the decision rules.
2. **Numbness or weakness.** Loss of sensation anywhere in the leg or foot, or a muscle that will not do its job: foot drop, a calf that cannot push you onto your toes, a knee that gives. Weakness that is getting worse is an emergency.
3. **Saddle anaesthesia.** Numbness or altered sensation in the area that would touch a saddle: inner thighs, buttocks, perineum and genitals. Emergency.
4. **Bladder or bowel change.** New difficulty starting or stopping urination, retention, incontinence, loss of the sensation of needing to go, or loss of control of the bowel. Emergency.
5. **Night pain.** Pain that wakes you, or that does not ease at all when you lie down and unload the spine.
6. **Fever.** Back pain together with a temperature, chills or feeling systemically unwell.
7. **Unexplained weight loss.** Losing weight without trying to, alongside the back pain.
8. **Trauma.** Back pain that started with a fall, a crash, a heavy impact or a lift that went badly wrong, especially in anyone whose bones may be weaker than average.

</Callout>

Each of these is on the list for a reason a clinician recognises. The first four point to the nerve roots, and in combination the saddle, bladder and bowel items are the classic picture of cauda equina compression, which is one of the few true surgical emergencies in spine care <Source id="statpearls" />. Night pain, fever and weight loss are the features that make a clinician think about infection, inflammatory disease or a tumour rather than a mechanical problem. Trauma raises the question of a fracture, and the threshold is lower in anyone older, on long-term steroids, or with known low bone density.

This article will not go further into what each one might mean, because that is exactly the reasoning the rule exists to take out of your hands. The same symptom has benign and serious explanations, and sorting them needs an examination.

## What "same day" means

Same day means today, not after the next session, not after a week of seeing whether it settles. For most of the list that means a general practitioner, urgent care or a physiotherapist who can escalate. For saddle numbness, bladder or bowel change, or weakness that is progressing, it means an emergency department, because the window in which cauda equina compression can be decompressed with a good outcome is measured in hours <Source id="statpearls" />.

Two things people get wrong:

- **Waiting because the pain is improving.** As the [radiculopathy article](/learn/pathology/radiculopathy/) explains, pain can ease while a nerve root is still losing function. Improving pain does not cancel a red flag.
- **Training "carefully" in the meantime.** There is no careful version. Self-direction has ended; the plan on this site is not written for anyone with one of these symptoms, and no article here can tell you what is safe.

## What this list is not

It is not a list of things that will happen to you. Most lifters will go through a whole career without a single one. It is not a list of reasons to fear back pain: ordinary back pain without any of these features is, by the guidelines' own framing, something to stay active through <Source id="nice-ng59" />. And it is not a substitute for a clinician's screening, which asks more questions than these and weighs them against your history. It is the minimum you should know by heart so that, if one of these turns up, you do not spend a week reading about it.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Eight red flags.** Symptoms below the knee, numbness or weakness, saddle anaesthesia, bladder or bowel change, night pain, fever, unexplained weight loss, trauma.
2. **Each one ends self-direction.** Stop the plan and see a clinician the same day; this site cannot sort it for you.
3. **Four are emergencies.** Saddle numbness, bladder or bowel change, and progressive weakness go to an emergency department, not a waiting list.
4. **A red flag is not a diagnosis.** It is a reason to hand the question over; the same symptom can have benign or serious causes.
5. **Improving pain does not cancel it.** Nerve function can decline while pain gets better.

</KeyList>

A strongman competitor has had moderate back pain for a week that is slowly improving. Last night they noticed they could not tell when their bladder was full and had to strain to start urinating. They feel the back is "nearly fine" and want to do a light session. What should they do and why?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Go to an emergency department today. New bladder change is a red flag, and a change in bladder sensation with a recent back problem is part of the cauda equina picture, which is time-critical. Improving back pain does not cancel it. There is no light session. Why it matters: the serious causes are separated from ordinary back pain by accompanying features, not by how bad the back feels.

A lifter argues that the red-flag list is "fear-mongering" because serious causes of back pain are rare. What is the honest reply?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Serious causes are rare, and that is why the list is short and why ordinary back pain without these features is treated by staying active. The list exists because those rare causes feel like ordinary back pain early on and are only told apart by the accompanying features, so knowing them is what lets everything else on the site be self-directed. Why it matters: a short, well-known screen is what makes confident self-management reasonable rather than reckless.
