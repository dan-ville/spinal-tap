<Lede>Guidelines in the UK, the US and from the WHO all recommend against routine imaging for low back pain, because the findings are common in people without pain and rarely change what is done. Imaging is warranted when a red flag is present, when a nerve root deficit is being assessed, or when a procedure is being planned that depends on it.</Lede>

<Callout type="rose" title="Imaging is a clinician's decision">
This article explains why the guidelines say what they say. It does not tell you whether you need a scan; that depends on an examination. If you have any of the [red flags](/learn/pathology/red-flags/), the question is already answered: see a clinician the same day, as the [plan](/plan/) sets out, and let them decide what to order.
</Callout>

## What the guidelines say

Three independent guideline groups reach the same position.

<Evidence id="nice-ng59" />

NICE tells clinicians in non-specialist settings not to offer imaging routinely for low back pain with or without sciatica, and to image only where the result is likely to change management <Source id="nice-ng59" />. The JOSPT clinical practice guideline, written for physical therapists in the United States, is built on the same premise and emphasises classification by examination rather than by scan <Source id="jospt-2021-lbp-cpg" />. The WHO guideline for chronic primary low back pain defines its scope as pain without an identified specific cause, which presumes that red-flag screening, not imaging, is how that exclusion is made in primary care <Source id="who-2023-clbp" />. The Lancet series describes routine imaging as one of the most common examples of low-value care in back pain worldwide <Source id="lancet-2018-lbp-series" />.

## Why routine imaging does not help

The argument has two halves.

**The findings are common in people with no pain.** The systematic review of asymptomatic populations pooled in the [degeneration article](/learn/pathology/degeneration-and-asymptomatic-findings/) found disc degeneration in about a third of pain-free twenty-year-olds and almost all pain-free eighty-year-olds, with bulges and protrusions following similar curves <Source id="brinjikji-2015-degeneration" />. A scan of someone with back pain will very often show something, and most of what it shows was there before the pain and will be there after it.

**The findings rarely change what is done.** For back pain without red flags or a nerve root deficit, management is the same whether or not the disc is bulging: stay active, structured exercise, education, time <Source id="nice-ng59" />. A scan that does not change the plan has a cost without a benefit. The cost is not only money and radiation in the case of X-ray or CT. There is reasonable evidence, summarised in the Lancet series, that early imaging is associated with worse outcomes, more procedures and longer disability, plausibly because an alarming report changes how people behave <Source id="lancet-2018-lbp-series" />. The pain-science track covers that mechanism.

## When imaging is warranted

Guidelines do not say "never". They describe situations where the result would change management, and these are the ones a clinician is weighing:

- **A red flag is present.** Suspected fracture, infection, cancer, inflammatory disease or cauda equina compression. Here imaging is diagnostic, often urgent, and the choice of modality is specific to the suspicion. This is the main reason the [red-flag list](/learn/pathology/red-flags/) routes you to a clinician rather than to a scan you book yourself.
- **A nerve root deficit is being assessed.** Where there is weakness or progressive neurological change, imaging shows which level and what is compressing it, which matters because a decision about decompression may follow. Leg pain alone, with normal strength and reflexes, usually does not meet this bar early on; the [radiculopathy article](/learn/pathology/radiculopathy/) explains why most of those cases are watched first.
- **A procedure is being planned.** An injection or an operation needs a target, so imaging precedes it. This is the same logic in reverse: when a procedure is on the table, the scan changes what is done.
- **Pain is not following the expected course.** NICE's wording is to consider imaging in specialist settings when the result is likely to change management, which in practice includes pain that has not improved over a reasonable period of guideline care. The scan here is a clinician's judgement call, not a default.

What is not on the list: wanting to know what is wrong, pain being severe, having had pain before, or lifting heavy. None of these changes the management on their own, and so none of them is, by itself, a guideline reason to image.

## Reading a report you already have

Many lifters arrive at this site with a scan in hand. Two pieces of advice.

First, the words on the report are defined terms with specific meanings, and the common ones are far less alarming than they sound. The [terminology article](/learn/pathology/bulge-protrusion-extrusion/) explains what a bulge, a protrusion, an extrusion and degeneration mean in the radiologists' nomenclature and how common each is in people without pain. Read that before reading your report a second time.

Second, a report describes an image, not you. It cannot see pain, and it was written by someone who had not examined you. Whether a finding matters is the clinician's question to answer by putting the image next to the examination. This site does not interpret reports, and nothing here should be read as saying whether a particular finding is or is not the cause of a particular pain.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Three guidelines, one position.** NICE, JOSPT and the WHO all treat routine imaging for low back pain as low-value care.
2. **Findings are common without pain.** Degeneration, bulges and protrusions appear in a large share of pain-free people at every age.
3. **Imaging is warranted when it changes management.** Red flags, a nerve root deficit under assessment, a planned procedure, or pain off its expected course.
4. **Early imaging can do harm.** It is associated with more procedures and longer disability, plausibly through how an alarming report is read.
5. **A report needs a clinician.** Learn what the words mean, but leave whether they matter to the person who examined you.

</KeyList>

A powerlifter with two weeks of back pain, no leg symptoms, normal strength and none of the red flags asks their doctor for an MRI "to see what's going on". What position do the guidelines take and what are the two reasons behind it?
<Fragment slot="answer">
NICE, JOSPT and the WHO all recommend against routine imaging in this situation. The reasons are that the findings a scan would show are common in people without pain, so they are unlikely to explain anything, and that the result would not change management, which is to stay active and exercise either way, while early imaging is associated with worse outcomes. Why it matters: a scan that does not change the plan has only costs, and one of those costs is how its report changes behaviour.

<Check id="when-imaging-is-warranted-2" kind="apply">
Name three situations in which the guidelines would support imaging, and say what each has in common.
<Fragment slot="answer">
A red flag such as suspected fracture, infection or cauda equina compression; a nerve root deficit, especially weakness, being assessed for possible decompression; and a planned injection or operation that needs a target. In each case the result would change what is done. Why it matters: the test for imaging is not how much someone wants to know but whether the answer would alter the decision.
</Fragment>
