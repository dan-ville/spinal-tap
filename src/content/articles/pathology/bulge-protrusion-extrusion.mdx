<Lede>Radiology reports use four words for disc material that has moved beyond its normal boundary. They describe shape and continuity, not pain, and three of them are common in people who have never had back pain.</Lede>

<Callout type="rose" title="Before you read a report">
This article teaches vocabulary. It cannot tell you what your scan means or whether a finding explains a symptom; only the clinician who examined you can do that. If you have any of the red flags listed at the top of the [plan](/plan/), stop self-directed work and see a clinician the same day.
</Callout>

## Why the words matter

The <Structure id="FMA13894">intervertebral disc</Structure> is a ring of <Structure>annulus fibrosus</Structure> around a gel-like <Structure>nucleus pulposus</Structure>, bounded above and below by the <Structure>vertebral endplate</Structure>. A disc is "normal" when all of its material sits inside the ring traced by the edges of the vertebral bodies. The four terms below describe, one at a time, how far outside that ring disc material has gone and whether it is still attached. Radiologists use a shared nomenclature so that the same picture gets the same word in any hospital <Source id="radiopaedia" />. Learn each one on its own before you try to tell them apart.

## Bulge

A **bulge** is a generalised extension of the disc beyond the vertebral edge, involving more than a quarter of the disc's circumference. Picture a tyre that is slightly under-inflated: the whole wall sits a little proud of the rim. Nothing has pushed through anything. By definition a bulge is not a herniation, and because it reflects the disc losing height and spreading sideways it is strongly associated with age <Source id="radiopaedia" />. In the systematic review of asymptomatic people, disc bulge was present in about 30% of pain-free 20-year-olds and about 84% of pain-free 80-year-olds <Source id="brinjikji-2015-degeneration" />.

## Protrusion

A **protrusion** is a focal herniation: disc material extends beyond the edge over less than a quarter of the circumference, and the piece that is outside has a *base* (where it meets the parent disc) that is wider than any part of it that lies beyond the edge. The shape is a broad-based bump rather than a mushroom. The outer annulus is usually stretched but still contains the material <Source id="radiopaedia" />. Protrusions are also common without pain: about 29% of asymptomatic 20-year-olds and 43% of asymptomatic 80-year-olds <Source id="brinjikji-2015-degeneration" />.

## Extrusion

An **extrusion** is a herniation in which the material outside the disc is *wider than its base* in at least one plane, or extends above or below the level of the disc space. The nucleus has pushed through a tear in the annulus and the piece beyond the edge has a neck narrower than its head: the mushroom shape. The extruded material is still connected to the parent disc. Because extrusion requires an annular tear, it is more often associated with a single loading event or a period of repeated flexion-compression loading than a bulge is <Source id="adams-dolan" />.

## Sequestration

A **sequestration** is an extrusion whose displaced fragment has *lost continuity* with the parent disc. It is a free fragment, and it can migrate up or down the spinal canal. Sequestration is the least common of the four, and because the fragment is now foreign to the tissue around it, it is also the type most likely to be cleared by the body's own inflammatory response over the following months; that resorption process is the subject of the next article <Source id="radiopaedia" />.

## Telling them apart

Once each term stands on its own, the discrimination is two questions asked in order:

1. **How much of the circumference is involved?** More than a quarter is a bulge. Less than a quarter is a herniation, so go to question 2.
2. **Is the outside piece wider than its base, or has it broken off?** Base wider than the outside piece: protrusion. Outside piece wider than its base, or extending above or below the disc level: extrusion. No longer connected: sequestration.

Reports sometimes add a direction (central, paracentral, foraminal) and a size. Those words tell you where the material sits in relation to the nerve roots; they are also not a statement about pain.

## What the words do not say

None of these four terms is a diagnosis of pain. The asymptomatic-prevalence review shows bulges and protrusions in a large share of people who have never had back pain <Source id="brinjikji-2015-degeneration" />. The companion meta-analysis shows that, in adults under 50, bulge, protrusion and extrusion are each more prevalent in people *with* back pain than in matched controls <Source id="brinjikji-2015-meta" />. Both facts are true at the same time: a finding can be common in pain-free people and still be more common in people with pain. How to hold those two together is the subject of the companion article on [degeneration and asymptomatic findings](/learn/pathology/degeneration-and-asymptomatic-findings/).

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Four words, one axis.** Bulge, protrusion, extrusion and sequestration describe how far disc material has moved beyond the vertebral edge and whether it is still attached.
2. **Bulge is not a herniation.** More than a quarter of the circumference, generalised, strongly age-related.
3. **Protrusion vs extrusion is a shape test.** Base wider than the outside piece: protrusion. Outside piece wider than its base: extrusion.
4. **Sequestration is a free fragment.** Extruded material that has lost continuity with the disc.
5. **Shape, not pain.** The same words appear on the scans of pain-free people; the companion article covers what prevalence does and does not tell you.

</KeyList>

<Check id="bulge-protrusion-extrusion-1" kind="apply">
A report describes "focal disc material at L4/5 extending beyond the vertebral margin, the displaced portion wider than its base, reaching below the level of the disc space". Which term is this, and which term is ruled out by the phrase "wider than its base"?
<Fragment slot="answer">
This is an extrusion: focal (so not a bulge), and the outside piece is wider than its base and extends below the disc level. "Wider than its base" rules out protrusion, whose defining feature is a base wider than anything beyond the edge. Why it matters: the protrusion/extrusion distinction is the one most often blurred in casual use, and it is the extrusion group that most often shows spontaneous resorption on follow-up imaging.
</Fragment>
</Check>

<Check id="bulge-protrusion-extrusion-2" kind="apply">
Two scans: one shows the disc edge sitting slightly beyond the vertebral margin all the way around; the other shows a small broad-based bump over a short stretch of the posterior edge, still contained by the annulus. Name each, and say which single question separates them.
<Fragment slot="answer">
The first is a bulge (generalised, more than a quarter of the circumference). The second is a protrusion (focal, base wider than the piece outside). The separating question is how much of the circumference is involved: more than a quarter is a bulge, less is a herniation. Why it matters: a bulge is not a herniation at all, and conflating the two inflates what a report appears to say.
</Fragment>
</Check>

<Check id="bulge-protrusion-extrusion-3" kind="concept">
Why does this article say that none of the four terms is a statement about pain, when one of its own sources reports that bulges are more prevalent in people with back pain?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Because the other source reports that bulges are present in roughly a third of pain-free 20-year-olds and most pain-free 80-year-olds. A finding can be more common in people with pain and still be common in people without it; the term describes shape, and the prevalence data shift probability rather than deliver a verdict. Why it matters: reading only one of the two Brinjikji reviews produces an over-confident conclusion in either direction.
