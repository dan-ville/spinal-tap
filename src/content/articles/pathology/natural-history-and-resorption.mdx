<Lede>Followed with repeat imaging, roughly two thirds to three quarters of lumbar disc herniations get smaller or disappear without surgery, most within about six months. The biggest, messiest-looking ones resorb most often. Symptoms usually improve before the picture does.</Lede>

<Callout type="rose" title="Patience has limits">
"Most herniations resorb" is a statement about populations and about time. It is not a reason to wait through progressive weakness, numbness in the saddle area, or any change in bladder or bowel control. Those are listed at the top of the [plan](/plan/) as same-day red flags, and nothing in this article overrides them.
</Callout>

## What "natural history" means

The natural history of a condition is what happens to it when nobody intervenes. For a lumbar disc herniation that question can be answered directly, because people who are managed without surgery sometimes get a second scan months later, and the two pictures can be compared. A herniation is said to have **regressed** or **resorbed** when the displaced material is smaller or gone on the follow-up image <Source id="radiopaedia" />.

The previous article defined the shapes a herniation can take: [protrusion, extrusion and sequestration](/learn/pathology/bulge-protrusion-extrusion/). Keep those in mind, because the type of herniation turns out to predict how likely it is to shrink.

## How often it happens

Two pooled estimates bracket the answer.

<Evidence id="zhong-2017-resorption" />

The 2017 meta-analysis pooled eleven small cohorts and found that about 67% of herniations managed without surgery had resorbed by follow-up, with a wide confidence interval because the cohorts were small and defined resorption in different ways <Source id="zhong-2017-resorption" />.

A 2025 systematic review updated the question with more and larger studies and arrived at a higher figure: about 77% showed regression on repeat imaging, most of it within roughly six months <Source id="resorption-2025-review" />. The two numbers are not in conflict. They come from different study pools and different definitions, and the honest reading is a range: **somewhere between two thirds and three quarters** of herniations visibly shrink without surgery, on a timescale of months rather than weeks.

## Which herniations shrink most

Both reviews agree on a pattern that surprises most people: the herniations that look worst on the scan are the ones most likely to disappear <Source id="zhong-2017-resorption" /> <Source id="resorption-2025-review" />.

| Type | Where the material is | Tendency to resorb |
|---|---|---|
| Contained protrusion | Still inside the stretched outer <Structure>annulus fibrosus</Structure> | Lowest |
| Extrusion | Through an annular tear, still attached | Higher |
| Sequestration | A free fragment in the canal | Highest |

The explanation is biological. <Structure>Nucleus pulposus</Structure> that stays inside the annulus is walled off from the bloodstream, as the anatomy track's article on [disc nutrition](/learn/anatomy/disc-nutrition/) describes. Material that has broken through into the epidural space is exposed to blood vessels and immune cells for the first time. The body treats it as foreign, grows new vessels into it, and clears it by inflammation and phagocytosis. A fragment that has fully separated is the most exposed of all, and so the most readily cleared. Water loss from the displaced material adds to the shrinkage. So a report that reads "large extrusion with a sequestered fragment" is describing, among other things, the type most likely to be gone on a scan a year from now.

## The picture lags the person

Resorption on imaging is not the same thing as recovery, and the two are only loosely coupled <Source id="resorption-2025-review" />. People commonly report large improvements in leg pain weeks before any change is visible on a repeat scan, and some people whose scans never change feel fine. The reverse also happens: a herniation that has visibly shrunk in someone whose symptoms have not resolved. The displaced material is one input to the symptom, alongside inflammation around the nerve root, how sensitised the nervous system has become, and what the person has been doing in the meantime.

Two consequences follow for a lifter:

1. **Progress is judged by symptoms and function, not by re-scanning.** A follow-up scan to "see if it has gone" tells you about the picture, and the picture lags.
2. **A baseline scan that looks bad is not a sentence.** It is a snapshot of a process that, more often than not, is already heading toward resolution.

## What this does not tell you

The reviews describe herniations that were managed without surgery and happened to be re-imaged. They cannot tell you whether your herniation will resorb, and they say nothing about what to lift while you wait. Nor are they an argument against surgery when it is indicated; the companion article on [radiculopathy](/learn/pathology/radiculopathy/) covers what happens when a herniation presses on a nerve root and how conservative and operative outcomes compare. Finally, remember from the previous article that the same words appear on the scans of people who have never had pain <Source id="brinjikji-2015-degeneration" />, so a herniation that "resorbed" was not necessarily the cause of anything in the first place.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Most herniations shrink without surgery.** About 67% in the 2017 meta-analysis and about 77% in the 2025 review; read it as two thirds to three quarters.
2. **The timescale is months.** Most regression is visible within roughly six months.
3. **Worse-looking resorbs more.** Sequestrations and extrusions regress more often than contained protrusions, because exposed material is cleared by the immune system.
4. **The scan lags the symptoms.** Recovery often arrives before visible regression; judge progress by function, not by re-imaging.
5. **Population, not prophecy.** The figures support patience with conservative care; they do not predict any one person, and they never override a red flag.

</KeyList>
