If you have numbness, weakness, or pain that travels below the knee, you are outside what self-directed reading can sort out. See a clinician before you train through it. Treat numbness in the saddle area, weakness that is getting worse, or any change in bladder or bowel control as a same-day emergency, as the [plan](/plan/) sets out. ## Back pain and radiculopathy are different things

Low back pain is felt in the back. Radiculopathy is dysfunction of a single <Structure>lumbar nerve root</Structure>, and its symptoms are felt where that root goes: down the leg. The two often arrive together, but they are different problems with different natural histories and different decision rules, and a great deal of confusion comes from treating "I have a disc herniation" as if it meant one thing <Source id="statpearls" />.

The anatomy track's article on [nerve roots and dermatomes](/learn/anatomy/nerve-roots-and-dermatomes/) covers how each root leaves the spine and which strip of skin and which muscles it serves. Radiculopathy is what happens when one of those roots is irritated or compressed, most commonly by a posterolateral disc herniation at L4/5 or L5/S1 <Source id="statpearls" />.

## What it looks like

Because a root carries sensory, motor and reflex traffic for a defined territory, radiculopathy can produce any of three things in that territory:

- **Pain** that travels down the leg, often sharper and more "electric" than the back pain, frequently worse than the back pain, and often following the root's dermatome. Pain that reaches below the knee is the classic marker.
- **Altered sensation**: numbness, tingling or pins and needles in the dermatome.
- **Weakness** in the muscles the root supplies, and a reduced reflex where the root carries one. L4 affects the knee reflex and ankle dorsiflexion; L5 affects big-toe extension and foot eversion; S1 affects the ankle reflex and plantar flexion <Source id="statpearls" />.

Pain alone is the most common presentation and the most benign. Sensory change adds weight. **Weakness is the finding that changes the conversation**, because a root that is losing motor function is a root under meaningful pressure, and the clinical questions become how much, how fast, and whether it is getting worse. That is why this article's callout routes anyone with numbness or weakness to a clinician rather than to a loading plan.

"Sciatica" is the everyday word for pain along the <Structure>sciatic nerve</Structure>, which is formed from the L4 to S3 roots. It describes a symptom, not a cause; most sciatica is radiculopathy from a herniation, but not all of it.

## Why a herniation hurts a root

Two mechanisms, usually both at once <Source id="statpearls" />:

1. **Mechanical compression.** Displaced disc material narrows the space the root passes through, in the lateral recess or the foramen, and deforms the root.
2. **Chemical irritation.** <Structure>Nucleus pulposus</Structure> outside the annulus provokes a strong inflammatory response, and the inflamed root becomes sensitive to pressure it would otherwise tolerate.

The second mechanism explains two things that otherwise seem odd. Large herniations can be painless if they are not inflaming a root, and small ones can be agonising if they are. And it explains why symptoms often settle long before the material is gone: the inflammation resolves while the mechanical picture is unchanged, as the [natural history article](/learn/pathology/natural-history-and-resorption/) describes <Source id="zhong-2017-resorption" />.

## Conservative or operative

For most people with radiculopathy from a herniation the first question is not "surgery or not" but "is this safe to wait on". When there is no progressive weakness and no emergency sign, guidelines and surgeons alike generally start with conservative care, because the natural history is favourable and most people improve over weeks to months. Surgery, usually a microdiscectomy, is considered when leg pain is severe and persists despite that, or earlier when there is significant or worsening motor loss.

The best outcome data for people who load hard come from elite athletes.

<Evidence id="sedrak-2021" />

Pooling studies of elite athletes with symptomatic lumbar disc herniation, about 82% of those managed without surgery and about 83% of those who had surgery returned to play, with no statistically significant difference, and return took roughly four to five months in both groups <Source id="sedrak-2021" />. An earlier meta-analysis of athletes after microdiscectomy found return-to-play rates in the same low-to-mid 80% range, with career length and performance after return not consistently worse <Source id="overley-2016" />.

Read carefully, these two reviews support a narrow but useful conclusion: in athletes whose sport demands heavy loading, surgery is not a prerequisite for getting back, and choosing surgery when it is indicated does not cost the return either. They do **not** say surgery is unnecessary. The evidence is observational, and the athletes who had surgery were chosen for it by their clinicians, usually because they were doing worse. An athlete whose leg stayed weak or whose pain would not settle is in a different position from one whose symptoms are already easing, and the same data describe both.

## What this means for a lifter

- **Leg symptoms get a clinician, not a self-written plan.** The decision about waiting versus operating depends on an examination you cannot do on yourself, especially strength and reflex testing.
- **Weakness is the deciding variable.** Pain is expected and usually improves. New or worsening weakness is the finding that shortens the timeline.
- **The timescale is months.** Four to five months to return to play is the norm in elite settings with full support; expect something similar, not faster.
- **Returning is normal.** In both treatment arms, more than four in five athletes got back to a sport that loads the spine hard. A diagnosis of radiculopathy is not the end of heavy training.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Radiculopathy is a root problem.** Symptoms are in the leg, along one root's territory, and are distinct from back pain even when both are present.
2. **Three possible signs.** Radiating pain (below the knee is the marker), altered sensation, and weakness or lost reflex; weakness matters most.
3. **Compression plus chemistry.** Displaced nucleus compresses and inflames the root; inflammation explains why symptoms can settle before the material resorbs.
4. **Similar return rates either way.** About 82% of elite athletes returned to play without surgery and about 83% with it, no significant difference, in roughly four to five months.
5. **Numbness, weakness or below-the-knee pain means a clinician.** Saddle numbness, progressive weakness or bladder or bowel change is same-day.

</KeyList>

A judoka has sharp pain running from the buttock to the outside of the shin and the top of the foot, worse than their back pain, with tingling over the big toe but normal strength. Which root is most likely involved, is this radiculopathy or back pain, and what should they do before returning to the mat?
<Fragment slot="answer">
The distribution over the lateral shin and dorsum of the foot with big-toe tingling points to L5. It is radiculopathy: the symptoms are in a root's leg territory, not the back. Below-the-knee pain and altered sensation route them to a clinician for examination before training; normal strength is reassuring but is something to be tested, not assumed. Why it matters: leg-territory symptoms change the decision rules, and strength testing is the part a person cannot reliably do on themselves.

Loss of plantar flexion points to S1 motor loss.
