<Lede>Self-coached lifters rarely fail from ignorance of anatomy. They fail from seven recurring habits, each of which substitutes a feeling for a reading. This article names the seven, shows what each looks like in a log, and pairs each with the one counter-rule on this site that closes it. Every counter-rule keys on load, volume, range or preparedness. None keys on the name of a movement.</Lede>

## Why the rules key on four things

A counter-rule has to trigger on something that can be read from the [log](/learn/self-coaching/the-log/). Four things qualify: the **load** on the bar, the **volume** of hard sets, the **range** a movement is taken through, and the **preparedness** fields, sleep and stress. A fifth candidate, the movement's identity, is excluded on purpose. "Deadlifts are bad for me" is not a reading; it is a verdict about a name, and acting on it teaches the lifter that the hinge is dangerous while teaching the body nothing about hinging. Every failure mode below ends in a rule that changes one of the four and leaves the name alone.

## The seven

### 1. Progressing on feel

**What it looks like.** Week 3, Monday: the warm-ups fly, the lifter adds 10 kg instead of the planned 2.5. The log shows a load step with no rep condition met the week before, and the next morning's verdict is bad.

**Why it happens.** A good warm-up is a preparedness reading, not a progression condition. The two are stored in different columns for a reason.

**Counter-rule, keyed on load.** A load rises only when the [progression rule's](/learn/training-science/progression-models/) written condition was met in the log, and only by the written step. The spike guard then checks the new load against the trailing-30-day single-session maximum: one step above it is allowed, more is not <Source id="impellizzeri-2020-acwr" />. A good day changes nothing except, perhaps, the next day's verdict.

### 2. Adding volume mid-week

**What it looks like.** Thursday's session has an extra two sets of RDL "because Tuesday went well", and Friday's set count exceeds the budget by 25 percent. The log shows a week with 10 hard sets against a budget of 8.

**Why it happens.** The dose-response between sets and strength is real, and the lifter has read about it <Source id="schoenfeld-volume" />. What they have not read is that the response flattens and that the budget exists so that volume can be cut when verdicts demand it.

**Counter-rule, keyed on volume.** The weekly axial hard-set budget is set when the [block is written](/learn/self-coaching/writing-your-first-block/) and may rise only at the [block review](/learn/self-coaching/weekly-and-block-reviews/), by a pre-set step, after a block with no cut. Neither the tree nor the weekly review can raise it. A session that would exceed the budget stops at the budget.

### 3. Never climbing back

**What it looks like.** A bad verdict in week 2 dropped the RDL to mid-shin. It is week 9 and the RDL is still at mid-shin, with eleven clean verdicts behind it. The lifter has quietly decided the floor is "not for them".

**Why it happens.** The regression felt like safety and nothing forced a return. Fear of a range is self-sustaining when nothing in the plan schedules the climb.

**Counter-rule, keyed on range.** A regressed movement climbs one rung back after two clean verdicts following its own sessions, in reverse order: load, then moment arm, then range. The weekly review asks the question explicitly and the climb is not optional. The disc tolerates the full range when it is loaded gradually; it is the sudden exposure, not the range itself, that the ladder protects against <Source id="adams-dolan" />.

### 4. Training through bad verdicts

**What it looks like.** Wednesday's morning stiffness is 4 above baseline and does not settle, and Wednesday's session is logged as planned with no regression. Saturday reads the same. By the weekly review there are two bad days and no cut.

**Why it happens.** The verdict was read as "sore" and soreness as normal. The lifter has a programme to follow and a programme feels more real than a stiffness score.

**Counter-rule, keyed on range and volume.** The [daily tree](/learn/self-coaching/daily-decision-tree/) runs every morning before the session is confirmed. A bad verdict drops one rung on the movement that changed most recently; two bad days in one week cut that week's axial hard sets by 40 percent with intensity held. The session is written by the tree's output, not by the plan's page for that day.

### 5. Chasing the rating

**What it looks like.** The phase cap is RPE 7. The log shows top sets at 8, 8.5 and 9 across three weeks, each annotated "had more in me really". Verdicts drift from clean to yellow.

**Why it happens.** RPE is honest only if the lifter is, and the pull toward a grind is strong when the number on the bar is the thing being measured. The scale works best near failure and worst when the lifter has an incentive to underrate <Source id="helms-rpe-rir" />.

**Counter-rule, keyed on load and preparedness.** The phase's cap is a ceiling on every working set; a set that would exceed it is ended or the load is reduced for the next set. The cap is lowered, never raised, by the preparedness fields: short sleep or high stress drops the day's ceiling by a point. A top set above the cap is logged as an error against the lifter, not a win.

### 6. Blaming the movement

**What it looks like.** After one bad verdict the deadlift is removed from the block and replaced with a machine. The log shows a hinge that disappears in week 3 and a lifter who, by week 8, has not hinged under load for five weeks.

**Why it happens.** The most natural reading of "my back hurt after deadlifts" is "stop deadlifting". It is also the reading that converts one bad morning into a permanent loss of capacity.

**Counter-rule, keyed on range, then load.** A bad verdict changes the movement's range first, then its moment arm, then its load, and never its name. The hinge gets shorter, closer to the body, or lighter. The regression ladder was written per lift when the block was planned, so the rung is a lookup; if the lifter finds no rung left below the load rung, the block review asks whether the budget or the starting load was wrong, not whether the hinge was.

### 7. Running the block past its end

**What it looks like.** The block was written for five weeks. It is week 9. There has been no deload, no review, and the loads have been "about the same" for a month. The lifter is not injured and not progressing.

**Why it happens.** Nothing felt wrong enough to stop, and the deload looks like a week lost. Without a date, a block has no end, and without an end there is no review to notice the stall.

**Counter-rule, keyed on volume.** The deload is scheduled when the block is written, every four to six weeks, with volume down 40 to 60 percent and intensity held <Source id="bell-2023-deload-delphi" />. The block review follows it on a date, reads the stall from the log and changes one thing: the budget, the progression model, perhaps a switch to APRE where double progression has stalled <Source id="mann-2010-apre" />. The deload is where the block ends; it is not a reward for a good block or a punishment for a bad one.

## The seven in one table

| # | Failure | Reads as | Counter-rule keys on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Progressing on feel | Unplanned load step | Load: written condition, written step, 30-day guard |
| 2 | Adding volume mid-week | Sets over budget | Volume: budget moves only at block review |
| 3 | Never climbing back | Permanent regression | Range: one rung up after two clean verdicts |
| 4 | Training through bad verdicts | Bad days, no regression, no cut | Range and volume: tree runs daily, 40 percent cut |
| 5 | Chasing the rating | Top sets over the cap | Load and preparedness: cap is a ceiling, lowered by sleep and stress |
| 6 | Blaming the movement | A lift vanishes | Range then load: rung down, same name |
| 7 | Running the block past its end | No deload, no review | Volume: deload on a date, then the review |

Notice the column that is missing. No row says "swap the movement", because no reading in the log ever supports it. A lifter who finds themselves wanting to is in row 6, and row 6 has a rule.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Every failure substitutes a feeling for a reading.** The log holds the readings; the seven are what happens when the lifter stops consulting it.
2. **Counter-rules key on load, volume, range or preparedness.** Those are the four things a log can show and a rule can act on.
3. **Movement identity is never a key.** A bad verdict changes a lift's range, moment arm or load, in that order; it never removes the lift <Source id="adams-dolan" />.
4. **Load and volume move on written conditions only.** The progression step, the 30-day guard and the block-review budget are the three locks <Source id="impellizzeri-2020-acwr" /> <Source id="schoenfeld-volume" />.
5. **The RPE cap is a ceiling that only preparedness can lower.** Nothing inside a block raises it <Source id="helms-rpe-rir" />.
6. **A block ends on a date, with a deload and a review.** Volume down, intensity held, then the decisions <Source id="bell-2023-deload-delphi" />.

</KeyList>

<Check id="seven-ways-self-coached-lifters-fail-1" kind="apply">
A log shows: week 4 of a Phase 1 block, Tuesday RDL stepped from 70 kg to 80 kg with the previous week's sets at 10, 9, 8 in an 8-to-12 range; Wednesday verdict bad; Wednesday session logged as planned; Friday RDL replaced by a back-extension machine. Which failure modes are present, and what should each day have read instead?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Three modes. Tuesday is mode 1, progressing on feel: the condition for a step was all sets at 12, not met, and the step was 10 kg where the written step was 2.5 kg; Tuesday should have read 70 kg, 3 × 8 to 12. Wednesday is mode 4, training through a bad verdict: the tree should have dropped the RDL one rung, range to mid-shin, for Friday, and if Wednesday's session was the second bad day of the week, cut the week's sets by 40 percent. Friday is mode 6, blaming the movement: the machine is a change of identity; Friday should have been RDL to mid-shin at 70 kg. Why it matters: each wrong day is a feeling acted on, and each correction is a reading from a column the log already had.
</Fragment>
</Check>

<Check id="seven-ways-self-coached-lifters-fail-2" kind="apply">
A lifter's block was written for five weeks. In week 7 the loads have been flat for three weeks, verdicts are clean, and the lifter proposes adding two sets to each session. Which failure modes does the proposal commit, and what does the plan say to do?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Two. The lifter is already in mode 7, the block has run past its end with no deload and no review, and the proposal is mode 2, raising volume outside the block review. The plan says: deload now, volume down 40 to 60 percent with intensity held, then hold the block review on a date and read the stall from the log. The review may raise the budget by its pre-set step if the block had no cuts, or switch the progression model, but a two-set increase decided on a flat Thursday is neither. Why it matters: the stall is the review's to diagnose, and the review cannot run until the block has ended.
</Fragment>
</Check>

<Check id="seven-ways-self-coached-lifters-fail-3" kind="concept">
In your own words, explain why a counter-rule that keyed on movement identity, such as "if a lift produces two bad verdicts, remove it", would fail the lifter even though it would stop the bad verdicts.
<Fragment slot="answer">
It would stop the verdicts by removing the exposure, which is the one thing the plan is trying to build. Range, moment arm and load are dials: each can be turned down and later turned back up while the lifter keeps practising the pattern. Identity is a switch, and a switch thrown off does not come back on by any rule in the log; it comes back, if at all, by courage, which the plan refuses to depend on. The rule would also hide the real cause, which was almost always a load step, a volume step or a range step taken too soon, and those are the things the four keys can see. Why it matters: a rule that keys on the name treats the movement as the hazard, when the hazard was the dose.
</Fragment>
</Check>
