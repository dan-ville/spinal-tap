<Lede>Strongman implements are odd loads, high moment arms and sustained bracing, which is exactly what makes them valuable late in a return and risky early in one. The plan brings them in as regressed variants first, the suitcase carry before the yoke, the yoke walk before the turn, the stone to a high platform before a low one. This article gives the regression ladder for each implement, says how each counts against the axial budget and the spike guard, and tags every loading decision as the heuristic it is.</Lede>

## Why strongman work comes last

Three features separate a stone from a deadlift. The load is not where the lifter puts it; its position moves as the implement moves, so the [moment arm](/learn/biomechanics/torque-and-moment-arms/) changes mid-rep. The spine is loaded for a duration, not a rep: a yoke walk is thirty seconds of bracing under a load that may exceed any barbell lift. And the implement has no fixed path, so the lift cannot be regressed by changing a bar height the way a deadlift can.

Each of those is a reason the implements are useful once the back is ready: they train bracing endurance and lateral stability under load, which the endurance norms the plan uses as gates are a proxy for <Source id="mcgill-1999-endurance-norms" />. Each is also a reason they are not Phase 1 tools. The spinal loads in a conventional deadlift already run to thousands of newtons of compression <Source id="deadlift-loads-2022" />; a stone adds flexion to a comparable load, and repeated flexion under compression is the mechanism the porcine work tied to disc injury, a mechanism rather than a prescription but one that argues for earning the lift <Source id="callaghan-mcgill" />.

The plan's rule: **each implement enters as a regressed variant and earns its range, moment arm and load in that order** `[heuristic]`. That is the [regression rule](/plan/) run forwards.

## Loaded carries

The carry is the implement the plan reaches first, because it is in the Phase 1 exit gates: suitcase carry at half bodyweight per hand for 30 m. The ladder, lightest demand first:

1. **Suitcase carry.** One hand, load at the side. The spine resists lateral bending; the moment arm is small and fixed. This is the gate lift.
2. **Farmer's carry.** Both hands. Double the load for the same lateral demand; the challenge becomes grip and bracing duration.
3. **Front-rack or Zercher carry.** Load in front. The moment arm moves forward and the extensors do the work; this is where carries start to count as a hinge-like axial load.
4. **Yoke.** Load on the shoulders, above the spine, with a frame that sways. Highest compression of any carry and the only one where a stumble has consequences for the whole load.

Progress one rung at a time, and inside a rung progress distance first, then load. A new rung starts at the load of the rung below, not the load the lifter imagines they can handle.

## Yoke

The yoke has its own ladder, because the implement can be made easier in ways a barbell cannot:

1. **Yoke walk, short, straight.** 10 to 15 m, no turn, load equal to the lifter's front-squat working weight. The frame is set so the crossbar sits where a high-bar squat would.
2. **Yoke walk, full distance.** 20 to 30 m straight.
3. **Yoke and turn.** The turn is where the load sways and the spine takes a lateral and rotational moment it did not take walking straight. It is its own rung for that reason.
4. **Load.** Only after the turn is clean at the starting load does the load rise, in the plan's usual steps.

Each yoke run is one hard set against the axial budget if the effort is RPE 7 or above <Source id="helms-rpe-rir" />, and the heaviest run in any session is logged against the [trailing-30-day maximum](/learn/training-science/workload-spikes-and-acwr/) like any other axial lift.

## Sandbag and stones

The stone is the implement with the longest ladder, because the lap-and-load has a flexion phase under compression that nothing else in the plan asks for. The sandbag is the stone's regression:

1. **Sandbag to lap.** Pick the bag from the floor to the lap, seated or in a deep squat. The flexion is there but the load is light and conforming.
2. **Sandbag to shoulder or over bar.** The lap-to-extension phase is added. The bag's give makes the moment arm forgiving.
3. **Stone to a high platform.** A rigid load, so the moment arm is fixed and longer, but a high platform shortens the extension phase and lets the lifter stop the rep before full flexion-to-extension.
4. **Stone to a low platform, or over bar.** The full movement.

Within each rung: lighter first, fewer reps first. A stone session is counted as hard sets by rep clusters, and the plan treats a stone rep at RPE 7 or above as axial hard work in the same budget as a deadlift. The lifting manuals in the tier C list carry setup detail for each implement that this site does not reproduce <Source id="prs-lifting-manual" />; what the site adds is the order and the rule for moving between rungs.

## How the rules carry over

Nothing new is introduced for the implements. The RPE cap applies; a yoke run that would be a grind is a run not taken. The weekly axial budget counts carries, yoke and stones alongside the barbell lifts, so a Phase 4 week that adds a yoke day gives up sets elsewhere. The spike guard applies per implement, and the first session of any new rung is by definition a new 30-day maximum, which is why a new rung starts at the load of the rung below. The regression rule runs backwards down the same ladder: a bad verdict after yoke-and-turn is a return to the straight walk, not a lighter turn.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Odd load, moving moment arm, sustained brace.** Why strongman work is valuable late and risky early.
2. **Every implement enters as a regressed variant.** Suitcase before yoke, walk before turn, sandbag before stone, high platform before low. `[heuristic]`.
3. **Carries: suitcase, farmer's, front-rack, yoke.** Distance before load within a rung; a new rung starts at the rung below's load.
4. **The yoke turn is its own rung.** Sway adds lateral and rotational moment that walking straight does not.
5. **The stone's flexion phase is why it has the longest ladder.** Sandbag to lap, sandbag to shoulder, stone high, stone low.
6. **The budget, the spike guard, the cap and the regression rule all apply unchanged.** Implements are axial hard sets like any other.

</KeyList>

Carries: suitcase, farmer's, front-rack or Zercher, yoke. Stones: sandbag to lap, sandbag to shoulder or over bar, stone to a high platform, stone to a low platform or over bar. <Check id="strongman-loading-2" kind="apply">
A Phase 4 lifter wants to add yoke-and-turn at 180 kg, having never used a yoke. Their front-squat working weight is 110 kg and their axial budget is 12 hard sets, already fully used by squat and deadlift days. Write the session the plan allows and name the three rules that shaped it.
<Fragment slot="answer">
The session is a straight yoke walk of 10 to 15 m at about 110 kg, and only if the lifter gives up at least that many hard sets from the squat or deadlift day that week. The three rules: the implement ladder, which says walk before turn and start at the front-squat load; the axial budget, which is already full, so yoke sets must displace other sets rather than add to them; and the spike guard, which makes the first yoke session a new 30-day maximum and so argues for a conservative load. The 180 kg turn is a rung three steps away, earned one rung at a time. Why it matters: an implement that is new to the log gets no credit for strength shown elsewhere, because the moment arm and the brace are different.
</Fragment>
</Check>
