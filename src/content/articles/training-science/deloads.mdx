<Lede>A deload is a planned reduction in training stress so that accumulated fatigue clears before it becomes a stall or a flare. The plan's rule is every 4 to 6 weeks, volume cut 40 to 60 percent, intensity held. The rule's form matches an international expert consensus, its numbers are a heuristic, and this article explains why intensity is held, how a deload differs from a regression, and how to read the week after.</Lede>

## The rule, and what stands behind it

The plan's Phase 2 rule reads: **deload every 4 to 6 weeks; volume down 40 to 60 percent; intensity held** `[heuristic]`. Each clause has a different footing.

**The form of the rule** is consensus. A Delphi study of strength and physique coaches and scientists agreed that a deload is a planned, temporary reduction, that it is most often achieved by cutting volume while keeping intensity, and that it is placed at the end of a block or a mesocycle rather than used only when something goes wrong <Source id="bell-2023-deload-delphi" />. Consensus is expert opinion collected carefully; it is not a trial, and the site rates it low certainty.

**The numbers** are convention. There is no trial that found 40 to 60 percent better than 30 or 70, or 4 to 6 weeks better than 3 or 8. The window is where the volume-landmark model expects a lifter to reach maximum recoverable volume if they started near minimum effective volume and added a set or two per week, which is a model and not a measurement <Source id="volume-landmarks" />. The percentage is large enough that the week feels different and small enough that nothing is detrained.

## Why intensity is held

Three reasons, two from the training literature and one specific to this site's reader.

First, fatigue tracks volume more closely than load. The dose-response evidence is a volume relationship; it is the number of hard sets that climbs through a block, so it is the number of hard sets that should come down <Source id="schoenfeld-volume" />. Second, a week at lighter loads is a week in which the lifter's rating of a heavy set drifts: an RPE 8 at 80 percent after a week at 60 percent is briefly unfamiliar, and unfamiliar is where misjudged sets happen <Source id="helms-rpe-rir" />. Keeping the load keeps the calibration.

Third, and most important here: a lifter with a back history who drops the load for a week learns, without meaning to, that the load was the problem. It was not; the accumulated sets were. Holding the load at fewer sets is the deload saying so in a form the nervous system can hear. The [graded-exposure article](/learn/pain-science/graded-exposure/) covers why that distinction matters.

## Deload is not regression

These get confused because both mean "less", and confusing them breaks the log.

| | Deload | Regression |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | the calendar, every 4 to 6 weeks | a bad next-day verdict |
| What changes | volume, 40 to 60 percent fewer hard sets | one rung: range, then moment arm, then load |
| Intensity | held | may fall, as the third rung |
| Duration | one week | until the verdict clears |
| Logged as | planned | reactive, with the verdict that caused it |

A regression in week 5 of a block does not cancel the deload in week 6; the regression runs on the movement that earned it, and the deload runs on the week. If two bad verdicts land in one week, the plan's own rule cuts that week's axial hard sets by 40 percent, which looks like a deload and is logged as a regression so that the review can count it.

## What a deload week looks like

The lifter from the [periodization example](/learn/training-science/periodization/) finished realization on 5 axial hard sets at the cycle's top load. The deload is not relative to that week; it is relative to the block's typical week, which was about 10 to 12. A 50 percent cut is 5 to 6 axial hard sets, at the intensification loads, in the same rep zones.

| Day | Lift | Sets × reps | Load | RPE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | trap-bar deadlift | 2 × 5 | week 6 load | 6 to 7 |
| 1 | suitcase carry | 2 × 30 m | usual | 6 |
| 3 | front squat | 2 × 5 | week 6 load | 6 to 7 |
| 3 | Romanian deadlift | 2 × 6 | usual | 6 |

Four things are kept: the lifts, the loads, the rep zones, and the two non-consecutive axial days. One is cut: the number of hard sets, and the sets that remain are stopped a point or two below the cap so that none of them is hard in the sense the hard-set calculator counts. The week should feel easy. If it does not, that is a reading, not a failure of the deload.

## Reading the week after

The deload's purpose is visible in the first week of the next block: the same loads should move at a lower RPE than they did at the end of the last block, and the next-day verdicts should be green. Three outcomes and what each says:

- **Loads feel lighter, verdicts clean.** The deload did its job. Start the next block at MEV as planned.
- **Loads feel the same.** Either fatigue was not the problem or the cut was too small. Hold the new block's week 1 for a second week before climbing.
- **Loads feel heavier or verdicts are yellow.** This is not fatigue; it is a flare or something else. The regression rule applies, and the plan's red-flag list is read before the next session.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Every 4 to 6 weeks, volume down 40 to 60 percent, intensity held.** `[heuristic]`; the form is expert consensus <Source id="bell-2023-deload-delphi" />, the numbers are convention.
2. **Fatigue tracks the sets, so cut the sets.** Load stays because the dose-response evidence is a volume relationship.
3. **Holding the load teaches the right lesson.** A lighter week tells a back-history lifter the load was the problem; it was the accumulated volume.
4. **Deload is calendar; regression is verdict.** Different triggers, different changes, logged differently.
5. **Cut relative to the block's typical week, not its last week.** Realization is already low volume.
6. **The week after is the test.** Lighter and clean means it worked; heavier or yellow means it was not fatigue.

</KeyList>

State the plan's deload rule in full, and say which part of it is consensus and which part is convention.
<Fragment slot="answer">
Every 4 to 6 weeks, cut volume by 40 to 60 percent, hold intensity. The form, a planned volume cut with intensity held at the end of a block, is what the Delphi consensus agreed; the specific window and percentage are convention, tagged heuristic. Why it matters: a rule whose footing you know is one you can adjust honestly, and the numbers are the adjustable part.

<Check id="deloads-2" kind="apply">
A lifter's block averaged 12 axial hard sets a week and ended with a 6-set realization week. They plan a deload of 2 sets per lift at a load 20 percent lighter than usual. Name the two mistakes.
<Fragment slot="answer">
First, dropping the load: intensity is held during a deload, and lightening it both drifts RPE calibration and teaches the lifter that load was the problem. Second, the cut should be judged against the block's typical 12, not the 6-set realization week; 2 sets per lift across two days is probably about right in count, but the lifter should check it lands at 5 to 7 axial hard sets, a 40 to 60 percent cut from 12, rather than reasoning from the already-low last week. Why it matters: a deload done at the wrong load or against the wrong baseline either fails to clear fatigue or quietly becomes a regression.
</Fragment>
</Check>
