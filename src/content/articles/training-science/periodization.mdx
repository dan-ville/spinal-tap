<Lede>Periodization is the planned variation of volume and intensity across weeks. The pooled trials say a periodized plan beats a non-periodized one for maximal strength, with low certainty and short trials. This article explains linear and block periodization, maps the plan's accumulation, intensification and realization blocks onto the MEV-to-MRV climb, and shows where the deload and the hard-set budget sit inside the calendar.</Lede>

## What periodization is, and what the evidence says

A non-periodized programme sets the dials once and leaves them. A periodized programme changes them on a schedule that was written in advance. That is the whole definition; everything else is a particular schedule.

The meta-analysis that pooled the trials found that periodized resistance training produced larger gains in one-repetition maximum than non-periodized training <Source id="williams-2017-periodization-meta" />. Three caveats travel with that finding. The trials were short, mostly under four months. The populations were mixed, and in untrained people almost anything works. And volume was often not equated between arms, so part of the difference may be that the periodized arms did more. The honest summary is that planned variation probably helps, that the size of the help is uncertain, and that no trial has compared two schedules in lifters returning from a back episode. The site therefore treats the choice of schedule as a `[heuristic]`, and the evidence as a reason to plan rather than a reason to plan one particular way.

## Two families of schedule

**Linear periodization** moves one way: volume high and intensity low at the start, volume down and intensity up toward the end, usually over 8 to 16 weeks. It is the oldest scheme and the one most trials used. Its weakness for a self-coached lifter is that the intensity climb is a calendar, and the calendar does not know how the back is.

**Block periodization** cuts the same arc into short blocks with one emphasis each, which is the shape the [plan's Phase 3](/plan/) uses:

1. **Accumulation.** Volume climbs, intensity moderate. Lifts in the 6 to 12 rep zone, RPE capped at 8. This is where hard sets are added.
2. **Intensification.** Volume drops, load rises. Lifts in the 3 to 6 rep zone, same RPE cap. Hard sets fall but each is heavier.
3. **Realization.** Volume low, load at its highest for the cycle. A short block, one or two weeks, where the block's work is expressed as a top set or a test. Then a deload.

Each block runs 4 to 6 weeks except the last, and the deload at the end of the cycle is part of the schedule, not a response to trouble <Source id="bell-2023-deload-delphi" />. Block periodization suits the returning lifter better than linear for one reason: the blocks are short enough that a flare costs a block, not a cycle, and the regression rule can restart the current block rather than the whole arc.

## Mapping the blocks onto the landmarks

The [volume article](/learn/training-science/volume-intensity-frequency/) introduced the volume landmarks, minimum effective, maximum adaptive and maximum recoverable volume, and said each time that they are a model and not a measurement <Source id="volume-landmarks" />. Periodization is what turns the model into a calendar.

| Block | Where in the landmarks | Weekly hard sets (axial) | Intensity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accumulation week 1 | at MEV | low end of the budget | moderate, RPE ≤ 8 |
| Accumulation weeks 2 to 4 | climbing through MAV | +1 to 2 sets per week | held |
| Accumulation last week | at or just under MRV, capped by the budget | top of the budget | held |
| Intensification | back into MAV | fewer sets | rising, heavier loads, same RPE cap |
| Realization | near MEV | few | highest of the cycle |
| Deload | below MEV, on purpose | 40 to 60% fewer | held |

Two things in that table are decisions this site makes rather than things the model says. The budget caps the top of accumulation; the block runs up to the cap, not to wherever MRV might be. And intensity is held during the deload, which the next article explains. The dose-response evidence behind the climb is the weekly-set relationship, which is a relationship with hypertrophy over short trials and not a guarantee that every added set is useful <Source id="schoenfeld-volume" />.

## What the calendar does not override

The schedule is a plan for load. It is not a plan for the back. Three rules sit above it:

- **The RPE cap.** Every block runs under the same cap. Intensification raises the load by lowering the reps, not by raising the rating <Source id="helms-rpe-rir" />.
- **The regression rule.** A bad next-day verdict drops a rung on range, moment arm, then load. If that happens in week 3 of accumulation, week 3 is repeated a rung down; the block does not advance because the calendar says so.
- **The hard-set budget and the spike guard.** Accumulation's climb stops at the budget, and no single session exceeds the trailing-30-day maximum, which the [workload-spikes article](/learn/training-science/workload-spikes-and-acwr/) explains.

## A one-cycle example

A lifter entering Phase 3 with an axial budget of 12 hard sets per week.

| Week | Block | Hard sets | Main-lift zone | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | accumulation | 8 | 3 × 8 to 10 | start at MEV |
| 2 | accumulation | 10 | 3 × 8 to 10 | |
| 3 | accumulation | 12 | 4 × 8 | at the budget; the block stops climbing here |
| 4 | accumulation | 12 | 4 × 8 | held at the cap |
| 5 | intensification | 9 | 4 × 5 | load up, sets down |
| 6 | intensification | 9 | 4 × 4 | |
| 7 | intensification | 8 | 3 × 3 to 4 | |
| 8 | realization | 5 | 2 × 3 then one top set of 3 | |
| 9 | deload | 5 | 3 × 5 at the week 6 load | volume down 55%, intensity held |

Week 3 is the point to notice. The model would let volume keep climbing to wherever MRV is; the budget says 12, so the climb stops and the lifter holds. Nothing in the example was decided by how the back felt on the day; the verdicts are logged in a separate column and the regression rule reads them.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **Periodization is planned variation.** Change the dials on a schedule written in advance.
2. **Periodized beats non-periodized for 1RM in pooled short trials.** Low certainty; volume often not equated; nobody has tested the question in returning lifters.
3. **Block periodization suits a return.** Accumulation, intensification, realization, deload; short blocks mean a flare costs a block, not a cycle.
4. **The blocks are the landmark climb on a calendar.** Accumulation starts near MEV and climbs through MAV; the budget, not MRV, caps the top. Model, not measurement.
5. **Intensification raises load by lowering reps.** The RPE cap is the same in every block.
6. **The calendar never overrides the verdict.** Regression rule, RPE cap, budget and spike guard sit above the schedule.

</KeyList>

<Check id="periodization-1" kind="recall">
Name the three blocks of block periodization in order and say what each does to volume and load.
<Fragment slot="answer">
Accumulation: volume climbs, load moderate. Intensification: volume drops, load rises. Realization: volume low, load at its highest, briefly, then a deload. Why it matters: knowing which block you are in tells you which dial is meant to be moving, so a stall can be read against the plan instead of against mood.

<Check id="periodization-2" kind="apply">
A lifter has an axial hard-set budget of 10. Week 1 of accumulation is 6 sets, and they are adding 2 sets per week. In which week does the climb stop, and what should the sets be in week 5 if the plan says accumulation lasts five weeks? Use MEV, MAV and MRV in your answer.
<Fragment slot="answer">
Week 1 is 6 sets, near MEV. Week 2 is 8, in MAV. Week 3 is 10, which is the budget, so the climb stops there. Weeks 4 and 5 hold at 10. Wherever this lifter's MRV actually is, and the model cannot tell them, the budget is the cap, so week 5 is 10 sets, not 14. Why it matters: the landmarks give the block a shape, but for axial work the budget is the number that is actually enforced, and it is enforced from the log, not estimated from a model.
</Fragment>
</Check>

<Check id="periodization-3" kind="concept">
In your own words, explain why the site calls the choice of periodization scheme a heuristic even though a meta-analysis found periodized training superior.
<Fragment slot="answer">
The meta-analysis compared periodized with non-periodized training, not one scheme with another, and its trials were short, mixed in training status and often did not equate volume. It supports planning variation; it does not say which plan, and it says nothing about lifters with a back history. So the finding is the reason to have a schedule, while the particular block structure is a coaching convention chosen because it is short, readable and restartable. Why it matters: the strength of the claim should match the strength of the evidence, and here the strong claim is "plan it" and the weak one is "plan it this way".
</Fragment>
</Check>
