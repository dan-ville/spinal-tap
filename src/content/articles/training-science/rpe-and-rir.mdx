## The scale

Rating of perceived exertion in strength training is anchored to repetitions in reserve, RIR: the number of further reps that could have been done with good form before failure <Source id="helms-rpe-rir" />. The two run in opposite directions and add to ten.

| RPE | RIR | What the set felt like |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0 | Could not have done another rep |
| 9 | 1 | One more rep was there |
| 8 | 2 | Two more reps were there |
| 7 | 3 | Three more; bar speed clearly submaximal |
| 6 | 4 | Four or more; a warm-up or technique set |
| ≤ 5 | 5+ | Light work |

Half points are allowed. RPE 8.5 means one rep certainly, maybe two. Anything below about RPE 6 is rated loosely, because far from failure the estimate is not reliable and does not need to be.

The scale's job is to choose load on the day. A percentage-based programme prescribes 80% of a maximum that was tested weeks ago and may not be there today after a poor night or a flare. An RPE prescription says "work up to a set of five at RPE 8" and the load that satisfies it is whatever load it is today. That is autoregulation, and it is the main reason the plan uses RPE caps rather than percentages.

## What the scale is good at and where it breaks

The validation data say three things. Ratings correlate with average bar speed, which falls as a set approaches failure. Ratings correlate with the reps actually left when a set is taken to failure afterwards. And both correlations are stronger close to failure and in experienced lifters <Source id="helms-rpe-rir" />.

The breakages follow directly. **Far from failure, the rating is a guess.** A lifter rarely knows whether a set was RPE 5 or 6, and it does not matter. **Novices under-rate.** Untrained people stop a set believing they have two reps left when they have five or six, because the discomfort of a hard set is unfamiliar. The framework itself is expert opinion built on small studies. And the scale measures effort, not tissue tolerance: a set can be RPE 7 and still produce a bad next-day verdict, which is why the plan runs the verdict and the RPE cap as separate rules.

## The honesty problem

The scale only works if the rating is honest, and honesty is hard in two opposite directions. A lifter who wants to be progressing rates a grinding set RPE 8 because the plan says RPE 8. A lifter who is afraid of their back rates a smooth set RPE 9 because fear is the loudest signal in the room. Both are rating something other than reps in reserve.

Two calibration habits fix most of it. First, occasionally take a set to actual failure on a movement where failure is safe, a row or a leg press, after rating it the rep before. The gap between rating and reality is your correction. Second, film the top set and watch the bar speed on the last rep: a rep that slows markedly is at most RPE 9, and a rep that did not slow at all was not RPE 9 whatever it felt like. Trained lifters get accurate within a rep in a few weeks of this.

For the self-coached lifter with a back history there is a third rule. If a rating is driven by pain or fear rather than by muscular effort, log it as such: "stopped at 3, pain not effort". That set is a symptom data point for the [graded exposure](/learn/pain-science/graded-exposure/) process, not a volume data point for the block.

## The plan's RPE caps

The [plan](/plan/) uses RPE in two places. During Phase 1 and the early return, working sets are capped at RPE 7 to 8, which is two or three reps in reserve: hard enough to count as volume, far enough from failure that form holds and the next-day verdict stays readable. In Phase 2 the deadlift gate is a set of five at RPE 8 or lower with no yellow verdict, and the gate article explains the wording: "RPE 8" means two honestly rated reps in the tank, not a grind called RPE 8 <Source id="helms-rpe-rir" />.

These caps are heuristics, and the plan labels them so. What is not a heuristic is the threshold the hard-set count uses. A set at RPE 7 or above counts; below that it does not, because the volume literature's "set" is a set near failure <Source id="schoenfeld-volume" />. The cap and the threshold between them define the band the block lives in: RPE 7 to 8, every working axial set, until the log earns something harder.

## From a rated set to an estimated maximum

A rated set contains an estimated maximum. If a set of five at RPE 8 had two reps in reserve, it was in effect a set of seven, and the Epley estimate for a seven-rep set gives a one-rep maximum without ever testing one. The calculator does the adding and the formula. Enter the weight, the reps completed and the RPE.

Use the estimate for what it is good for, which is watching a trend across weeks at a stable RPE and seeing how far a working set sits from a load gate. Do not use it to plan a maximum attempt; the estimate inherits the rating's error, which is largest in exactly the lifters most likely to want to test it.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **RPE and RIR add to ten.** RPE 8 is two reps in reserve; RPE 10 is failure.
2. **The scale chooses load on the day.** It replaces a stale percentage with today's capacity, which is why the plan prescribes caps rather than percentages.
3. **It is accurate close to failure and in trained lifters.** Far from failure it is a guess; novices under-rate effort.
4. **It measures effort, not tolerance.** A clean RPE 7 set can still produce a bad next-day verdict; the two rules run separately.
5. **Calibrate it.** Occasional safe sets to failure and bar-speed on film close the gap within weeks. Log pain-limited stops as pain, not as RPE.
6. **A rated set gives an estimated maximum.** The calculator adds reps in reserve and applies Epley; use it for trends and gate distance, never to plan a max attempt.

</KeyList>

A lifter finishes a set of six and says they had three more reps. What RPE is that, and would the set count as a hard set for the plan's budget?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Three reps in reserve is RPE 7. It counts, because the hard-set threshold is RPE 7 or above; one more rep in reserve and it would not have. Why it matters: the threshold is the line between volume that is spent against the budget and practice that is not, so rating accurately to the rep decides what the week's count is.

<Check kind="concept">
In your own words, explain why the RPE scale is more trustworthy in a lifter with years of training than in a beginner, and what the beginner should do about it.
<Fragment slot="answer">
The validation data show ratings track bar speed and real reps in reserve best near failure and in experienced lifters, because experience teaches what the last few reps feel like; beginners have rarely felt them and stop early while believing they are close, so they under-rate. The beginner's fix is calibration: rate a set, then take it to failure on a safe movement and compare, and film the top set to check whether the bar actually slowed. Why it matters: a plan built on RPE caps is only as good as the rating, and a systematic under-rating means a block that looks like RPE 8 was done at RPE 6 and produced little.

<Check kind="apply">
A lifter in Phase 2 pulls 140 kg for five and logs "RPE 8". On the film, the fifth rep did not slow at all. A week later they pull 140 for five again, rated RPE 8, and the fifth rep visibly grinds. Which log entry is wrong, and in which direction should it be corrected before the e1RM trend is read?
<Fragment slot="answer">
The first entry is wrong. A rep that did not slow had more than two in reserve, so that set was RPE 7 or lower and the estimated maximum from it is too low. The second set, with a grinding last rep, was RPE 9 or more, so its rating is also wrong but in the other direction: it had at most one in reserve, the estimate from "RPE 8" over-states the maximum, and the week-on-week trend that looked flat actually fell. Why it matters: the estimate inherits the rating's error, and a trend built from two dishonest ratings can hide a drop in capacity that the next-day verdict is about to report.
</Fragment>
