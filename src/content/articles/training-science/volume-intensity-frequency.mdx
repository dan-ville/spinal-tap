<Lede>Every programme is a setting of three dials: how many hard sets, how heavy, and how often. The dose-response evidence says more weekly sets produce more muscle within the studied range, with low certainty and an undefined ceiling, and that frequency matters mainly as a way to distribute volume. The volume-landmark model (MEV, MAV, MRV) gives a vocabulary for setting the dials across a block; it is a model, not a measurement, and this article says so every time it uses it.</Lede>

## Three dials, one budget

Strip any programme down and it is three numbers per movement pattern per week. **Volume** is how many hard sets. **Intensity** is how heavy those sets are, expressed as a percentage of a maximum or, more usefully for a self-coached lifter, as proximity to failure. **Frequency** is how many sessions the volume is spread across. The three are not independent. Heavier sets cost more to recover from, so volume has to come down as intensity goes up; more sessions let the same volume be done fresher, which is most of what frequency is for.

For a lifter with a back history there is a fourth constraint that ordinary training science does not carry: the [axial hard-set budget](/plan/) that the plan tracks separately. The reason is spelled out in the biomechanics track. The spinal loads in a heavy deadlift are estimated at several times bodyweight in compression, and no study of repeated deadlifting exists, so accumulation has to be managed with the tools of this track rather than with a measured tissue budget <Source id="deadlift-loads-2022" />. Volume is the dial that budget constrains.

## What the volume evidence says

The best-known synthesis pooled fifteen studies and found a graded relationship between weekly sets per muscle group and muscle growth: each extra weekly set bought a small additional gain, and ten or more weekly sets produced roughly double the growth of fewer than five <Source id="schoenfeld-volume" />.

<Evidence id="schoenfeld-volume" />

Read the card's caveats before you read its effect. The participants were mostly untrained, the studies were short, and the outcome was muscle size, not strength and not pain. The upper limit of the dose-response was not found because nobody studied it. So the finding is a direction, more sets grow more muscle within a range of roughly four to twenty weekly sets, and not a target. Nothing in it says a strong lifter with a flare history should do twenty sets of hinges.

Frequency has its own meta-analysis from the same group, and its conclusion is the one that matters for planning: when weekly volume is held equal, splitting it across more sessions makes little difference to growth. Frequency is therefore a recovery and quality tool. Two sessions of five hard sets will generally be done with better bar speed and better form than one session of ten, which is the whole case for it. The source entry notes that pairing <Source id="schoenfeld-volume" /> with the frequency review is the right move when frequency is the claim.

Intensity, in the sense of proximity to failure, is covered in the [RPE and RIR article](/learn/training-science/rpe-and-rir/). The one point to carry here is that a set only counts toward volume if it is hard. The literature's "set" is a set taken close to failure. Five sets at RPE 5 are warm-ups, not volume, and the hard-set calculator below only counts sets at RPE 7 or above for that reason <Source id="helms-rpe-rir" />.

## Counting hard sets

The plan's budget is a weekly count of axial hard sets, so the count has to come from the same arithmetic every week. Enter the number of axial sessions, the axial sets per session and the RPE those sets are done at. The calculator applies the threshold and also shows what the week looks like after the plan's forty-percent cut, which is the regression rule for two bad next-day verdicts in one week.

## The volume landmarks: a model, not a measurement

The next vocabulary is a coaching model, and it is introduced here on its own, in one block, so the three terms are learnt together before they are mixed with anything else. Later articles will quiz them interleaved; this one does not.

The model proposes that for a given muscle or movement there are landmark weekly set counts <Source id="volume-landmarks" />:

- **MEV, minimum effective volume.** The fewest hard sets per week that still produce progress. Below it you maintain or lose; at it you gain slowly.
- **MAV, maximum adaptive volume.** The range in which the most progress happens for the recovery spent. Most of a block is meant to sit here.
- **MRV, maximum recoverable volume.** The most hard sets per week you can recover from. Above it fatigue accumulates faster than you adapt, performance stalls or drops, and a deload is due.

The model's use is as a shape for a block: start a block near MEV, add a set or two per week through MAV, reach MRV, deload, and repeat with the landmarks having moved slightly. That shape is what the [periodization article](/learn/training-science/periodization/) turns into a calendar.

<Evidence id="volume-landmarks" />

Say what the card says. The model is not a measurement. Nobody has a trial in which MEV or MRV was determined for a population and then used to set training. The numbers attached to the landmarks in coaching articles are estimates, and your own landmarks shift with sleep, stress, training age and, for the reader of this site, with the current state of your back. The landmarks are useful as a shared language for reading a log: "I am past MRV" is a precise way of saying that performance has dropped while effort has risen. They are not useful as targets that override the next-day verdict.

Two things the model does well for a back-history lifter. It makes the deload a planned event instead of an emergency. And it gives the hard-set budget a direction of travel: the budget is a cap on where MRV is allowed to be for axial work, and the block runs up to the cap rather than to the landmark.

## Setting the dials, in order

**Frequency first.** Pick the number of sessions you can actually do every week, then spread the volume across them. For axial work, two or three sessions is where most plans land, because it keeps each session's spinal loading modest and lets the next-day verdict be read per session.

**Intensity second.** Choose the proximity to failure the block is for. Early in a return, two to three reps in reserve is the ceiling, as the [graded exposure article](/learn/pain-science/graded-exposure/) sets out. The RPE scale gives that a number.

**Volume last, and counted.** Start near the low end, which the model calls MEV, and add sets week to week only while the log says recovery is keeping up. The budget caps the top. The calculator counts it. The next-day verdict, not the landmark, decides whether the next set is added.

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **A programme is three dials.** Hard sets, how heavy, how often. They trade against each other.
2. **More weekly sets grow more muscle, within a range, with low certainty.** Mostly untrained participants, short trials, size not strength or pain, no ceiling found.
3. **Frequency distributes volume.** At equal weekly sets, more sessions change little; they buy quality and per-session readability.
4. **Only hard sets count.** A set at RPE 7 or above is volume; below that it is practice. The calculator applies the threshold so the budget is counted the same way every week.
5. **MEV, MAV, MRV are a model, not a measurement.** Minimum effective, maximum adaptive, maximum recoverable. Use them to shape a block and read a log, never as targets that override symptoms.
6. **The axial hard-set budget caps MRV for spinal loading.** The block runs up to the cap, not to the landmark.

</KeyList>

<Check id="volume-intensity-frequency-2" kind="concept">
In your own words, explain why the volume dose-response finding is a direction and not a target for a lifter returning from a back episode.
<Fragment slot="answer">
The meta-analysis found more weekly sets grew more muscle, but its participants were mostly untrained, its trials were short, its outcome was muscle size rather than strength or symptoms, and it never found the upper limit, so the finding says "more is more within the studied range" and nothing about where any individual's ceiling sits. A returning lifter has an extra constraint the studies did not have, the spinal loading of each hard axial set, for which no cumulative measurement exists. Why it matters: the trend justifies adding sets while the log tolerates it, and the budget and verdict decide when to stop, which is the opposite of chasing a number.
</Fragment>

<Check id="volume-intensity-frequency-3" kind="apply">
A lifter plans three axial sessions a week, four sets each, at RPE 6 because they are early in a return. They log it as twelve weekly hard sets against the budget. What has gone wrong, and what should the calculator report?
<Fragment slot="answer">
Nothing has gone wrong with the training; what has gone wrong is the count. A hard set is one at RPE 7 or above, so twelve sets at RPE 6 are practice volume and the calculator reports zero hard sets against the budget. If the lifter wants those sets to count as a stimulus the intensity dial has to come up, and the budget is then spent accordingly. Why it matters: counting sub-threshold sets as hard inflates the budget, so the lifter either under-trains while believing they are at the cap, or cuts volume that was never load-bearing when a bad week arrives.
</Fragment>
</Check>
