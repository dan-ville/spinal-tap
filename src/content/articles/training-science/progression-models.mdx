<Lede>A progression model is a rule for what to do with the load next time, written before the session so the decision is not made under fatigue or fear. This article works two of them in full, double progression and autoregulatory progressive resistance exercise, then fades each into a second example with the decisions left blank for you to make. Both run inside the plan's RPE caps and the hard-set budget, and both leave the regression rule untouched.</Lede>

## Why a written rule

Left to the moment, progression is driven by mood. On a good day the load jumps; after a twinge it stalls for a month. A model replaces that with a rule that was decided in advance and that the log can check. For a self-coached lifter with a back history the rule has one extra job: it has to be compatible with the [regression rule](/plan/), which says a bad next-day verdict drops a rung on range, then moment arm, then load. A progression model moves the load up; the regression rule moves it down; neither overrides the other, and both are logged.

Two models cover most of what a returning lifter needs. Double progression is the simplest rule that works and is where this track starts. APRE adds autoregulation, letting today's performance set today's load, which is the same idea as the [RPE scale](/learn/training-science/rpe-and-rir/) made mechanical <Source id="helms-rpe-rir" />.

## Double progression

The rule has two parts, which is the name. Fix a rep range and a load. Progress reps within the range first; when every set reaches the top of the range, add load and drop to the bottom of the range. It is a convention, not a trial finding, and the site tags it `[heuristic]`; its justification is that it adds volume before intensity, which matches the direction of the dose-response evidence <Source id="schoenfeld-volume" />, and that it can be run entirely from the log.

### Worked example, complete

A lifter in Phase 1 is doing Romanian deadlifts for three sets in a 6-to-8 rep range at an RPE cap of 8. The rule: when all three sets reach 8 at RPE 8 or below, add 2.5 kg and return to 6.

| Week | Load | Sets × reps | Top-set RPE | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 60 kg | 6, 6, 6 | 7 | Below the top of the range; hold the load, try for more reps |
| 2 | 60 kg | 8, 7, 6 | 8 | First set reached 8, the others did not; hold the load |
| 3 | 60 kg | 8, 8, 8 | 8 | All sets at the top at or under the cap; add 2.5 kg next week, back to 6 |
| 4 | 62.5 kg | 6, 6, 6 | 7.5 | Range restarted at the bottom; hold the load |
| 5 | 62.5 kg | 8, 8, 7 | 8.5 | Top-set RPE over the cap; the third set did not reach 8; hold the load, and the rating says do not chase it |

Three things to notice. The load moved once in five weeks, which is normal and fine. The RPE cap did work in week 5: the lifter had the reps to try for 8 on the third set but the rating said the set would have been RPE 9, so the rule says wait. And the decision column is a mechanical reading of the row before it; nothing in it was a judgement about how the back felt, because that judgement belongs to the next-day verdict, which is logged in a different column and triggers a different rule.

## APRE: autoregulatory progressive resistance exercise

APRE makes the day's performance set the day's load. It was tested against a fixed linear plan over six weeks in trained college athletes and produced larger strength gains in that trial <Source id="mann-2010-apre" />. One short trial in one population is the whole evidence base, so the model carries low certainty; what recommends it to a self-coached lifter is that it is explicit, and that its third set is a built-in readiness test.

A session on APRE has four sets of the same lift. The protocol has three versions named for the target reps, APRE 3, 6 and 10; the plan's Phase 1 and 2 caps fit the 6 and 10 versions, so the 6 version is worked here.

1. **Set 1**: half the target reps at about half the working load, a warm-up.
2. **Set 2**: the target reps at about three-quarters of the working load.
3. **Set 3**: as many good reps as possible at the working load, stopping at the RPE cap rather than at failure. This is the test set.
4. **Set 4**: as many good reps as possible at a load adjusted by the table below, using the reps from set 3. The reps from set 4 set next session's working load by the same table.

The adjustment table for APRE 6, in kilograms, adapted from the published pound values:

| Reps on the test set | Adjustment |
|---|---|
| 0 to 2 | subtract 2.5 to 5 kg |
| 3 to 4 | subtract 0 to 2.5 kg |
| 5 to 7 | hold the load |
| 8 to 12 | add 2.5 to 5 kg |
| 13 or more | add 5 to 7.5 kg |

Two modifications for this site's reader. The published protocol takes sets 3 and 4 to failure; here they stop at the RPE cap, which is the plan's rule and takes priority, and the rep counts are therefore "reps to the cap" <Source id="helms-rpe-rir" />. And the adjustments at the bottom of the table are halved from the original, because a lifter in a return has less to gain from a large load swing than from a readable log.

### Worked example, complete

A lifter in Phase 2 runs APRE 6 on the trap-bar deadlift with a working load of 120 kg and an RPE cap of 8.

| Session | Set 3 (working load) | Reps to cap | Set 4 adjustment | Set 4 load | Reps to cap | Next session's working load |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 120 kg | 9 | 8 to 12: add 2.5 to 5; takes 5 | 125 kg | 6 | 5 to 7: hold; 125 kg |
| 2 | 125 kg | 6 | 5 to 7: hold | 125 kg | 5 | 5 to 7: hold; 125 kg |
| 3 | 125 kg | 4 | 3 to 4: subtract 0 to 2.5; takes 2.5 | 122.5 kg | 6 | 5 to 7: hold; 122.5 kg |

Session 3 is the one to study. The test set came in at four, two below the previous week at the same load. The table says take a little off for set 4, and set 4 then came back to six, which is what a flat day looks like rather than a lost week. The lifter's log also records the next-day verdict for session 3; if it was yellow, the regression rule runs on top of this and the next session starts a rung down on range or moment arm, with the APRE load adjustment still applied.

Fill in the faded Romanian-deadlift example. The lifter prefers the smaller of each adjustment range. Then say what session 3 tells the lifter beyond the load arithmetic.
<Fragment slot="answer">
Session 1: seven reps is "hold", so set 4 is 70 kg; four reps on set 4 is "subtract 0 to 2.5", the smaller choice is 0, so next session's working load is 70 kg. Session 2: working load 70 kg; eleven reps is "add 2.5 to 5", smaller choice 2.5, so set 4 is 72.5 kg; seven reps is "hold", next working load 72.5 kg. Session 3: working load 72.5 kg; two reps is "subtract 2.5 to 5", smaller choice 2.5, so set 4 is 70 kg; five reps is "hold", next working load 70 kg. Beyond the arithmetic, session 3's test set dropped from eleven at 70 kg to two at 72.5 kg, which is far larger than a 2.5 kg jump explains, so the drop is readiness, not strength: the lifter should check the log's sleep and verdict columns, log "stopped at 2, pain or effort?" honestly, and expect the next session to tell them whether it was a bad day or a flare. Why it matters: APRE's test set is a readiness gauge as well as a load selector, and a reading that cannot be explained by the load is the log asking a question.

## Which model, when

Double progression for Phase 1 and for any lift that is being re-learnt after a flare, because its steps are small, its load changes are rare and its decision needs no table. APRE for Phase 2 and onward on the main lifts, because its test set reads readiness every session and it moves load faster when the log earns it. Either model lives inside the RPE caps and the [hard-set budget](/learn/training-science/volume-intensity-frequency/), and either is paused, not abandoned, when the regression rule fires. The block-level shape they run inside, the climb from MEV toward MRV and the deload that follows, is a model rather than a measurement <Source id="volume-landmarks" /> and is the subject of the [periodization article](/learn/training-science/).

## Key points

<KeyList>

1. **A progression model is a rule written before the session.** It moves load up; the regression rule moves it down; both are logged and neither overrides the other.
2. **Double progression: reps first, then load.** Fix a range, fill it, add load, restart at the bottom. A convention, tagged heuristic, run entirely from the log.
3. **The RPE cap is part of both rules.** A set over the cap does not count toward the top of the range and does not count as reps to the cap.
4. **APRE lets the test set choose the load.** Four sets; the third is a readiness test; a table adjusts the fourth and the next session. One short trial in trained athletes supports it, low certainty.
5. **This site stops APRE sets at the cap, not at failure.** The plan's rule takes priority; the table is read in reps to the cap.
6. **A test-set drop the load cannot explain is a readiness signal.** Read the verdict and sleep columns before reading the table.

</KeyList>

<Check kind="concept">
In your own words, explain why both progression models are run from written rules rather than from how the lift feels on the day, given that APRE is explicitly about adjusting to the day.
<Fragment slot="answer">
APRE does respond to the day, but through a measured quantity, the reps achieved on the test set, read against a table decided in advance; the lifter does not choose the adjustment by feel. The reason is that feel is dominated by mood, fatigue and fear, which for a lifter with a back history pull in unpredictable directions: an anxious day under-loads, a good day over-jumps. A rule converts the day into a number and the number into a decision the log can audit, and leaves the back's own signal to the next-day verdict and the regression rule. Why it matters: separating "what the performance was" from "what I felt about it" is what makes a self-coached block checkable a month later.
</Fragment>
</Check>
