---
source: belavy-2017
claim: Long-term runners have intervertebral discs with greater hydration and glycosaminoglycan content and thicker discs than non-athletic controls, consistent with discs adapting to habitual loading.
design: Cross-sectional MRI study comparing lumbar discs in long-distance runners, joggers and non-sporting controls (79 participants), with accelerometry to characterise loading.
population: Healthy adults aged 25 to 35 without back pain, grouped by running history and weekly running volume.
effect: Runners showed higher T2-time (a hydration and glycosaminoglycan proxy) in the nucleus and greater disc height relative to vertebral height than controls. Accelerometry suggested the association was strongest for loading in the range of fast walking to slow running rather than higher impact activity.
certainty: low
topic: biomechanics
kind: observational
caveats: Cross-sectional design, so it cannot show that running caused the disc differences; people with better discs may select into running. Sample was young and pain-free, and findings concern running, not lifting. It is the best available human evidence that discs respond to loading, but it is a single observational study.
