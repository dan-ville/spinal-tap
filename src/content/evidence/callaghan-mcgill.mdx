---
source: callaghan-mcgill
claim: Highly repetitive flexion and extension under modest compression produced posterior disc herniation in a porcine spine model, while the same compression without motion did not.
design: In-vitro experiment on porcine cervical motion segments cycled through repeated flexion and extension under a range of constant compressive loads until failure or a cycle limit.
population: Porcine cervical spine specimens used as a surrogate for human lumbar segments; no living participants.
effect: Specimens cycled under compression in the mid range showed progressive annular tracking of nucleus material and posterior herniation after thousands of cycles. Higher compression without flexion cycling tended to fail the endplate instead, consistent with the cadaveric human work (adams-dolan).
certainty: low
topic: biomechanics
kind: lab
caveats: Animal tissue, in vitro, loaded for thousands of uninterrupted cycles with no recovery or remodelling, which is unlike any training week. Porcine cervical segments differ from human lumbar segments in size and geometry. The study shows a mechanism is possible, not that flexed lifting in humans causes herniation; frame it as one input into dose management rather than a rule about posture.
