# Chapter 9. Concurrent training and conditioning

*Light first draft.*

## Abstract

Most people who lift also want some aerobic fitness. This chapter asks whether endurance training
blunts gains in strength, muscle and power, what moderates the effect, how the molecular explanation
has changed, and how strength athletes can condition with the least cost. In short trials, mostly in
untrained people, adding endurance work does not measurably reduce muscle growth or average maximal
strength. Explosive strength and rate of force development are the most consistently blunted
qualities. Penalties appear in men, with same-session training and at high endurance volumes, and in
the one controlled trial of strength-qualified men, same-session running roughly halved squat gains.
Separating the two modes by at least six hours, or by a day, is the most effective mitigation. The
early molecular "switch" model has not held up in humans; fatigue and the quality of strength
training fit the pattern better. The strongest reason to condition is health: cardiorespiratory
fitness is one of the strongest predictors of survival.

## How to read the numbers in this chapter

Citations are evidence-record keys such as [@schumann-2022-concurrent]. **Unmarked numbers** were read
in the full text of the source. A **dagger (†)** marks a number read only in the abstract. Grades:
**established**, **promising**, **contested**, **speculative** and **convention** (practitioner
practice with no direct data). Intervals are 95% confidence intervals unless stated otherwise.
*Concurrent training* (CT) means resistance training (RT) combined with endurance training in the
same program. Negative effect sizes mean smaller gains with concurrent training.

The population caveat runs against reassurance. The literature is dominated by untrained or
"recreationally active" young men, 6–12-week interventions and lower-body tests. The largest
meta-analysis could not identify well-trained people because studies reported training status too
poorly [@schumann-2022-concurrent]; another found highly strength-trained athletes essentially absent
[@huiberts-2024-concurrent-sex]. The best data in trained men lean toward interference.

## 9.1 Where the idea came from

In 1980, untrained people who added very heavy endurance training to heavy leg training gained
strength in step with a strength-only group for about seven weeks, then plateaued and declined in
weeks 9–10† [@hickson-1980]. The dose was extreme: about 11 sessions a week, including intervals at
maximal aerobic power and daily runs of up to 40 minutes [@baar-2014-concurrent]. A 1990 study using
three moderate sessions a week found no interference† [@sale-1990-concurrent]. From the start, the
answer depended on the dose.

## 9.2 What concurrent training does and does not blunt

**Muscle size: not measurably blunted** (established for short trials in untrained and recreational
people). Across 15 imaging studies, concurrent and resistance-only training grew whole muscle equally
(SMD −0.01, −0.16 to 0.18), with no heterogeneity at all [@schumann-2022-concurrent]. A network
meta-analysis found no type of concurrent training differed from resistance training
[@chen-2024-concurrent-nma], and a 2026 umbrella review of 17 meta-analyses called hypertrophy
"comparable"† [@held-2026-concurrent-umbrella]. At the level of individual fibres there is a small,
borderline penalty (SMD −0.23, −0.46 to −0.00; p = 0.050), with a larger point estimate in type I
fibres, though type I and type II did not differ significantly [@lundberg-2022-fiber-concurrent]. In
strength-qualified men, muscle cross-section rose +17.0%† with resistance training and +8.8%† with
concurrent training, with no between-group test in the abstract [@gomes-2026-trained-morphology]. That
does not show interference, but it is not reassuring either.

**Maximal strength: no average penalty in mixed samples** (promising). Across 37 studies, lower-body
maximal strength gains did not differ (SMD −0.06, −0.20 to 0.09; I² 0%) [@schumann-2022-concurrent].
Two 2026 meta-analyses of interval-based concurrent training agree† [@kikuchi-2026-hiit-concurrent]
[@ferraro-farro-2026-sit]. The trials are small and of modest quality (mean PEDro 4.3 of 10), so "no
significant difference" is not equivalence.

**But the average hides subgroups** (contested):

- **Sex.** Men showed a lower-body strength penalty (SMD −0.43, −0.64 to −0.22); women did not (0.08,
  −0.34 to 0.49), though the female estimate rests on 59 participants [@huiberts-2024-concurrent-sex].
- **Training status.** One meta-analysis found a penalty only in "trained" participants (ES −0.35,
  −0.59 to −0.11) [@petre-2021-concurrent], but "trained" meant three months of any structured
  training, and 14 of its 15 trained interventions used running. Another, classifying by
  strength-training status, found no moderation, and the penalty reached significance only in
  *strength-untrained* participants [@huiberts-2024-concurrent-sex]. The direction is unresolved.
- **Strength-qualified men.** In the one resistance-controlled trial of men squatting at least 1.5 ×
  body mass, 30 minutes of running after lifting, three times a week for 11 weeks, roughly halved
  gains: squat 1RM +8.1% vs +15.6% (p = 0.003), jump +5.4% vs +10.5% [@gomes-2025-load-velocity]
  (promising for same-session concurrent training; one small trial, n = 10 per group). Two caveats:
  the participants were also endurance-trained (VO2max at least 50 mL/kg/min), and the design was the
  one the meta-analyses flag as worst, running immediately after lifting.

**Explosive strength is the most consistently blunted quality** (promising). Concurrent training
reduced gains in jumping, rate of force development and power by SMD −0.28 (−0.48 to −0.08) across 18
studies [@schumann-2022-concurrent], and −0.35 in another analysis [@huiberts-2024-concurrent-sex]. In
one 21-week trial of middle-aged men, two concurrent sessions a week left 1RM (+21%† vs +22%†) and
muscle size unaffected but abolished the gain in rate of force development† [@hakkinen-2003-concurrent].
The penalty depends on the conditioning: sprint-interval work did not blunt jumping in two 2026
meta-analyses† [@kikuchi-2026-hiit-concurrent] [@ferraro-farro-2026-sit].

## 9.3 What moderates interference

**Time between modes is the most consistent moderator** (promising). The explosive-strength penalty
was significant when the modes were less than 20 minutes apart (SMD −0.31, −0.62 to −0.01) and not
significant with at least 3 hours between them, although the two subgroups did not differ
significantly from each other [@schumann-2022-concurrent]. In "trained" participants, the strength
penalty was confined to same-session training (ES −0.66 vs −0.10 with more than 2 hours)
[@petre-2021-concurrent]. Three hours is not always enough: with about 3 hours between modes,
intervals after lifting still reduced jump gains [@lee-2020-order], and in rugby players, 6 hours
between modes was still worse than 24 for some outcomes† [@robineau-2016-recovery]. Acute recovery
fits: after very short intervals, losses in force (−7%) and rate of force development (−17%) were
gone by 6 hours in elite female hockey players [@petre-2024-hiit-recovery]; after cycling, leg-press
repetitions were down at 4 and 8 hours and recovered by 24†
[@sporer-2003-aerobic-recovery]; and isometric pull force was still reduced 24 hours after a hard
interval session† [@grammenou-2024-fitness-recovery]. Recovery takes from 6 to more than 24 hours,
depending on the session.

**If both must be in one session, lift first** (promising; small). Lifting first gave slightly better
lower-body dynamic strength: 6.91% (1.96 to 11.87) more gain across 10 studies, with no effect on
isometric strength, hypertrophy or aerobic capacity [@eddens-2018-sequence]; SMD 0.19 (0.02 to 0.37)
across 19 trials, with the benefit concentrated in older adults and women [@gao-2023-sequence]. The
umbrella review found no significant sequence effect† [@held-2026-concurrent-umbrella]. For trained
young lifters the benefit is unproven.

**Endurance volume matters at the extremes** (promising at very high volumes; speculative within
typical doses). Hickson's 11 sessions a week clearly interfered. In one small isolated-limb trial, a
1:1 ratio of strength to endurance training blunted strength and a 3:1 ratio did not†
[@jones-2013-ratio], and cross-study correlations point the same way† [@wilson-2012-concurrent]. But
the largest meta-analysis found no frequency moderation within the doses typically studied
[@schumann-2022-concurrent]. No threshold has been defined.

**Modality and intensity effects conflict** (contested). Running looked worse in an older
meta-analysis† [@wilson-2012-concurrent] and for type I fibre growth (SMD −0.81, 3 studies)
[@lundberg-2022-fiber-concurrent]. Yet continuous cycling was the most interfering mode for
lower-body strength in a network meta-analysis (−0.38, −0.62 to −0.14), and interval running the
least (−0.08) [@chen-2024-concurrent-nma]; another found no modality effect once one influential study
was removed [@schumann-2022-concurrent]. The clearest signal concerns sprint work: sprint-interval and
repeated-sprint training did not blunt jumps (SMD 0.14†), while non-sprint intervals, short and long
pooled, did (−0.53†) [@kikuchi-2026-hiit-concurrent]. Sprint intervals also raised VO2max (SMD 0.78,
0.31 to 1.24) without blunting strength [@ferraro-farro-2026-sit] (promising for "no harm" in non-elite
people). A practical reading is that what matters is how much *local* fatigue and glycogen depletion
the conditioning imposes on the muscles being trained, and how close it is to lifting (convention).

**Sex.** Men may show more interference, perhaps because of larger, more fatigable type II fibres
(speculative) [@huiberts-2024-concurrent-sex]. No trial has been designed to test interference in
women [@mikkonen-2024-females-concurrent].

**Fatigue runs both ways.** Lifting also degrades the quality of later endurance sessions for hours
to days† [@doma-2017-alternate]. In one trial, more fatiguing sets (40% velocity loss) gave the most
muscle growth but the smallest gains in aerobic speed [@tundidor-duque-2026-velocity-loss]
(speculative).

## 9.4 How the mechanism story changed

**The switch model.** In isolated rat muscle, endurance-like stimulation activated the energy sensor
AMPK without raising protein synthesis, while resistance-like stimulation activated the growth
pathway mTOR and raised myofibrillar synthesis 5.3-fold†; the authors called it an "AMPK–PKB switch"†
[@atherton-2005-ampk-switch]. The idea that endurance exercise blocks growth signalling through AMPK
became the standard explanation for interference† [@nader-2006-concurrent].

**It does not hold in humans** (promising). In 8 trained men, interval cycling before lifting raised
AMPK activity about 90%†, yet growth signalling after lifting (S6K1 activity) was the same as with
lifting alone† [@apro-2015-ampk]. Most acute human studies find growth signalling intact after prior
endurance work, though not all: some markers were lower after continuous cycling, and three
growth-related microRNAs fell after intervals† [@fyfe-2016-signalling]. Reviews conclude that
"multiple integrated, rather than isolated, effectors" are more likely
[@coffey-2017-concurrent-review], and alternatives such as the sirtuin SIRT1 and stress responses in
the endoplasmic reticulum remain largely untested [@baar-2014-concurrent].

**Size is preserved; strength and speed are not.** Over 16 weeks in untrained men, concurrent training
with long intervals preserved protein synthesis, satellite cells, myonuclei and fibre growth, yet
strength gains were smaller (p = 0.016†) [@conceicao-2026-hiit-mps]. This dissociation is the key to
the current view: interference falls on the quality of strength training, not on the machinery of
growth. Residual fatigue from endurance work reduces the velocity and intent of lifting, which costs
maximal and explosive strength more than volume-driven hypertrophy [@schumann-2022-concurrent]
(promising). Acute endurance sessions also reduce spinal motor-neuron excitability and rate of torque
development† [@gomes-2025-acute-neural] (speculative). In the strength-qualified cohort, concurrent
training blocked gains in rate of torque development and neural measures during the first,
maximal-strength block, then allowed them in the later block† [@gomes-2025-neural-training].

**Training history may sharpen specificity** (speculative). In a training-accustomed state, resistance
training alone produced larger signalling for ribosome production than concurrent training, though
baseline ribosome markers rose *more* with concurrent training, so the net effect on growth is
unknown [@fyfe-2018-ribosome].

## 9.5 Conditioning for strength athletes

No trial has compared conditioning methods head to head in competitive powerlifters, weightlifters or
strongman athletes. What follows is extrapolated.

- **Moderate continuous work, 2–3 sessions a week, on separate days or well apart from lifting,** is
  compatible with maximal strength and muscle in the pooled data (promising) [@schumann-2022-concurrent]
  [@lee-2020-order].
- **Sprint intervals** raise aerobic capacity without measurable strength or jump costs in non-elite
  people (promising for "no harm") [@ferraro-farro-2026-sit]. Cycling or sled sprints avoid the
  hamstring demands of running sprints (convention).
- **Non-sprint intervals** can blunt jump gains and lean-mass gains [@kikuchi-2026-hiit-concurrent]
  [@fyfe-2016-intensity]; use them sparingly near power or peaking blocks (convention).
- **Choose modes that spare the prime movers being trained,** such as upper-body ergometry, rowing,
  incline walking or moderate sled work (convention; not directly tested).
- **Heavy resistance training added to sports with large aerobic loads** still improved maximal strength
  and power in elite athletes, at low-to-moderate certainty [@wang-2026-hirt-elite].

**Strongman-style conditioning.** A strongman session of sled drags, farmer's walks, tyre flips and
presses produced heart-rate, energy-expenditure and lactate responses comparable to a duration-matched
heavy lifting session† [@harris-2016-strongman-acute] (promising for acute load). Resistance circuit
training raises VO2max in healthy, mostly untrained adults† [@munoz-martinez-2017-circuit]. Tyre-flip
training improved intermittent endurance in novices (ES 0.45–0.66), in a study with no non-training
control and no VO2max measure [@wong-2020-tyre-flip]. None has been compared with conventional aerobic
training in strength athletes (speculative). Implement medleys probably build work capacity at the
same local-fatigue cost as intervals.

## 9.6 What conditioning gives a lifter

**Aerobic capacity.** Concurrent training improves aerobic capacity relative to resistance training
alone (SMD 0.77†) [@held-2026-concurrent-umbrella]. Resistance training alone slightly reduced VO2max
in endurance-trained men (−3.2%) [@gomes-2025-load-velocity].

**Recovery between sets: unresolved** (contested). Practitioners often say cardio improves recovery.
In 28 resistance-trained adults, VO2max was not significantly related to fatigue within sets or
recovery between sets [@lundberg-2024-vo2max-recovery], but with 14 per sex the study could miss
moderate correlations. A second study, not yet in the evidence base, reported that VO2max correlated
with squat repetitions maintained across short-rest sets. Ten minutes of easy cycling after an
upper-body session did not change next-day bar velocity or perceived recovery†
[@vondrasek-2026-post-cycling]. The link is better supported for repeated high-intensity intermittent
efforts, where aerobic fitness speeds the resynthesis of phosphocreatine†
[@tomlin-2001-aerobic-recovery]. That fits dense, high-volume work and strongman medleys better than
heavy singles with long rests.

**Health and survival: the strongest reason** (established for the association). Each 1-MET (metabolic
equivalent) higher cardiorespiratory fitness was associated with 13% lower all-cause mortality
(RR 0.87†, 0.84 to 0.90) across 33 studies and about 100,000 people [@kodama-2009-crf]. In 122,007
patients referred for treadmill testing, elite fitness was associated with an 80% lower risk of death
than low fitness (HR 0.20, 0.16 to 0.24), with no upper limit of benefit [@mandsager-2018-crf].
Combined muscle-strengthening and aerobic activity was associated with lower all-cause, cardiovascular
and cancer mortality than neither [@momma-2022-msa]. These are observational associations. For
strength athletes, who often carry high muscle mass and modest aerobic fitness, maintaining
cardiorespiratory fitness is the most defensible reason to condition, and at moderate doses its cost
in interference is small by comparison.

## 9.7 Critical appraisal

- **Population.** No long-term trial in competitive strength athletes. The most trained cohort, men
  squatting about 1.6 × body mass, was also endurance-trained, and is reported across three papers from
  one 30-man trial.
- **Power.** Most trials have 10 or fewer per arm; "no difference" claims, including for women, sprint
  intervals and separated sessions, rest on non-significant subgroups.
- **Definitions.** "Trained" means different things in different meta-analyses, which explains part of
  their disagreement on training status.
- **Duration and site.** Mostly 6–12 weeks, mostly lower-body outcomes with lower-body conditioning;
  upper-body interference is rarely studied and appears negligible.
- **Read level.** The umbrella review, several 2026 meta-analyses and the key mechanism trials were
  read only as abstracts. The umbrella review's abstract contains an internally inconsistent sequence
  estimate and is cited here only for direction.

## 9.8 Open questions

- Do small per-block deficits in trained lifters accumulate over years, or are they recovered in later
  specific blocks?
- What weekly dose of aerobic work maintains cardiorespiratory fitness in strength athletes at no
  measurable strength cost?
- Does interference differ between running and cycling once local muscle load is matched?
- Do strongman medleys and loaded carries condition as well as conventional aerobic work?
- Does raising aerobic fitness let lifters tolerate more productive training volume?

## 9.9 Practical implications

- Condition: the health case is strong, and moderate conditioning costs little muscle or maximal
  strength (**established** association; **promising** for low cost).
- Keep hard conditioning at least 6 hours from heavy or explosive lower-body lifting, ideally on a
  different day (**promising**).
- If both must share a session, lift first (**promising**, small effect).
- Prefer sprint intervals or easy continuous work over longer hard intervals when power matters
  (**promising** for sprint intervals; **contested** for modality generally).
- Choose conditioning that spares the muscles being trained hardest (**convention**).
- Raise conditioning in general-preparation phases and reduce it before peaking or power blocks
  (**convention**).
- Do not count on cardio to speed recovery between heavy sets (**contested**).

## 9.10 Summary

Adding endurance training to strength training does not measurably reduce muscle growth in the short
trials available, and does not reduce maximal strength on average. Explosive strength is more
sensitive, and penalties appear in men, with same-session training and at high endurance volumes; in
the one trial of strength-qualified men, same-session running roughly halved squat gains. The
molecular switch model has given way to an explanation based on fatigue and the quality of strength
training, which is why separating sessions works. Sprint intervals and easy continuous work cost
least. The main reason a strength athlete should condition is cardiorespiratory fitness, one of the
strongest predictors of survival.
