# Chapter 1. Introduction and methods

*Light first draft.*

## Abstract

This thesis asks how strength and mobility are built in healthy adults who already train, and how
far the current evidence, searched through November 2026, can be trusted for them. It covers ten
topics: the neuromuscular basis of strength, training dose, programming, hypertrophy, flexibility,
strength through range, connective tissue, concurrent training, recovery and nutrition, and
individual differences. A synthesis chapter draws them together. Each topic was researched from
the primary literature, then checked by an independent pass that tried to refute its main claims
against the full texts. Every source has a structured evidence record, every claim carries a grade,
and every number is marked by whether it was read in the full text or only the abstract. The
recurring limitation is the evidence itself: most trials are short studies of untrained young men,
so most conclusions for trained lifters are extrapolations, and the thesis says so where it matters.

---

## 1.1 The question

People who lift want two things that are often treated as separate: to get stronger, and to move
well through a full range. Coaching folklore on both is abundant. In the last decade the research
literature has also grown quickly, with large dose–response meta-analyses, Bayesian re-analyses and a
stream of 2025–2026 trials. This thesis asks what that literature now supports, in four parts:

1. **What builds strength and muscle**, and in what dose (Chapters 2–5).
2. **What builds range of motion and strength through it** (Chapters 6–7).
3. **What surrounds training**: connective tissue, conditioning, recovery, nutrition and monitoring
   (Chapters 8–10).
4. **Who it applies to**: individual response, sex, age and technology (Chapter 11).

Chapter 12 combines the answers into one model.

## 1.2 Scope

The population of interest is **healthy, trained adults**, from recreational lifters to competitive
strength athletes. The thesis stands alone. It is not a rehabilitation guide and does not frame its
questions around pain or injury recovery. Clinical evidence (tendinopathy, back pain, osteoarthritis,
sarcopenia) is mentioned only where it illuminates a mechanism, and is labelled as indirect. Injury
*prevention* in healthy people is in scope where the evidence bears on training choices. Youth and
older adults appear where they show how age changes the response (Chapter 11).

## 1.3 Key terms

- **Maximal strength**: the greatest force or torque a person can express in a specified task.
  Usually measured as a one-repetition maximum (**1RM**), the heaviest load lifted once, or as
  maximal isometric force.
- **Hypertrophy**: growth of muscle, measured as whole-muscle volume or cross-sectional area (best by
  MRI), muscle thickness (ultrasound), fibre area (biopsy) or lean mass (DXA). These measures often
  disagree (Chapter 5).
- **Range of motion (ROM)**: how far a joint moves. *Passive* ROM is reached with outside help;
  *active* ROM under the person's own muscle power. **Flexibility** is the trainable soft-tissue part
  of ROM. **Mobility** is used here for usable range: range a person can control and produce force in
  (Chapter 7).
- **Effort**: how close a set ends to failure, usually rated as repetitions in reserve (**RIR**) or
  rating of perceived exertion (**RPE**).
- **Trained**: people with a history of regular resistance training. Studies define it loosely, often
  as little as six months to a year, which matters for how far results transfer to experienced
  lifters.
- **Effect sizes**: standardised mean differences (SMD, g, d) in standard-deviation units, where
  about 0.2 is small, 0.5 moderate and 0.8 large, although papers apply these labels inconsistently.

## 1.4 Method

The thesis was produced in four stages.

**1. Research.** Each literature chapter began from a set of research questions (five per chapter on
average). The literature was searched in November 2026 through PubMed's E-utilities, Europe PMC,
PubMed Central full texts, publisher and institutional repository pages, the SportRxiv and bioRxiv
preprint servers and general web search. Each chapter ran a dedicated sweep for 2025–2026 work.
Searches favoured meta-analyses and randomised trials from 2020 onward, with older primary studies
where they founded an idea or remain the best test of it. Full texts were read wherever open access
allowed; many publisher pages were blocked or paywalled, so a substantial share of numbers come from
abstracts. Each chapter's findings, disagreements, mechanisms and gaps were written up as research
notes (`notes/`).

**2. Evidence records.** Every source cited has a structured record in `/evidence/sources/`, giving
its design, population, sample, findings with the effect exactly as reported, limitations, and the
**read level**: full text, abstract or secondary. Records are shared between chapters. The evidence
base holds about 660 records: 180 meta-analyses, 6 umbrella reviews and 33 systematic reviews; 206
randomised or crossover trials; and observational, mechanistic, consensus and narrative sources.
About 60% date from 2020 or later. A third of records (209) were read in full text.

**3. Independent verification.** A separate pass for each chapter took its 14–15 most load-bearing
claims and checked each against the primary source, in full text where possible. It also searched
specifically for newer or contradicting evidence, and spot-checked numbers outside those claims. It
returned a verdict for each claim (confirmed, corrected or refuted), a corrected wording and a grade,
updated the evidence records, and added records for contradicting studies it found
(`verification/`). The chapters use the corrected versions. These passes found errors of every kind:
wrong DOIs (20 in one chapter's records), studies described as finding the opposite of what they
found, non-significant results presented as findings, subgroups mislabelled, and "no trial exists"
claims that a better search refuted.

**4. Writing and synthesis.** Each chapter was written from its notes, verification report and
evidence records, without new literature searches. Chapters 2 and 3 were written at full length;
Chapters 4–11 are light first drafts of about 2,000–3,000 words, to be expanded. An evidence audit
and a final editorial pass close the thesis.

**Who did the work.** The research, verification and writing were carried out by AI research agents
(Claude, made by Anthropic), working to a plan, research questions and conventions set by the
thesis's owner. Verification was done by separate agent passes that had not written the notes they
checked. That separation catches many errors, as the list above shows, but it is not a substitute
for expert peer review. Readers should treat the thesis as a carefully sourced synthesis whose every
number can be traced and checked, not as authoritative advice.

## 1.5 How to read the evidence

**Grades.** Every claim carries one of five grades:

- **established**: consistent evidence from several good syntheses;
- **promising**: good but limited or indirect evidence;
- **contested**: credible evidence points different ways;
- **speculative**: plausible but thinly supported;
- **convention**: practitioner practice with no direct data.

A grade applies to the population studied. A finding *established* in untrained young men may be only
*promising* for trained lifters, and chapters say so.

**Read level.** Citations are evidence-record keys such as `[@morton-2018-protein]`. An effect size is
presented as verified only when its record says it was read in the full text. In each chapter,
unmarked numbers were read in full text and a dagger (†) marks a number read only in the abstract.
Chapter 2 also uses a section sign (§) for numbers its researcher read in full but its verifier could
not reach, and a double dagger (‡) for content known only secondhand.

**Recurring cautions.** Non-significance is not equivalence: "no difference" from small trials usually
means "not shown". Agreement between papers from the same laboratory or cohort is consistency, not
replication. Industry ties are noted where they are common, as in the creatine, protein and collagen
literatures.

## 1.6 What changed from the first pass

The thesis replaces an earlier, shorter review written in the same month (round 0). That review could
read only abstracts and search snippets, and it was framed around a back-rehabilitation plan. Reading
full texts, checking claims independently and dropping the rehabilitation framing changed many of its
conclusions, mostly by lowering certainty:

- **Concurrent training** was "established" not to interfere with maximal strength. It now carries
  caveats: penalties in men, with same-session training, and a halving of squat gains in the one trial
  of strength-qualified men (Chapter 9).
- **Periodization**'s small advantage was "established". It is now contested, because the trials
  compare periodized training only with monotonous training (Chapter 4).
- **The 10-minutes-a-week stretching plateau** was "established". It is now speculative, read by eye
  from sparse data (Chapter 6).
- **Resistance training equals stretching for ROM** was "established". It is now contested: the
  intervals are too wide to show equivalence (Chapter 6).
- **The 1.6 g/kg/day protein break point** was "established". It is now a promising range, since the
  model behind it was not statistically significant (Chapter 10).
- **Eccentric over concentric training for hypertrophy** moved from established to contested
  (Chapter 5).

## 1.7 Limits of the method

- **Population.** Most trials are 6–12 weeks long in untrained or recreational young men. Women, older
  lifters and competitive strength athletes are scarce.
- **Access.** About two-thirds of evidence records were read only at abstract level, because many full
  texts were paywalled or blocked by bot checks.
- **Scope of verification.** Verification covered each chapter's load-bearing claims and a sample of
  other numbers, not every sentence.
- **No new searches while writing.** Evidence published after the research pass (late November 2026)
  is not included.
- **Synthesis, not meta-analysis.** The thesis summarises and grades existing analyses; it does not
  pool data itself.

## 1.8 Structure

| Chapter | Question |
|---|---|
| 2. The neuromuscular basis of strength | What sets maximal strength, and what changes when it rises? |
| 3. Dose | How do volume, load, effort, frequency and rest shape strength and hypertrophy? |
| 4. Programming and periodization | How should training be arranged over weeks and months? |
| 5. Hypertrophy | How does muscle grow, and which exercise features change it? |
| 6. Flexibility and range of motion | What limits range, and how do stretching and lifting change it? |
| 7. Strength through range | How strong are people across their range, and do mobility systems work? |
| 8. Connective tissue | How do tendon, ligament, bone, cartilage and disc adapt? |
| 9. Concurrent training | Does conditioning blunt strength gains, and how can it be combined? |
| 10. Recovery and nutrition | How do sleep, protein, supplements and recovery methods affect training? |
| 11. Frontiers | How do individuals, sexes and ages differ, and which technologies work? |
| 12. Synthesis | What model of building strength and mobility does the evidence support? |

Each literature chapter opens with an abstract and a note on reading its numbers, works through its
questions, and closes with a critical appraisal, open questions, practical implications with grades, a
summary and a table of key claims.
