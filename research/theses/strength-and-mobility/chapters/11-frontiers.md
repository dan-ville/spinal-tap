# Chapter 11. Frontiers: individual response, sex, age and technology

*Light first draft.*

## Abstract

The earlier chapters describe average responses. This chapter asks how far individuals depart from
them, and what the newest evidence says about sex, the menstrual cycle, age and training technology.
People do differ in how much they gain, and the differences partly repeat across training blocks,
but raw ranges greatly overstate them, and there is no evidence yet that individuals need different
programme variables. "Non-response" is mostly a matter of dose, duration and which outcome is
measured. No genetic test predicts response. Men and women gain muscle by the same relative amount;
men gain slightly more in absolute terms. Training around menstrual-cycle phases gives no advantage in
the best trial so far. Older adults remain highly trainable, and heavier loads build more strength.
Velocity-tracking devices are useful for trends but overestimate 1RM; AI-written programmes are
generic and have never been tested on outcomes. Most of this evidence comes from untrained people.

## How to read the numbers in this chapter

Citations are evidence-record keys such as [@rantila-2025]. **Unmarked numbers** were read in the
full text of the source. A **dagger (†)** marks a number read only in the abstract. Grades:
**established**, **promising**, **contested**, **speculative** and **convention** (practitioner
practice with no direct data). Intervals are 95% confidence intervals unless stated otherwise;
Bayesian studies report highest-density intervals (HDI) or credible intervals (CrI).

Two warnings run through every section. First, the response-heterogeneity, sex, menstrual-cycle and
older-adult literatures are dominated by untrained people over 6–24 weeks; trained-population results
are flagged where they exist. Second, several "frontier" products, including genetic tests,
cycle-syncing programmes, readiness scores and AI coaching apps, are sold ahead of the evidence.

## 11.1 Individual response to training

### 11.1.1 Raw ranges overstate it

In 585 untrained adults doing the same 12-week arm programme, muscle size changed by −2% to +59%† and
1RM by 0% to +250%† [@hubal-2005]. Ranges like these are routinely quoted as proof that people respond
very differently. But with no control group, they mix true differences in response with measurement
error and ordinary day-to-day biological variation (established) [@atkinson-2015]
[@hecksteden-2015]. Plots of individual change lines can show large "individual differences" even when
everyone's true response is identical, and regression to the mean adds to the illusion.

The correct first test compares the spread of changes in a training group with the spread in a
time-matched control group. The difference gives the standard deviation of individual responses
(SD_IR): SD_IR = √(SD_train² − SD_control²) [@atkinson-2015] [@atkinson-2019]. Only if it is
meaningfully above zero is it logical to look for moderators, genes or "non-responders". Counting
"responders" against a threshold is biased: counts can differ between samples simply because their
mean responses differ† [@atkinson-2019], and different classification methods applied to one
endurance trial agreed on only 11 of 20 people† [@hecksteden-2018]. For coaches, the practical tool is
a confidence interval around each person's change, built from the test's typical error and compared
with a pre-set smallest worthwhile change [@swinton-2018-individual-response].

### 11.1.2 Real differences exist, partly stable

The best direct test is to train the same people twice (promising, in novices). Twenty untrained adults
did two identical 10-week programmes separated by 10 weeks of detraining. The SD of individual
responses exceeded zero for every outcome, and responses repeated across blocks: r = 0.70 for thigh
muscle size, 0.76 for biceps size and 0.67 for leg-press 1RM, but only about 0.39 (not significant) for
biceps-curl 1RM [@rantila-2025]. Something stable about the person matters, though whether it is
biology or behaviour (effort, adherence, diet, daily activity) is not established. In trained men,
differences between people in muscle growth were about 40 times larger than differences between their
legs trained with different variables† [@damas-2019]: who you are matters more than which reasonable
programme you do.

### 11.1.3 General trainability, not a personal programme

**People appear to differ in general trainability, not in which variables suit them** (contested). In
the only replicated test in recreationally trained people (16 adults, legs trained at about 8 vs 16
sets a week over two 11-week phases), general responsiveness repeated across phases (r = 0.67–0.70†),
but the person-specific benefit of higher volume did not (r = 0.04–0.06†); the study is still a
preprint and too small to detect moderate effects [@robinson-2025-volume-individual]. The counter-signal
comes from older adults: those classified as non-responders to 1 set a week grew, as a group, on a
second leg trained with 4 sets† [@lixandrao-2024-volume-nonresponders], though classification and
comparison in the same data invite regression to the mean.

A meta-analysis of variation across controlled trials found more spread in training groups than
controls on absolute measures, but *less* relative to the larger mean change (strength lnCVR −0.61,
−0.76 to −0.47) [@steele-2023-variation]. That shows absolute-variance statistics overstate
heterogeneity; partly because of how the ratio behaves when control changes are near zero, it cannot
show that heterogeneity is absent.

### 11.1.4 "Non-responders"

Non-response is best described as specific to an outcome and a dose (promising). In the replicated
trial, about one in five novices failed to exceed measurement error on the same single strength outcome
in both blocks, but nobody failed on more than one outcome, and nobody repeatedly failed to grow
[@rantila-2025]. In older adults, extra volume helped many 1-set non-responders, though a subgroup did
not grow at either volume† [@lixandrao-2024-volume-nonresponders] [@lixandrao-2025-molecular]. In 110
older adults, every participant improved on at least one outcome, and non-response fell from 12 to 24
weeks† [@churchward-venne-2015]. All these counts use thresholds, so the proportions are approximate.
Before attributing low response to biology, a 2026 expert group advises checking adherence,
progression, effort, sleep and diet [@roberts-2026-heterogeneity-conference] (convention).

### 11.1.5 Mechanisms and genetics

The mechanisms most consistently linked to high hypertrophic response across laboratories are ribosome
production and satellite-cell expansion with new myonuclei [@roberts-2026-heterogeneity-conference]
(promising). In one cluster analysis, extreme responders grew fibres 58%† and expanded satellite cells
117%†, while non-responders did neither† [@petrella-2008-satellite]; clustering forces groups even
from noise. Circulating hormones within the normal range do not separate high and low responders
(promising). Androgen receptors are contested: training-induced changes in receptor markers did not
track growth, but baseline receptor content was associated with hypertrophy in men in two studies†
[@morton-2018-androgen-receptor] [@bergamasco-2024-androgen-receptor].

**No genetic predictor of response has been validated** (established). Of about 500 candidate gene
variants in one large cohort, only two were associated with hypertrophy, and genome-wide hits have
proven hard to replicate; experts estimate a robust polygenic score would need over 100,000
participants [@roberts-2026-heterogeneity-conference]. Even baseline grip strength needed 195,180
people to find 16 genetic loci† [@willems-2017-grip-gwas]. A 2024 score said to explain 47% of
hypertrophy variance in 440 adults† was derived and tested in the same cohort with a liberal threshold
[@yang-2024-gwas]; in our assessment it is at high risk of overfitting until replicated elsewhere.
Consensus statements say genetic tests have no role in individualising training†
[@webborn-2015-genetic-testing], or should not be given undue weight
[@roberts-2026-heterogeneity-conference].

## 11.2 Sex differences

**Men and women gain muscle by the same relative amount** (established in untrained young adults).
Across 29 studies, relative gains were equal (difference 0.69%, −1.50% to 2.88%), while absolute gains
slightly favoured men (SMD 0.19, 0.11 to 0.28), more in the upper body (0.30) than the lower (0.17),
because the baseline gap is larger there [@refalo-2025-sex]. Training status did not change this, but
the trained subgroup had only 13 observations. In older adults, relative size gains also did not
differ† [@jones-2021-sex-older].

**Women gain more relative upper-body strength in young adults** (promising). An earlier meta-analysis
found larger relative upper-body strength gains in women (ES −0.60†) and similar lower-body gains†
[@roberts-2020-sex]; in adults over 50 the upper-body difference was not significant (g −0.29†)
[@jones-2021-sex-older]. The likely reason is lower baseline strength and more room for neural and
skill learning, not superior adaptation; no trial has isolated it. Across 295 strength and
conditioning studies, adding sex did not improve prediction of strength and power outcomes; that model
had no hypertrophy outcomes [@swinton-2024-dose-response].

**Mechanisms.** Baseline sex differences in strength emerge around age 15 and stem from muscle mass and
type II fibre area, not from voluntary activation† [@nuzzo-2023-sex-review]. The roughly tenfold
higher testosterone in men does not explain the near-identical relative hypertrophy. Repetitions to
failure at a given percentage of 1RM showed little moderation by sex across 269 studies†
[@nuzzo-2024-reps-1rm], so sex-specific repetition tables are not supported. Men show more
interference from concurrent endurance training than women (Chapter 9)
[@huiberts-2024-concurrent-sex].

## 11.3 The menstrual cycle and contraception

**Acute performance.** Across all exercise outcomes, performance may be trivially lower in the early
follicular phase (ES −0.06, CrI −0.16 to 0.04), from mostly low-quality evidence that pooled endurance
and strength [@mcnulty-2020-menstrual]. In 28 resistance-trained women followed through two
mesocycles with hormonally verified phases, bar velocity deviated from predicted values by only about
0.01–0.02 m/s across phases; self-rated motivation was a significant predictor of similar size
[@munteanu-2026-menstrual-vbt] (promising). One weaker strength-only meta-analysis reports
small-to-moderate phase effects for isometric strength (SMD 0.60†, 7 studies)
[@niering-2024-menstrual-strength], so this is contested for isometric strength. For dynamic training,
phase effects appear too small to matter.

**Training adaptation: phase-based programming gives no advantage** (promising). The claim that loading
training into the follicular phase is superior† [@kissow-2022-phase-based] rests mainly on two small
trials with weak phase detection, unsupervised sessions and unequal volume
[@colenso-semple-2023-menstrual]. Muscle protein synthesis responded identically to exercise in the
follicular and luteal phases [@colenso-semple-2025-mps]. In a 2026 trial, 24 women each trained their
two legs under two of four conditions over three cycles: high volume concentrated in the follicular
phase, in the luteal phase, continuous training, or none. All training beat no training, with no
differences among the training conditions (all p ≥ 0.17) [@dsouza-2026-menstrual-rct]. The
participants were untrained or recreationally active, and the group overlaps with the earlier null
studies, so independent replication is needed. Cycle and phase lengths vary within women by more than
a week and ovulation is not guaranteed, so calendar-based phase programming often targets the wrong
hormonal state [@colenso-semple-2023-menstrual].

**Oral contraceptives do not change adaptations** (promising): hypertrophy 0.01 (−0.11 to 0.13),
strength 0.10 (−0.08 to 0.28), across 8 studies and 325 women [@nolan-2024-contraceptive]. No data
exist for non-oral methods. Any acute performance effect is most likely trivial
[@elliott-sale-2020-ocp].

## 11.4 Age

**Older adults remain highly trainable** (established for strength). In adults aged 75 and over,
training improved strength by ES 0.97† (0.50 to 1.44) and muscle size by 0.30†
[@grgic-2020-very-elderly]. In healthy older adults, standardised strength effects (SMD 1.57) were
several times larger than size effects (0.42) [@borde-2015-older-dose], consistent with large neural
contributions. Age did not change mean responses in a pooled cohort aged 19–78† [@ahtiainen-2016].
Competitive powerlifters retained or still gained strength into their 60s, with slight losses only in
men over 69† [@latella-2024-powerlifting-age] (promising; competition data with survivor bias). Muscle
size gains are lost when training stops† [@grgic-2022-detraining-older].

**Why older muscle responds less.** "Anabolic resistance" involves blunted protein synthesis and
ribosome production after exercise; lower testosterone may matter in older men, and the diabetes drug
metformin blunted hypertrophy in one trial [@roberts-2026-heterogeneity-conference] (promising). The
benefit of protein supplementation falls with age [@morton-2018-protein]. Strength and power decline
faster than muscle mass and predict function better, so programmes for ageing lifters should
prioritise them† [@fyfe-2022-minimal-dose].

**Dose for older adults.** Loads of at least 70% 1RM produced larger strength gains than lighter loads
(leg press SMD 0.95†) [@shen-2026-intensity-older] (promising). No significant difference in falls or
adverse events was found, but the estimates are too imprecise to show equal safety (RR 2.68† and
2.42†, intervals spanning about 0.65 to 11). Indirect comparisons suggest higher-volume programmes
build somewhat more lean mass in older women (g 0.52† vs 0.34†, each against control; GRADE low)
[@nunes-2024-volume-older-women] (speculative to promising). Minimal doses, such as single hard sets or
short "exercise snacks", improve strength across ages† [@fyfe-2022-minimal-dose]; specific numbers
such as 1–3 sets once or twice a week come mostly from younger or mixed-age data and remain
convention for older lifters.

## 11.5 Technology

**Velocity devices** (promising). Linear position transducers appear the most accurate and reliable
devices, though most validation studies lack a gold-standard criterion [@weakley-2021-devices].
Predicting 1RM from the load–velocity relationship has a standard error of about 10% of 1RM (9.8%,
7.4 to 12.2), worse for the squat (about 12%), and systematically overestimates by about 4.5 kg (3.7%)
[@greig-2023-load-velocity]. A 2026 meta-analysis reporting high correlations (ICC 0.91) for 1RM
prediction rests on only 9 effects and measures ranking rather than absolute error
[@claassen-2026-load-velocity]. Velocity-based 1RM estimates are good for tracking trends, not for
replacing a strong lifter's 1RM test.

**Velocity-based training** gives strength gains similar to percentage-based training, with a small
advantage not excluded (SMD 0.21, −0.01 to 0.43), and small advantages for jumping and change of
direction [@wang-2026-vbt] (promising; Chapter 4).

**Wearables and readiness.** A smartwatch app recognised 88.4% of sets but produced a usable 1RM
prediction in only 8.9% of attempts [@oberhofer-2021-smartwatch]. Two small trials of HRV-guided
resistance training, in untrained young men and older women, found no benefit†
[@de-oliveira-2019-hrv-rt] [@bittencourt-2024-hrv-rt]. There are no controlled trials in
strength-trained people, and none of commercial readiness scores (speculative).

**AI programming** (speculative). Programmes generated by large language models are rated as moderate
in quality and generic [@washif-2024-chatgpt]. Newer model versions score better (2.4 to 4.1 out of 5
across three versions) but still need human oversight [@genc-2025-chatgpt-versions], and expert raters
barely agree with one another (Fleiss' κ about 0) [@havers-2025-ai-plans]. Every study rates
programme text; no randomised trial has tested AI-generated programmes on strength or hypertrophy
outcomes.

## 11.6 Critical appraisal

- **Population.** Response heterogeneity, sex, menstrual and older-adult data come almost entirely
  from untrained or older people. The trained-population exceptions are one replicated trial of 16
  people (a preprint), one study of trained men, 13 observations in the sex meta-analysis, and one
  study of trained women's bar velocity.
- **Counting responders.** Proportions of "non-responders" depend on thresholds and are biased.
- **Single groups.** The phase-based training null comes from one research group.
- **Commercial pressure.** Genetic tests, cycle-syncing programmes, readiness scores and AI coaches
  are marketed without outcome trials.
- **Read level.** Several load-bearing items were read only as abstracts, including the replicated
  volume trial, the older-adult volume and intensity analyses, and the earlier sex meta-analysis.

## 11.7 Open questions

- Do trained lifters show stable person-level differences in rate of gain?
- Does any programme variable have a person-specific optimum different from the population
  dose–response?
- Does phase-based training matter in trained women or in women with symptomatic cycles?
- How do trained masters lifters respond to different doses?
- Do AI-generated or app-adapted programmes produce better outcomes than coach-designed or template
  programmes?

## 11.8 Practical implications

- Do not label anyone a non-responder from one block or one outcome; raise the dose, extend the time,
  check adherence and measure several outcomes first (**promising**).
- Judge individual progress against the test's typical error and a smallest worthwhile change
  (**established** method).
- Ignore genetic tests for training decisions (**established**).
- Program women by the same principles as men (**established** for relative hypertrophy).
- Train consistently through the menstrual cycle; adjust for symptoms and motivation, not calendar phase
  (**promising**).
- Keep older lifters training heavy (≥70% 1RM) for strength, add volume for size, and do not stop
  (**promising**).
- Use velocity devices for trends and autoregulation, not exact 1RM (**promising**).
- Treat AI-written programmes as drafts for an expert to adjust (**speculative**).

## 11.9 Summary

People differ in how much they gain, and part of that difference repeats, but raw ranges exaggerate
it, non-response is mostly a dose and measurement problem, and there is no evidence yet that
individuals need different programme variables or that genes can predict response. Men and women
respond alike in relative terms. Phase-based training around the menstrual cycle gives no advantage in
the best trial so far, though that trial needs independent replication. Older adults stay trainable,
especially with heavy loads. Velocity devices help track trends, and AI programming has not been
tested on outcomes. Most of this frontier evidence is in untrained people, which is the main limit on
applying it to trained lifters.
