# Chapter 12. Synthesis: an evidence-based model for building strength and mobility

*Light first draft.*

## Abstract

This chapter draws Chapters 2–11 into one model. Strength and mobility are built by a small number of
signals, delivered in a dose with diminishing returns, specific to the task and the range trained,
and expressed by tissues that adapt on very different clocks. Muscle grows from tension on fibres
in sets taken close to failure, across a wide range of loads. Maximal strength grows from heavy,
frequent practice of the target task, and over a career from added muscle. Range of motion grows
mostly through tolerance, whether by stretching or by loading through full range, and usable range
means strength at the ends of it. Nerves adapt in weeks, tendon material and muscle architecture in
weeks, muscle in months, bone over a year, and the tendon core and cartilage barely at all. Most
other choices, such as the periodization model, supplements beyond creatine and caffeine, recovery
gadgets and named systems, matter far less than claimed, and a few, such as regular cold-water
immersion and hard same-session conditioning, can blunt gains. The model's main weakness is that it
rests on short trials in untrained young men.

## How to read this chapter

Grades are those used throughout: **established**, **promising**, **contested**, **speculative** and
**convention**. A synthesis grade is never higher than the grade of the chapter evidence it rests on.
Each principle names the chapters that carry its evidence; a few headline sources are cited as
evidence-record keys. Numbers are not repeated here; the chapters give them with their read levels.

## 12.2 Principle 1: train for tension, and for strength train heavy and often

**Muscle grows from tension, not from the by-products of training** (established that loading is the
primary stimulus). Across loads from about 30% of 1RM upward, hypertrophy is similar when sets end
close to failure; very light loads grow less even to failure (Chapters 3 and 5)
[@lopez-2021-load] [@lasevicius-2018-intensity]. The leading explanation, fatigue-driven recruitment
of high-threshold motor units, is promising, not settled. Acute hormone rises, metabolic stress, the
pump and muscle damage are not independent drivers (established for hormones; promising for the rest).
The early molecular "switch" model in which endurance signalling blocks growth has not held up in
humans (Chapter 9).

**Effort matters for growth, up to a point** (promising). Growth rises as sets end closer to failure,
and stopping about 1–3 repetitions short has matched failure in trained lifters, with less fatigue
(Chapter 3) [@robinson-2024-proximity-failure] [@refalo-2024-failure-rir-trial].

**Maximal strength needs heavy, specific, frequent practice** (established for load and specificity;
promising for frequency). 1RM gains depend on load, much of the advantage of heavy training is
practice with heavy loads, and frequent exposure to the lift helps beyond total volume. Proximity to
failure barely matters for strength (Chapters 2 and 3).

**Over a career, muscle sets the ceiling** (promising between people; contested within a person).
Between trained people, muscle size is the main determinant of strength. Whether, and how much, adding
muscle raises a particular lifter's 1RM is unresolved; the best reading is that it does, less than
one-to-one and with a delay (Chapter 2). The practical consequence is a two-pronged model: practise the
target lift heavily to express capacity, and build muscle to raise it.

## 12.3 Principle 2: dose with diminishing returns, and the minimums are low

**More hard sets grow more muscle, with diminishing returns** (established for direction; promising
for shape). Returns diminish without a clear plateau up to about 25 weekly sets per muscle, but
whether raising volume above habitual moderate-to-high levels adds anything for trained people is
contested (Chapter 3) [@pelland-2026-volume-frequency]. Counting indirect sets as half predicts
outcomes best.

**Strength saturates early with volume** (promising). Detectable strength gains from extra direct sets
flatten after a few sets per week in mostly novice data; powerlifting convention is about three to six
heavy sets per lift per week.

**Minimum and maintenance doses are low** (promising). About one hard set a week measurably builds
strength, about four build muscle, and about one session a week at maintained load holds most strength
for months (Chapter 3). For busy periods and for older lifters, the floor is lower than most
programmes assume (Chapter 11).

**Frequency at equal volume is a scheduling choice for size**, but helps strength up to about twice a
week per lift (Chapter 3). **Rest** matters mainly for preserving set quality.

## 12.4 Principle 3: train the task and the range you need

**Strength is specific** (established). Gains transfer only partly between tests that differ in
movement, equipment, limb configuration or velocity (Chapter 2). Accessory gains reach the main lift
mainly through added muscle.

**Strength is specific to range, and long lengths transfer further** (promising). Deep squats raise
both deep and shallow strength; shallow squats raise mostly shallow strength. Long-length isometrics
build strength across angles and shift peak torque toward longer lengths; short-length isometrics do
not (Chapter 7).

**Train through the lengthened range, but full range is the default** (promising). Avoiding the
lengthened range costs growth; emphasising it adds a small amount, mostly in muscles that cross two
joints and in distal regions of the muscle, and in trained lifters lengthened partials have matched
full range rather than beaten it (Chapter 5). Exercise selection decides *where* muscle grows more than
*how much*.

**Contraction type and special methods change shape, strength or power more than size** (promising).
Eccentric training lengthens fascicles; accentuated eccentrics are a strength tool and flywheels a
power tool; blood-flow restriction matches heavy loads for growth at lighter loads but builds less
strength (Chapter 5).

## 12.5 Principle 4: mobility is tolerance plus loaded range

**Stretching reliably increases passive range** (established), mainly by raising tolerance of the
stretch, with a smaller reduction in muscle stiffness; whether it lengthens fascicles is contested
(Chapter 6). Held positions (static or PNF) beat dynamic stretching for lasting gains; frequency and
intensity do not matter, and the dose evidence is inconsistent.

**Full-range resistance training improves range too** (promising), and has not been shown to differ
from stretching, though the comparisons cannot exclude moderate differences, and one trial favoured
stretching at the shoulder (contested) (Chapters 6 and 7). Eccentric training improves range
(established for direction), partly through the same tolerance mechanism.

**Usable range is strength at the ends of range** (speculative as a measure). Passive range says
little about whether a lifter can produce force in a position. The most defensible "mobility" outcome
is active range and strength near end range, in the positions a lifter's sport needs (Chapter 7).

**Stretching is a range tool, not a strength, muscle or injury tool** (promising). It builds a little
strength, barely any muscle and shows no detectable effect on injury risk; long static holds before
maximal efforts cost a little isolated strength, short ones inside a warm-up do not (Chapter 6).

**Named mobility systems package reasonable exercises around untested claims** (convention for the
systems). Their components (end-range isometrics, contract–relax stretching, deep loaded positions,
eccentrics) have evidence; their outcome claims do not (Chapter 7).

## 12.6 Principle 5: tissues adapt on different clocks

| Tissue or property | Responds within | Lost after stopping | Grade | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Neural and skill (early strength) | weeks | slowly; strength outlasts size | established (early) | 2 |
| Tendon stiffness and modulus | 4–8 weeks | within about 2 months | promising | 8 |
| Fascicle length (eccentric, long-length work) | weeks | largely within 3–4 weeks | promising | 7 |
| Passive range of motion | weeks | slowly and partly | promising | 6 |
| Muscle size | months | months; regained faster | established (gain) | 3, 5 |
| Bone | months to a year | slowly | promising | 8 |
| Tendon core and cartilage collagen | essentially not replaced in adults | — | established | 8 |

Three consequences follow (mostly speculative, because no outcome trials test them):

1. **Strength can outrun tendon.** Strength rises fast and persists; tendon stiffness rises more slowly
   and detrains faster. Periods of rapid strength gain and returns after layoffs are when tendon strain
   is likely highest, so load should ramp more cautiously then (Chapter 8).
2. **Architectural and range gains need upkeep.** Fascicle-length and range gains fade within weeks,
   so the work that builds them has to continue at some dose (Chapters 6 and 7).
3. **Some tissues cannot be "trained up".** The adult tendon core and cartilage are essentially
   permanent, so a long career depends on not exceeding their capacity rather than on building it.
   Bone favours few, varied, heavy loads, and raising load costs it far more fatigue life than adding
   repetitions (Chapter 8).

## 12.7 Principle 6: protect the signal

Most things around training shift the response only at the margins. A few can blunt it.

**Things that can blunt gains:**

- **Regular cold-water immersion after lifting** (promising), through reduced amino-acid delivery and a
  damped repair response (Chapter 10).
- **Hard conditioning close to lifting** (promising). Same-session or short-gap endurance work costs
  explosive strength and, in the one trial of strength-qualified men, roughly halved squat gains;
  separating modes by six hours or a day removes most of the cost (Chapter 9).
- **Energy deficits** impair lean-mass gains; strength impairment has not been shown (promising)
  (Chapter 10).
- **Sleep loss** costs a few percent of strength and more of lifting quality acutely; whether chronic
  short sleep blunts gains is untested (speculative for adaptation) (Chapter 10).
- **High-dose anti-inflammatory drugs**: contested, with trials pointing both ways (Chapter 10).

**Things that help a little:**

- **Protein** adds a small amount to training gains, with most of the benefit by about 1.6 g/kg/day
  (promising for the range) [@morton-2018-protein], more when dieting.
- **Creatine** adds strength, by less than headline figures suggest, and a little directly measured
  muscle (established for direction) (Chapter 10).
- **Caffeine** gives a small acute boost to strength and bar velocity (established).

**Things that help comfort, not adaptation:** massage and compression (promising) (Chapter 10).

**Conditioning is worth its small cost.** Cardiorespiratory fitness is one of the strongest observed
predictors of survival (established association) [@mandsager-2018-crf], and at moderate doses,
placed well, it costs little muscle or maximal strength (Chapter 9).

## 12.8 Principle 7: content matters more than arrangement

**What is in a programme matters more than how it is sequenced** (promising). Periodized training beats
constant training by a small margin that mostly reflects variation and test practice; linear and
undulating models give similar results; no model reliably beats another (Chapter 4). Autoregulation by
effort ratings or bar velocity is best understood as a way of keeping load and effort in step with
strength, not as a superior system (promising). Tapering before competition is supported; routine
deloads are mostly convention (Chapter 4).

**Individual differences do not yet justify individual programmes** (contested). People differ in
general trainability, partly stably, but there is no replicated evidence that any variable has a
person-specific optimum. Low response is mostly a matter of dose, duration, adherence and which
outcome is measured (Chapter 11). Men and women gain muscle by the same relative amount; menstrual
phase-based programming gave no advantage in the best trial; older adults stay highly trainable
(Chapter 11).

## 12.10 A worked default for a trained adult

The defaults below assemble the graded principles into one week for an intermediate lifter who wants
strength, muscle and usable range. The whole is **convention built from promising and established
parts**; it has not been tested as a package.

- **Structure.** Three or four full-body or upper–lower sessions a week. Each main lift is practised at
  least twice a week (Chapter 3).
- **Strength work.** Two or three working sets of each main lift above about 80% of 1RM, stopping a
  repetition or two short of failure; heavier singles or doubles weekly near competition (Chapters 2–4).
- **Hypertrophy work.** About 10–15 hard sets per muscle per week, counting indirect work as half, at
  1–3 repetitions in reserve, with loads from moderate to heavy (Chapter 3).
- **Range.** Full-range exercises chosen to load the lengthened position of each target muscle, such as
  deep squats, seated or hip-flexed hamstring curls, overhead triceps work and straight-knee calf raises
  (Chapters 5 and 7). Add held stretching only for joints whose range limits a needed position
  (Chapter 6). Test active range and strength in those positions, not general flexibility (Chapter 7).
- **Tendon and bone.** Heavy compound lifting covers most tendons; add a few sets of heavy 3-second
  contractions where a tendon is not loaded heavily, and some varied or impact loading for bone
  (Chapter 8).
- **Conditioning.** Two or three sessions a week of easy continuous work or sprint intervals, at least
  six hours from heavy lower-body lifting, ideally on separate days (Chapter 9).
- **Recovery and nutrition.** Enough sleep, especially the end of the night; about 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day of
  protein; 3–5 g of creatine a day; no routine cold-water immersion in building phases (Chapter 10).
- **Monitoring and adjustment.** Effort ratings in each session, bar velocity at a standard warm-up load,
  and a short wellness questionnaire; judge progress against the test's typical error over several
  weeks, and raise the dose before concluding someone does not respond (Chapters 4, 10 and 11).

## 12.11 Where the evidence is weakest

Six weaknesses recur in every chapter.

1. **Population.** Most trials study untrained or recreationally active young men. Women, masters
   lifters and competitive strength athletes are scarce, so most conclusions for trained lifters are
   extrapolations. Where trained-lifter trials exist, they sometimes disagree with the pooled averages,
   as in concurrent training (Chapter 9).
2. **Duration.** Trials last 6–12 weeks. Careers last decades. Whether short-term effects (volume
   returns, long-length advantages, interference) persist, grow or fade is largely unknown.
3. **Measurement.** Muscle growth measured by DXA, ultrasound, biopsy and MRI disagree; fascicle length
   is mostly extrapolated; range is mostly passive and tolerance-bound. Several debates are partly
   measurement disputes (Chapters 2, 5, 7 and 10).
4. **Power.** Small samples make "no significant difference" common; the thesis reads it as "not
   shown", not "equivalent".
5. **Concentration.** Key findings come from a few laboratories, and some literatures (creatine,
   protein, collagen, stretching) have common industry ties or author networks.
6. **Access.** About two-thirds of the evidence records were read only as abstracts (Chapter 1).

## 12.12 A research agenda

The studies that would most change this model:

1. **Long-term trials in trained lifters** (a year or more), comparing volumes and training emphases
   with imaging outcomes. This is the decisive test of whether added muscle raises 1RM, and of the
   high-volume region (Chapters 2 and 3).
2. **Replicated-period trials in trained people** to test whether any programme variable has a
   person-specific optimum (Chapter 11).
3. **Range trials with usable outcomes**: does loaded full-range training or stretching better improve
   active range and strength in the positions lifters need (Chapters 6 and 7)?
4. **Direct measurement of sarcomere number** after eccentric and long-length training in humans
   (Chapter 7).
5. **Concurrent-training trials in strength athletes** mapping conditioning dose and spacing against
   strength cost (Chapter 9).
6. **Chronic sleep-restriction trials** with training outcomes (Chapter 10).
7. **Tendon-load monitoring** validated against injury in adults (Chapter 8).
8. **Outcome trials of AI-generated and app-adapted programmes** against coach-designed ones
   (Chapter 11).

## 12.13 Conclusion

The evidence supports a model simpler than the training industry suggests. Lift heavy and often for
strength, take enough hard sets close to failure for muscle, train through full range with emphasis on
the stretched position, and stretch the joints whose range you need. Respect that tissues adapt on
different clocks: ramp load carefully when strength is rising fast or after a break, and keep doing the
work that builds range and architecture. Protect the training signal with sleep, adequate protein and
sensible placement of conditioning, and avoid the few things that blunt it. Beyond that, most choices
are matters of preference and adherence. What would most improve this model is not another short trial
in untrained young men, but long trials in the people this thesis is about.
