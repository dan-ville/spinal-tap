# Chapter 4. Programming, periodization and autoregulation

*Light first draft.*

## Abstract

Chapter 3 asked how much training builds strength and muscle. This chapter asks how that training
should be arranged over weeks and months. It examines four questions: whether periodized training
beats non-periodized training, and whether any named model (linear, undulating, block, conjugate)
beats the others; what the conceptual critique of periodization amounts to; whether autoregulation
(adjusting load or volume to effort ratings or bar speed) beats fixed percentages; and what is known
about deloads, tapering and peaking. The argument is that what matters is the *content* of
training: enough heavy, specific practice, loads that keep pace with rising strength, hard-enough
sets and some variation. The *sequence* in which that content is arranged matters much less than
periodization doctrine claims, at least over the 6–12 weeks that trials last. Autoregulation is best
understood as a way of getting the dose right, not as a superior system. Peaking before competition
is the one planning element where practice and evidence agree. Routine deloads are the clearest
case of convention running ahead of the data.

## How to read the numbers in this chapter

As in Chapter 3, citations are evidence-record keys such as [@zhang-2026-lp-up]. **Unmarked
numbers** were read in the full text of the source. A **dagger (†)** marks a number read only in
the abstract. Grades: **established**, **promising**, **contested**, **speculative** and
**convention** (practitioner practice with no direct data). Intervals are 95% confidence intervals
unless stated otherwise.

## 4.1 Programming is not periodization

Two terms are routinely confused. *Periodization* is the planned sequencing of training periods
across a season: build general capacity, convert it to specific capacity, peak for competition.
*Programming* is the session-to-session prescription: which exercises, how many sets and repetitions,
at what load. Most studies labelled "periodization" in fact compare programming schemes over a
single block of 6–12 weeks [@kataoka-2021-periodization-definition]. The American College of Sports
Medicine's 2026 position stand concedes that the reviews it graded conflate the two
[@currier-2026-acsm-position-stand]. "Linear", "undulating" and "block" are therefore best read as
labels for how load and volume vary *within* a block:

- **Linear (LP):** volume falls and load rises gradually across the block.
- **Undulating (UP, or DUP when daily):** load and repetitions vary from session to session or week
  to week, so heavy, moderate and light days alternate.
- **Block:** consecutive blocks concentrate on one quality (accumulation, transmutation, realization).
- **Conjugate:** several qualities (maximal-effort and dynamic-effort days) are trained concurrently,
  with exercises rotated.
- **Reverse linear:** load falls and repetitions rise across the block.

This distinction matters for everything that follows. A trial lasting eight weeks can test whether
undulating loads beat steadily rising loads. It cannot test whether a year planned in advance beats a
year planned as it goes.

## 4.2 Does periodization work?

### 4.2.1 Periodized against non-periodized training

Two meta-analyses dominate. Williams and colleagues pooled 18 studies (81 effects, 612 participants)
and found that periodized training produced larger one-repetition maximum (1RM) gains than
non-periodized training, effect size (ES) 0.43 (0.27–0.58) [@williams-2017-periodization]. That
estimate shrinks under scrutiny: it fell to 0.38 (0.09–0.66) when effects were nested within
studies, and to 0.23 (0.13–0.33) when 14 outlying effects were removed, and Egger's test suggested
publication bias. Equating volume did not moderate the effect. Moesgaard and colleagues, pooling 35
studies with volume equated, found ES 0.31 (0.04–0.57)† for strength and no difference for
hypertrophy, ES 0.13 (−0.10 to 0.36)† [@moesgaard-2022-periodization]. The ACSM panel, counting
reviews, concluded that the effect of periodization on strength "cannot be determined" and that it
has no effect on hypertrophy: "with appropriate progressive overload, periodization is not
significantly superior to nonperiodized programs" [@currier-2026-acsm-position-stand].

The honest summary is a small advantage for periodized over constant programs, with no added
hypertrophy (grade: **contested**).

### 4.2.2 What the comparison actually tests

Afonso and colleagues audited the 21 primary studies behind the older meta-analyses
[@afonso-2019-periodization-meta]. Every one compared periodized training with a *constant*
program, that is, one without planned variation. None compared it with training that varied without
a plan. None tested a timing prediction, such as when an adaptation should appear. Eight of the 21
were not about periodization at all (they compared volumes, set numbers or supervision), and all
scored at maximal risk of bias on the audit's instrument. Whether Moesgaard's comparators were of
the same kind is a reasonable inference but unverified, because its full text could not be read.

The meta-analytic effect is therefore best read as "variation beats monotony", not "a plan beats
flexibility" (grade: **established** for the audit's findings). Two further problems weaken the
literature. Studies last weeks while periodization claims concern months. And the 1RM test rewards
programs that rehearse it: periodized and undulating arms usually reach heavy, test-like loads more
often, and practising the 1RM test alone can match high-volume training for 1RM gains
[@mattocks-2017-practice-test]†.

### 4.2.3 Model against model

- **Linear against undulating.** The newest synthesis, 29 studies and 704 participants to July
  2025, found no difference for upper-body strength, standardized mean difference (SMD) 0.08 (−0.15
  to 0.31), or squat strength, SMD 0.08 (−0.10 to 0.26) [@zhang-2026-lp-up]. Crucially, it also
  found none in trained participants (upper body 0.03, −0.34 to 0.40; squat −0.02, −0.22 to 0.18).
  An earlier subgroup suggesting undulating training helps trained lifters, ES 0.61
  (0.00–1.22)† [@moesgaard-2022-periodization], was therefore not reproduced. Hypertrophy does not
  differ either, d = −0.02 (−0.25 to 0.21), but 12 of the 13 studies behind that estimate used
  untrained participants [@grgic-2017-lp-dup-hypertrophy] (grade: **promising**).
- **Flexible against fixed undulating.** Letting lifters choose which session to do that day gave the
  same results as a fixed order (total +36.8 vs +40.4 kg†) [@colquhoun-2017-fdup].
- **Block.** No meta-analysis exists for maximal strength, and the small trials conflict. A 2026
  crossover in 20 functional-fitness athletes favoured concentrated loading for deadlift 1RM
  (d = 0.85†) [@micke-2026-block-hift]. A 2023 trial favoured mixed sessions over block for bench
  1RM [@bartolomei-2023-mixed-block]†. A larger 2026 trial (n = 80) found no program superior
  [@puschkasch-mock-2026-power-phase]† (grade: **speculative**).
- **Reverse linear.** Not superior in 11 low-quality studies [@gonzalez-rave-2022-reverse] (grade:
  **promising**).
- **Conjugate.** No controlled trial was found (grade: **convention**).

## 4.3 The conceptual critique

The empirical weakness has a theoretical counterpart. Kiely argues that periodization rests on Hans
Selye's general adaptation syndrome and on Walter Cannon's homeostasis, and that stress science has
moved on [@kiely-2018-periodization]. Stress responses depend on the individual, the context and how
the stressor is appraised; fixed set-points have given way to *allostasis*, the idea that the body
anticipates and adjusts rather than returning to a fixed state. If the response to a given training
stress varies this much, adaptations cannot be scheduled weeks in advance. Kiely attributes the
doctrine's persistence to path dependence, a cultural inheritance rather than evidence (grade:
**speculative**). Buckner and colleagues add that Selye studied responses to toxic doses of noxious
agents, a poor model for a squat session [@buckner-2017-gas]†. Defenders reply that the critics
ignore how Selye's model evolved [@cunanan-2018-gas]†, and that block periodization's sequencing is
supported by elite practice [@stone-2021-block-periodization]†. Neither side has longitudinal data.

Two further points sharpen the critique. Periodization has no agreed definition, so its superiority
cannot be tested as defined [@kataoka-2021-periodization-definition]†. And the assumption that
fatigue "accumulates" across sessions and must be dissipated on schedule is itself shaky: the
evidence is "equivocal", and prolonged strength loss may reflect muscle damage rather than
accumulated fatigue [@kataoka-2022-fatigue-accumulation]†.

Coaches have partly absorbed the critique. In an international survey of 106 coaches, 71% called
their planning periodization, and most accepted a season goal and dividing the season into periods.
But only 33% agreed that adaptations occur in fixed timeframes (40% disagreed), and only 10% kept
targets fixed [@anyadike-danes-2023-coaches-planning]. Kiely co-authored the survey, so it comes
from the critics' side. Asian practitioners similarly report hybrid models and adjustment on athlete
feedback [@washif-2025-coaches-periodization]† (grade: **convention**).

The decisive experiment has not been done: a periodized plan against an equally varied, responsive
approach, over a season, in competitive lifters.

## 4.4 Autoregulation

Autoregulation adjusts training to the lifter's state on the day, using effort ratings (RPE, rating
of perceived exertion, or RIR, repetitions in reserve), bar velocity, or rules such as APRE
(autoregulatory progressive resistance exercise, where repetitions achieved in a set to failure set
the next load).

### 4.4.1 Does it beat fixed percentages?

The syntheses disagree, and the split tracks their methods.

- Hickmott and colleagues (15 studies, trained participants) found similar 1RM gains: mean
  difference 2.07 kg (−0.32 to 4.46), SMD 0.21 [@hickmott-2022-autoregulation]. It pooled 247
  comparisons from 133 participants with a fixed-effect model, so even this interval is too
  narrow, and it included adolescents.
- Zhang and colleagues found a clear advantage, SMD 0.64 (0.43–0.85), but only APRE separated, and
  that rested on non-randomised studies pooled as independent outcomes
  [@zhang-2021-autoregulation].
- A 2025 network meta-analysis ranked APRE first [@huang-2025-autoregulation-nma]. Its own
  discussion attributes the advantage to APRE's sets to failure, which confound method with effort
  and volume.
- The ACSM panel judged the effect "cannot be determined" [@currier-2026-acsm-position-stand].

Summary: no clear difference, with the positive estimates coming from APRE-to-failure protocols
(grade: **contested**).

### 4.4.2 Why it sometimes wins: dose

The two trials in trained lifters that favoured autoregulation suggest a mechanism. In Helms 2018
(21 trained men, 8 weeks), lifters choosing loads by RPE selected heavier bench loads, 84.1% vs 78.7%
1RM, and the RPE group's squat advantage (+17.1 vs +13.9 kg, ES 0.50) came at similar average loads
but higher effort (RPE 7.2 vs 6.5) [@helms-2018-rpe-percentage]. In Graham and Cleather 2021 (31
trained men, 12 weeks), the RIR group trained heavier (front squat 83.2% vs 80.4% 1RM) and gained
more (front squat +11.7% vs +8.3%) [@graham-2021-rir-autoregulation].

Two things plausibly produce this. Percentages of a *pre-training* 1RM become relatively lighter as
strength rises, so fixed loading under-doses late in a block. And the same percentage feels
different to different people: a fixed %1RM can place individuals at widely different distances
from failure [@pelland-2022-proximity-methods]†. Autoregulation lets load climb with strength and
equalises effort. The usual rationale, matching training to daily readiness, has never been
isolated experimentally (grade: **promising** that dose differed; **speculative** for the
mechanism).

### 4.4.3 Velocity-based training

Velocity-based training (VBT) prescribes load from bar speed. Against percentage training it shows
at most a small, statistically unreliable strength advantage: SMD 0.21 (−0.01 to 0.43), GRADE
moderate, with small advantages for jump (0.27) and change of direction (0.45) [@wang-2026-vbt];
an earlier review found SMD 0.06 (−0.51 to 0.63)† [@orange-2022-vbt] (grade: **promising**).

*Velocity-loss* thresholds end a set once speed has fallen by a set percentage, and so govern
proximity to failure. Higher thresholds increase hypertrophy (b = 0.006 per percentage point of
velocity loss) and slightly impair jump performance (b = −0.040), while across 0–40% the threshold
has little effect on strength [@jukic-2023-velocity-loss]. A small advantage for thresholds of 25%
or less appears only in studies that added other training [@hickmott-2022-autoregulation]. A 2026
bench trial (n = 158) found 25% beat 0% and 15%, so very low thresholds may under-dose strength
[@pareja-blanco-2026-intensity-velocity-loss]†. This fits Chapter 3: strength is insensitive to
proximity to failure across a wide range [@robinson-2024-proximity-failure]. The velocity-loss
literature comes from a few laboratories, mostly Smith-machine work in men (grade: **promising**
for hypertrophy and jump; **contested** for strength).

### 4.4.4 How accurate are effort ratings?

RIR-based training only works if lifters can judge how many repetitions they have left. Pooled
across 12 studies, lifters underpredict repetitions to failure by about one, 0.95 (0.17–1.73), but
heterogeneity is extreme (I² = 97.9%) [@halperin-2022-rir-accuracy]. Accuracy is better in sets of
12 or fewer repetitions to failure, slightly better closer to failure and in later sets, and does
not depend on training status. Trained lifters called 1–3 RIR on the bench press at 75% 1RM to
within about 0.65 repetitions† [@refalo-2024-rir-accuracy-bench]. In long sets the error is large:
about 2 repetitions at "RPE 9" and 5 at "RPE 5" in 16-repetition squat sets†
[@zourdos-2021-rir-accuracy]. First sets are underpredicted even at 80% 1RM†
[@mansfield-2020-rir-accuracy].

RIR is probably adequate for the heavy, low-repetition work of strength sport, but direct data
above about 80% 1RM are sparse (grade: **promising** for the pooled estimates; **speculative** above
85% 1RM).

## 4.5 Deloads

A Delphi panel of strength and physique coaches defined a deload as "a period of reduced training
stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance
preparedness for subsequent training" [@bell-2023-deload-delphi]. Unlike a taper, it is not meant
to raise performance and can sit anywhere in a plan. Among surveyed athletes who deload (about 88%
of respondents), deloads last 6.4 days every 5.6 weeks, are mostly pre-planned, and cut sets,
repetitions, load and effort [@rogerson-2024-deload-survey] (grade: **convention**).

The experimental evidence is very thin:

- In 39 trained adults on a high-volume program to failure, a one-week *complete break* at week 5
  did not change hypertrophy, power or endurance, and somewhat reduced lower-body strength gains
  (Smith squat −4.5 kg, credible interval −10.4 to 2.8; posterior probability 0.85 favouring
  continuous training) [@coleman-2024-deload]. The "deload" was cessation, not the reduced week
  athletes use, and the study was funded by a commercial coaching company that had a role in its
  design.
- In 19 untrained men, reduced-volume weeks at weeks 4 and 8 gave the same hypertrophy and 10RM
  gains as continuous training [@pancar-2026-deload].
- Very small doses maintain strength for months [@spiering-2021-maintenance]†, so a reduced-volume
  week is unlikely to cost adaptations.

The likely value of deloads is psychological and logistic (adherence, aches, life stress), which
nobody has tested (grade: **promising** for the trial results; **speculative** for any benefit).

## 4.6 Tapering and peaking

Peaking is the planning element on which practice and evidence converge. The evidence-based
recommendation for powerlifting is to cut volume by roughly 30–70% (smaller cuts of 30–50% looked
better), keep intensity high (at least 85% 1RM, or lower it in the final days), taper over 1–2 weeks
with either a step or an exponential reduction, then stop training for 2–7 days
[@travis-2020-taper-review]. Strength was maintained or improved after up to 7 days of cessation
and fell by 1–4% after longer breaks. The experimental base is two powerlifting taper studies with
33 participants. Endurance research offers a firmer anchor: about two weeks, volume cut 41–60%,
intensity and frequency kept [@bosquet-2007-taper]†. Its transfer to strength is inference.

Fatigue decays faster than fitness. Cutting volume while keeping heavy loads removes fatigue while
preserving the heavy-load neural stimulus. Trials of the final days off are small and their effects
trivial: in 12 lifters, two days off raised bench and total, and four days off raised deadlift but
lowered bench, all by about g = 0.1† [@burke-2023-cessation].

Athletes do much the same. Across seven surveys and interview studies, tapering is reported by 78–99%
of strength-sport athletes (powerlifting figures come from samples of tapering lifters). Athletes
mostly use 5–9-day step tapers, cut volume by about 40–45%, stop 1.5–4 days out, and train the
deadlift heavy last (6–8 days out), then the squat (5–6.5) and bench (3–5)
[@winwood-2026-taper-sr] (grade: **promising** for the parameters; **convention** for lift-specific
timing).

## 4.7 Critical appraisal

- **Duration.** No trial lasts more than about six months. Periodization's claims concern seasons
  and careers.
- **Populations.** Most participants are young men; "trained" often means 1–3 years' experience.
  Several syntheses used here include adolescents (Hickmott, Huang, Zhang 2026), untrained people
  (Grgic, Williams) or older people (Zhang 2026). Women, masters lifters and strongman athletes are
  barely studied.
- **Comparators.** Periodization has been tested only against monotony.
- **Test specificity.** Programs that rehearse the 1RM test are rewarded for it.
- **Statistics.** Small samples, dependent effects pooled as independent, and fixed-effect models
  produce intervals that are too narrow.
- **Survey bias.** Some prevalence figures come from samples selected for using the practice.
- **Read level.** Several key numbers, including all of Moesgaard 2022, were read only in the
  abstract.

## 4.8 Open questions

- Does a pre-planned periodized season beat an equally varied, responsive one in competitive
  lifters?
- Does autoregulation help beyond getting the dose right? This needs a trial matching load and
  effort while varying only the decision rule.
- Do reduced-load deloads help or cost trained lifters, and should they be scheduled or triggered?
- How accurate are RIR ratings for heavy singles, deadlifts and strongman implements, and in women?
- Taper trials with competition 1RM as the outcome and more than 20 participants.

## 4.9 Practical implications

- Prioritise content over sequence: heavy, specific practice of the target lifts, loads that rise
  with strength, hard sets, and enough variation to avoid monotony (**promising**).
- Treat linear, undulating and block as interchangeable tools; choose for adherence and logistics
  (**promising** for linear vs undulating; **speculative** for block).
- Plan in short horizons: a coarse season plan anchored to competitions, programmed in 2–6-week
  blocks and adjusted from training data (**convention**, consistent with the evidence).
- Use RPE/RIR or velocity to keep load and effort tracking strength, rather than fixed percentages
  of an old 1RM (**promising**).
- Deload if it helps adherence or recovery, reducing volume rather than stopping; do not expect it
  to improve adaptation (**speculative**).
- Peak with a 1–2-week taper, cutting volume about 30–50% while keeping loads heavy, then rest 2–4
  days; train the deadlift heavy earliest (**promising** for the parameters; **convention** for the
  timing).

## 4.10 Summary

Arranging training matters less than its content. Periodized programs beat constant ones by a small
margin, which mostly reflects variation and test practice; no named model reliably beats another;
and the theory behind periodization rests on outdated stress science. Autoregulation matches or
slightly beats fixed loading, most plausibly because it keeps load and effort in step with strength.
Tapering is well supported by practice and modestly by evidence; deloads are mostly convention.
