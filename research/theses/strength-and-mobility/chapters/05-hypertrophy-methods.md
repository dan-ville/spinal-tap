# Chapter 5. Hypertrophy: mechanisms, muscle length and contraction type

*Light first draft.*

## Abstract

Chapter 3 established how much training grows muscle. This chapter asks how growth happens and
which features of an exercise change it: the mechanism, muscle length and range of motion,
contraction type and special methods, and where growth occurs and how it is measured. The argument is that mechanical tension on recruited fibres is the stimulus. Hormones,
metabolites and damage are at most indirect contributors. Muscle length matters in a narrower way
than social media suggests. Avoiding the lengthened range clearly costs growth. Deliberately
emphasising it adds a small amount, mostly in biarticular muscles and distal regions, and so far
nothing measurable in trained lifters. Special methods change shape, strength or power more than size.
Exercise selection decides *where* muscle grows more than *how much*.

## How to read the numbers in this chapter

Citations are evidence-record keys such as [@maeo-2021]. **Unmarked numbers** were read in the full
text of the source. A **dagger (†)** marks a number read only in the abstract. Grades:
**established**, **promising**, **contested**, **speculative** and **convention** (practitioner
practice with no direct data). Intervals are 95% confidence intervals unless stated otherwise;
Bayesian studies report credible intervals (CrI) and Bayes factors (BF, where values below 1 favour
no difference).

One caveat runs through the whole chapter. Almost every mechanistic, muscle-length and
exercise-selection trial measured with MRI used **untrained** young adults, usually for 8–12 weeks,
often training one limb per condition. Eleven of the 12 studies in the main muscle-length
meta-analysis were in untrained people [@varovic-2025-muscle-length]. The trials in trained lifters
are fewer, shorter and mostly use ultrasound. Every recommendation for trained lifters is an
extrapolation, and the text says so where it matters.

## 5.1 What makes muscle grow

**Mechanical loading is the primary stimulus** (established). The leading model runs as follows.
Tension on the fibres of recruited motor units is sensed by mechanosensors. It is transduced,
largely through the signalling hub mTORC1, into a rise in muscle protein synthesis, more ribosomes
(greater capacity to make protein) and new nuclei donated by satellite cells, the muscle's resident
stem cells [@wackerhage-2019] [@roberts-2023-mechanisms]. A 2026 review reaches the same conclusion:
loading is the driver and dietary protein a minor supporting factor [@greyvenstein-2026]. The
sensors are not identified in humans; candidates such as the elastic protein titin are
**speculative**.

**Tension on fibres, not load on the bar.** From about 30% of one-repetition maximum (1RM) upward,
the load matters little when sets are taken close to failure. In 49 trained men, sets of 20–25
repetitions at ~30–50% 1RM to failure grew muscle as much as heavier sets† [@morton-2016-load-trained].
This is established in untrained people and promising in trained people. The usual explanation is fatigue. As a
light set approaches failure, higher-threshold motor units are recruited, and as their fibres slow
down they experience high tension (the force–velocity relationship). This explanation is
**promising**, not settled. When sets went to failure, fibre glycogen use and anabolic signalling
did not differ between heavy and light loads† [@morton-2019-fibre-activation]. But in trained men,
heavy contractions still used larger motor units throughout, and full recruitment at low loads "may
be dependent on the subject"† [@muddle-2018-motor-units].

There is a floor. Sets at 20% 1RM *taken to failure* grew about half as much as heavier sets
(vastus lateralis +8.9% vs +19.5–20.5%†) [@lasevicius-2018-intensity]. And at 30% 1RM, effort
matters. In 144 untrained adults, light sets stopped well short of failure grew the elbow flexors
0.06 cm, against 0.17 cm for sets to failure [@kataoka-2026]. Chapter 3 covers load and effort in
detail.

**What growth is made of.** Whether fibres grow mainly through bigger myofibrils (the contractile
bundles inside a fibre), more myofibrils, or both is unresolved [@jorgenson-2020]. A cross-sectional
comparison of long-term trained and untrained men found biceps 70% larger, fibres 29% larger, an
estimated 34% more fibres, 105% more myofibrils in total and 7% tighter myosin packing
[@maeo-2024-trained-fibers]. Fibre and myofibril numbers were estimated by division, not counted,
and the trained men may differ for reasons other than training. Still, the picture is of denser
contractile tissue, not diluted tissue.

## 5.2 Hypotheses the evidence has weakened

| Older idea | What weakened it | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Acute, exercise-induced rises in growth hormone, IGF-1 and testosterone drive growth | An arm trained in a high-hormone milieu grew no more than one trained in a low-hormone milieu (+10% vs +12%†; P = 0.25) [@west-2010]. Acute hormone responses did not predict gains in trained men [@morton-2016-load-trained] | established (for transient rises within the physiological range; chronic androgen status is a separate question) |
| Metabolic stress (lactate, acidity, cell swelling) is an independent anabolic signal | Supporting protocols never separated metabolites from contraction; the only human test of post-exercise metabolite pooling found no added growth [@dankel-2017-metabolites] | promising that metabolites are not independently anabolic; speculative that they work by forcing recruitment |
| Muscle damage drives growth | In the first sessions, when damage peaks, protein synthesis goes to repair and does not predict growth; only from week 3 onward does it track hypertrophy (r ≈ 0.9†, n = 10) [@damas-2016-myops] | promising |
| Tension, damage and metabolic stress are co-equal drivers | The original three-factor model [@schoenfeld-2010-mechanisms] is now cited mostly as history; its authors have moved to a tension-centred view | contested as originally stated |
| Sarcoplasmic hypertrophy (growth of the fluid and non-contractile fraction) is a major mode of growth | In 15 high responders, very high-volume training raised fibre area 23%† while actin and myosin concentration fell ~30%†, with borderline statistics (P ≈ 0.05) [@haun-2019]; long-term trained muscle shows more myofibrils, not dilution [@maeo-2024-trained-fibers] | contested; if it occurs, likely transient |

The sarcoplasmic dispute (a myth to one group [@van-every-2025-hypertrophy-mechanisms], plausible
but hard to measure to another [@roberts-2020-sarcoplasmic]) is partly about measurement (section
5.8).

## 5.3 Myonuclei and muscle memory

Each muscle nucleus serves a limited volume of cytoplasm, its *myonuclear domain*. The classic theory
held that a fibre could grow about 26% before needing new nuclei. A meta-analysis of 27 studies and
903 participants found nuclei added even with ≤10% fibre growth, more consistently above ~22%†
[@conceicao-2018] (promising). The domain is flexible rather than a hard ceiling.

*Muscle memory* is the observation that lost muscle is regained faster than it was first built. That
it happens in practice is **convention**, strongly held by coaches. Its cellular basis is
**contested**. One proposal is that nuclei gained in training are kept during detraining and speed
regrowth. In 12 untrained participants, type 2 fibre nuclei rose 33%† and stayed raised through 16
weeks off, but the regrowth on retraining was not clearly faster [@cumming-2024]. In mice,
such nuclei were lost within six months in two of three muscles [@murach-2020-muscle-memory]. An
epigenetic memory, with genes that stay more readily switched on after detraining, is the main
alternative [@seaborne-2018] (speculative). Strength outlasts size: after detraining,
strength "remained elevated (~60%)"† when size had returned to baseline, consistent with a neural
memory [@psilander-2019].

## 5.4 Muscle length and range of motion

### 5.4.1 Three different comparisons

"Long-length training" covers three different manipulations: (a) **lengthened partials**, partial
repetitions in the stretched part of the range; (b) **exercise or joint position** that lengthens a
muscle crossing two joints (seated vs prone leg curl, overhead vs neutral triceps work, standing vs
seated calf raise); and (c) **isometrics at long lengths**. Studies also compare three kinds of
condition: long vs short partials, long partials vs full range of motion (ROM), and long vs short
positions over a full range. A win for "long over short" does **not** imply that lengthened partials
beat full ROM. Mixing these comparisons explains most apparent disagreements.

### 5.4.2 What the meta-analyses show

- **Full vs partial ROM.** In a Bayesian meta-analysis of 23 studies, full and partial ROM differed
  trivially for muscle size, SMD 0.04 (−0.17 to 0.25) [@wolf-2023-rom]. In an exploratory subgroup,
  long partials slightly beat full ROM (SMD −0.28, −0.81 to 0.16), while short partials vs full ROM
  was itself small and imprecise (0.08, −0.24 to 0.42). Nineteen of the 23 studies used short-length
  partials. Strength was ROM-specific.
- **Short partials lose.** Individual trials consistently find that partials confined to short
  lengths grow less than long partials, and less than full ROM in some (e.g. final-range knee
  extensions grew no more than control at mid-thigh) [@pedrosa-2022] [@pedrosa-2025]
  [@kassiano-2023-rom-review] (promising).
- **Long vs short mean length.** Pooling 12 studies of any length manipulation, longer mean lengths
  gave SMD 0.05†, 0.07† and 0.09† at proximal, mid and distal sites [@varovic-2025-muscle-length].
  Most of each estimate lay inside the pre-registered band of no practical importance, but this is
  **per 21.8% difference in muscle length**, the average contrast in the studies. A preprint version
  that scaled the same model to a 50% contrast gave about SMD 0.20 distally.
- **Long vs short partials only.** A 2026 meta-analysis of 8 studies, mostly of the vastus lateralis,
  found a moderate advantage for long partials: ES 0.28† overall and 0.43† distally [@strey-2026]
  (full text embargoed).

The analyses agree on direction and distal bias, not size. A defensible reading
is that **avoiding the lengthened range costs growth** (promising), and that **among conditions
that reach long lengths, the extra benefit of emphasising them is small** and most visible distally
and in biarticular muscles (contested). That supports avoiding short-range bias, not replacing
full ROM with lengthened partials.

### 5.4.3 Biarticular muscles: the strongest trials

The clearest evidence comes from muscles that cross two joints, where one joint's position sets the
muscle's length.

- **Seated vs prone leg curl** (20 untrained, 12 weeks, MRI). Seated curls, with the hip flexed,
  grew the hamstrings more (+14.1% vs +9.3%). The effect appeared in the biarticular heads, while
  the short head of biceps femoris, which crosses only the knee, grew equally (+10% vs +9%)
  [@maeo-2021]. This internal control is the best single argument that length itself causes the
  difference.
- **Overhead vs neutral triceps extension.** The long head grew more (+28.5% vs +19.6%†), but so did
  the single-joint heads (+14.6% vs +10.5%†), despite lighter loads [@maeo-2023]. Length alone does
  not explain that.
- **Standing vs seated calf raise.** The gastrocnemius grew +9–12% standing against +0.6–1.7% seated
  (the latter not significant) [@kinoshita-2023]. With the knee bent, the gastrocnemius is both
  short and nearly unable to produce force, so length and tension cannot be separated here.
- **Incline vs preacher curl.** Incline curls grew the biceps slightly more and preacher curls the
  brachialis and brachioradialis more. Total elbow-flexor growth was the same (+17.6% vs +19.2%†)
  [@kobayashi-2026]. Position redistributed growth among synergists without changing the total.

### 5.4.4 Trained lifters

Every head-to-head trial in trained lifters so far finds lengthened partials roughly equivalent to
full ROM (promising):

- 30 trained participants, 8 weeks, ultrasound: elbow-flexor and extensor thickness differed by
  −0.23 to 0.82 mm, with Bayes factors of 0.16–0.39 favouring no difference
  [@wolf-2025-lengthened-partials].
- 13 trained participants, preacher curl: a trivial distal advantage for partials (SMD 0.10, BF
  4.87), and slightly more 1RM gain with full ROM [@havers-2025].
- 23 lifters with 7 years' training: leg press to ~100° knee flexion grew the quadriceps as much as
  maximal depth (~154°)† [@larsen-2025-leg-press]. Past the point where the vasti are already long,
  extra depth added nothing measurable.
- Long-length isometrics roughly matched full-ROM knee extensions over 6 weeks, with a possible
  proximal advantage† [@varovic-2025-isometric].

The large effects come from untrained people and short-length comparators.

### 5.4.5 Why length might matter

- **Passive tension.** On the long, descending limb of the length–tension curve, titin and other
  elastic structures add passive force to the active force. In mice, stretch-induced growth scaled
  with titin stiffness† [@van-der-pijl-2018], and titin-linked proteins sit upstream of mTORC1 in
  lengthwise growth† [@van-der-pijl-2026] (speculative for humans) [@pedrosa-2026-review].
- **Stretch alone is a weak growth signal.** Prolonged static stretching produces small growth:
  d = 0.20 overall across 42 studies, rising slightly with very long durations
  [@warneke-2024-stretch] (promising).
- **Lengthwise (serial) growth.** Long-length and eccentric training increase ultrasound-estimated
  fascicle length more than short-length or concentric training [@mcmahon-2026] [@franchi-2014]
  (promising; mostly extrapolated). No study has estimated serial sarcomere number after long- vs
  short-length training [@wolf-2026-longitudinal], and the one human estimate, after Nordic
  training, found longer sarcomeres, not more of them† [@pincheira-2022-sarcomere] (speculative).
- **Diminishing returns.** If sarcomere addition shifts the length–tension curve, the advantage
  may shrink with training age [@pedrosa-2026-review] (speculative).

## 5.5 Contraction type and special methods

**Eccentric vs concentric.** Per repetition at similar relative effort, lowering (eccentric) and
lifting (concentric) work grow muscle similarly. The largest meta-analysis found SMD 0.29†
(−0.13 to 0.70), I² 84%, at very low certainty; the subgroups favouring eccentric work (short
trials, thickness outcomes) are those most exposed to early swelling [@da-silva-2025-ecc-con]
[@schoenfeld-2017-ecc-con] (contested). Any extra benefit comes from the heavier loads eccentric
work allows [@roig-2009]. What eccentric work changes is shape. In a trial with 6 per group, it
lengthened fascicles more (+12% vs +5%†) and grew the distal region more, while concentric work
increased pennation angle [@franchi-2014] (promising). Repetition tempo between about 1 and 3.5
seconds barely matters (difference 0.09†, −0.04 to 0.22) [@enes-2025-tempo]; slow eccentrics are not
a hypertrophy lever on their own.

**Accentuated eccentric loading (AEL)**, a heavier load for the lowering phase than for the lift, is
a strength tool. In 28 strength-trained men, AEL raised isometric torque more (+18% vs +1%) and
improved voluntary activation, but muscle growth was similar to traditional training [@walker-2016].
Reviews agree that its hypertrophy does not consistently exceed traditional training
[@zhong-2025-ael] [@cowley-2026-advanced-nma] (promising).

**Flywheel (isoinertial) training** stores energy in a spinning disc during the lift and returns it
on the way down. It grows muscle about as well as conventional training; its distinctive benefit is
power and jumping (power SMD 0.47†, 0.10 to 0.84) [@hu-2024-flywheel] (promising). Claims of superior growth rest on
small, unmatched comparisons [@maroto-izquierdo-2017] and pre-post effect sizes [@petre-2018].

**Isometrics.** Holds at long muscle lengths grew more muscle per week than holds at short lengths
(0.86–1.69%† vs 0.08–0.83%†) and transferred better to dynamic performance [@oranchuk-2019]
(promising). Suggested parameters are about 70–75% of maximal effort in 3–30-second holds
[@lum-2019-isometric] (convention).

## 5.6 Blood-flow restriction

Blood-flow-restriction (BFR) training uses a cuff around the top of an arm or leg to restrict venous outflow
during light-load exercise.

- **Light-load BFR grows muscle like heavy loads but builds less strength** (established). Difference
  in hypertrophy against heavy loads: ES −0.07 (−0.21 to 0.07), I² 0%; strength −0.34 (−0.52 to
  −0.16) [@geng-2024]. An umbrella review of 47 meta-analyses found the same pattern, with every
  included review of low-to-moderate quality [@oborn-2025]. The strength gap reflects specificity:
  no practice with heavy loads.
- **Trained people.** Geng's trained subgroup favoured BFR for hypertrophy, but from only three
  effects [@geng-2024] (speculative). In national-level powerlifters, two short BFR blocks grew the
  vastus lateralis +7.7% vs +0.5%† and added nuclei to type I fibres [@bjornsen-2019] (promising).
- **Mechanism.** The cuff speeds fatigue, which forces recruitment of higher-threshold units at
  20–40% 1RM. In untrained adults, light submaximal sets with BFR grew muscle nearly as much as light
  sets to failure without a cuff (0.14 vs 0.17 cm), and both beat light submaximal sets without BFR
  (0.06 cm) [@kataoka-2026] (promising). BFR is one route to the same high-recruitment state that
  failure produces.
- **Adding BFR to heavy sets adds nothing** (size g 0.08†, very low certainty) [@mckee-2026].
- **Prescription.** 20–40% 1RM, commonly 30-15-15-15 repetitions with short rests, at 40–80% of
  individually measured occlusion pressure [@patterson-2019] (convention).

## 5.7 Growth is regional: exercise selection

Growth differs along a muscle and between synergists, so single-site measures misrepresent training
effects [@nunes-2024-sites] (established). Exercise selection decides *where* muscle grows:

- **Rectus femoris.** Knee extensions grew it +13.2% against +1.1% for the leg press, while the vasti
  grew similarly [@kinoshita-2026]; squats did not grow it at all [@kubo-2019] [@earp-2015]. In the
  squat and leg press it shortens at the knee as it lengthens at the hip, so it works near constant
  length at low relative force.
- **Gluteus maximus and adductors.** Full squats grew them more than half squats (+6.7% vs +2.2%† for
  the gluteus maximus) [@kubo-2019], and the leg press grew the gluteus maximus +15.4% where knee
  extensions did not [@kinoshita-2026]. But deep hip flexion is not required for the glutes: hip
  thrusts grew them as much as squats, while squats grew the quadriceps and adductors more
  [@plotkin-2023-hip-thrust] (promising).
- **Hamstrings** respond to hip position (section 5.4.3). Hip-flexed eccentric work grew the
  hip-extending heads, Nordics the others [@maeo-2024-nordic].
- **Calves.** The gastrocnemius needs a straight knee [@kinoshita-2023], and partials from a stretched
  ankle grew its medial head more than full ROM (+15.2% vs +6.7%†) [@kassiano-2023-calf].
- **Resistance profile with range matched.** Cable and dumbbell lateral raises grew the deltoid
  equally in trained lifters† [@larsen-2025-lateral-raise].

Muscle activity (EMG) is an unreliable guide: it predicted growth in the knee-extension trial but not
the hip-thrust trial.

## 5.8 Measuring hypertrophy

MRI of muscle volume or of cross-sections at several sites is the reference standard. Most of the
decisive trials above used it. The other methods disagree with it and with each other, so
"hypertrophy" is not one construct [@haun-2019-measurement] (established):

- **Ultrasound thickness** at mid-thigh tracked MRI cross-section change (r = 0.69†) but not volume
  change (r = 0.33†, n = 9) [@franchi-2018-mt]. Extended-field ultrasound agrees better
  [@fukumitsu-2026]. Single-site thickness trials deserve less weight than MRI trials.
- **DXA lean mass** includes water, glycogen and connective tissue. Its change tracked MRI only
  moderately (r = 0.58†); biopsy fibre-area change did not track it at all (r = −0.57†, P = 0.054,
  n = 12) [@ruple-2022-imaging] (promising).
- **Swelling.** Early gains *may* include oedema. One ultrasound study found raised echo intensity at
  week 3 [@damas-2016], but an MRI study found no fluid signal change while muscle area rose from week
  4 [@maeo-2018] (promising, not universal).

## 5.9 Critical appraisal

- **Training status.** The mechanistic and exercise-selection evidence is almost entirely from
  untrained adults. Trials in trained lifters are few, short (6–8 weeks) and mostly ultrasound-based.
- **Duration.** Nearly all trials last 6–12 weeks. Whether long-length advantages persist or fade is
  untested.
- **Coverage.** Thigh, calf and arm muscles dominate. Chest, back, shoulders and trunk, and the
  multi-joint lifts, are barely studied for length effects.
- **Design.** One-limb-per-condition designs may shrink differences (training one limb affects the
  other); sites chosen after the fact may inflate them.
- **Read level.** Many numbers here, including the Strey and Varovic meta-analyses, Maeo 2023 and
  Kubo 2019, were read only in abstracts.

## 5.10 Open questions

- Do long-length effects hold in well-trained lifters over more than 12 weeks, measured with MRI?
- Do lengthened partials *added* after full-ROM sets beat extra full-ROM sets in trained people?
- Is the lengthwise growth from long-length and eccentric work serial sarcomere addition?
- Is BFR's apparent advantage in trained people real, or an artefact of three effect sizes?
- Do retained myonuclei speed regrowth in humans by a meaningful amount?

## 5.11 Practical implications

- Take sets close to failure with any load from about 30% 1RM upward; avoid very light loads
  (~20% 1RM) as the main stimulus (**established** in untrained people, **promising** in trained).
- Train through the lengthened range; do not bias sets toward the shortened range (**promising**).
- Use full ROM by default. Lengthened partials are a reasonable option, for example after full-ROM
  sets or when load must be lighter, but not a superior one (**promising** for equivalence in trained
  lifters).
- Choose exercises by where you want growth: knee extensions for the rectus femoris, seated or
  hip-flexed curls for the hamstrings, straight-knee calf raises for the gastrocnemius, overhead work
  for the triceps long head (**promising**, from untrained trials).
- Treat AEL as a strength tool and flywheel training as a power tool, not as hypertrophy upgrades
  (**promising**).
- Use light-load BFR when heavy loading is impractical, for example during a lighter week; do not add
  it to heavy sets (**promising**).
- Do not chase hypertrophy through hormones, "the pump" or soreness (**promising**).
- When judging studies, or your own progress, distrust early, single-site or DXA-only changes
  (**promising**).

## 5.12 Summary

Muscle grows in response to tension on recruited fibres. Load matters little above a floor, provided
effort is high; hormones, metabolites and damage are not independent drivers. Training through the
stretched range matters, mainly because avoiding it costs growth. Emphasising it adds a little in
untrained people, mostly in biarticular muscles and distal regions, and nothing measurable yet in
trained lifters. Special methods each have a niche, but none clearly beats conventional training for size.
Exercise selection decides where muscle grows, and the measurement method shapes what studies show.
