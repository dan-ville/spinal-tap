# Chapter 6. Flexibility and range of motion

*Light first draft.*

## Abstract

This chapter asks what limits how far a joint moves, how stretching and resistance training change
it, and what stretching does to strength, muscle size and injury risk. Chronic stretching reliably
increases passive range of motion (ROM), by about one standard deviation over a few weeks. Most of
that gain is a raised tolerance for the stretch sensation; a smaller part is reduced muscle
stiffness. Whether stretching lengthens muscle fascicles is disputed. Static and PNF stretching
beat dynamic stretching for chronic gains; frequency and intensity do not matter, and the evidence
on volume is inconsistent. Full-range resistance training improves ROM about as much as stretching,
although the comparisons are imprecise. Long static stretches before maximal efforts cost a little
isolated strength; short ones inside a warm-up cost nothing measurable. Stretching builds strength
slightly and muscle barely, and shows no detectable effect on injury risk.

## How to read the numbers in this chapter

Citations are evidence-record keys such as [@ingram-2024-stretching-dose]. **Unmarked numbers** were
read in the full text of the source. A **dagger (†)** marks a number read only in the abstract.
Grades: **established**, **promising**, **contested**, **speculative** and **convention**
(practitioner practice with no direct data). Intervals are 95% confidence intervals unless stated
otherwise. Effect sizes (g, d, SMD) are in standard-deviation units; around 0.2 is small, 0.5
moderate and 0.8 large, although papers label them inconsistently.

Two caveats apply throughout. First, the population: mostly recreationally active or untrained
young men, and mostly two muscle groups. Plantar flexors made up 60% of the studies in the main
mechanism meta-analysis and hamstrings 29% [@ingram-2025-stretching-mechanisms]. No trial studied
chronic ROM in competitive strength athletes. Second, the outcome: "ROM" almost always means
*passive* end range, the angle at which the participant asks to stop. It is partly a sensory measure
by construction.

## 6.1 What limits range of motion

Passive ROM is limited by three things: joint geometry (bone shape, which matters most at the
ankle), the passive resistance of muscle, tendon, fascia, nerve and joint capsule, and the person's
willingness to tolerate the stretch sensation. Plotting the torque needed to move a joint against
its angle gives the *passive torque–angle curve*. A ROM gain can then happen in two ways
[@weppler-2010-extensibility]:

- **Mechanical.** The curve shifts: less torque is needed at the same angle, because the tissue is
  less stiff or longer.
- **Sensory.** The curve is unchanged, but the person follows it further and accepts more torque at
  end range. The torque at the stopping point, the *maximum tolerable passive torque*, is the
  standard index of stretch tolerance.

Muscle activity does not limit slow stretches. Reflex activity is negligible during slow passive
stretching, and the torque decline during a 90-second hold is the same with or without an intact
spinal cord (33% vs 38%†). It is viscoelastic *stress relaxation*, the tissue slowly giving way, not
the muscle letting go [@magnusson-1996-stress-relaxation] (established).

## 6.2 How much stretching improves ROM

**One session** gives a small-to-moderate, short-lived gain: g = 0.63 (0.52 to 0.75; GRADE low)
across 189 studies [@ingram-2024-stretching-dose], and SMD 0.56 (0.43 to 0.68; GRADE moderate) in a
second meta-analysis [@behm-2023-acute-rom] (established). A narrative review reports that the gain
typically lasts under 30 minutes† [@behm-2016-acute-stretching]. Technique does not matter for the
acute gain (p = 0.72) [@behm-2023-acute-rom], and other warm-up activities such as foam rolling,
cycling or light lifting give similar acute gains [@warneke-2025-delphi-consensus].

**Weeks of stretching** give a large gain: g = 0.96 (0.84 to 1.09; GRADE moderate) for static
stretching against passive controls [@ingram-2024-stretching-dose], and SMD 1.00 (0.84 to 1.17)
across all techniques, with evidence of reporting bias [@konrad-2023-chronic-rom] (established). In
degrees, hamstring stretching adds about 12° to a straight-leg raise (9.6 to 14.5)†
[@medeiros-2016-hamstring-static]. Ankle gains are smaller, probably because bone limits the range.

Who gains most is mostly unclear. People with less baseline flexibility gain more (g = −0.33,
−0.59 to −0.08) [@ingram-2024-stretching-dose]. Training status did not moderate the effect in
either large meta-analysis, and women gained more in one but not the other.

## 6.3 Why range improves: tolerance and tissue

The classic view is the **sensory theory**: short programmes raise ROM by raising tolerance, with
little change in the tissue. Early trials fit it. After four weeks, the angle at maximal tolerated
torque rose 8°† while extensibility at a standard torque did not change (−1°†)
[@folpp-2006-stretch-tolerance]. After six weeks, dorsiflexion rose from 30.9° to 36.3°† with no
change in passive torque, fascicle length or muscle or tendon stiffness†
[@konrad-2014-static-stretching]. A meta-analysis of programmes of 3–8 weeks concluded that
adaptations "seem to mostly occur at a sensory level"† [@freitas-2018-chronic-stretching-mtu].

Not every early trial fits, though. In one, muscle stiffness fell 18% over three weeks
[@blazevich-2014-plantar-flexor-stretch]; another attributed most of a 31%† gain to reduced passive
stiffness [@guissard-2004-stretch-training].

The current picture is **tolerance plus some tissue change** (promising for tolerance as the largest
contributor; speculative for which tissue changes). A 2025 meta-analysis of 65 studies found that
chronic stretching raised tolerable torque (g = 0.74, 0.38 to 1.10; GRADE very low) and slightly
reduced *overall* stiffness (g = 0.37, 0.18 to 0.56; GRADE low)
[@ingram-2025-stretching-mechanisms]. Both were associated with ROM gains, though among stiffness
measures only passive torque at a given angle was. A re-analysis with models that handle multiple
effects per study found reduced *muscle* stiffness (−0.38) but no change in tendon stiffness (0.04)
[@warneke-2024-passive-properties-recalculation]. Tendon, which is mostly collagen, is plausibly the
less compliant element. One caution: end ROM is *defined* at the tolerable torque, so the link
between ROM and tolerance is partly circular [@lima-2026-rom-mechanisms-review].

**Fascicle length is contested.** One meta-analysis found that stretching lengthened fascicles
slightly at rest (SMD 0.17, 0.01 to 0.33), but only with high volume and intensity
[@panidi-2023-fascicle-meta]. Its high-volume programmes also lasted twice as long (10.6 vs 5.1
weeks), so volume and duration are confounded. A larger meta-analysis found no effect (g = 0.07,
−0.25 to 0.26) even though half its studies used at least 90 minutes of stretching, and attributed
the difference to how the first handled several measurements per study
[@ingram-2025-stretching-mechanisms]. The longest trial, six months of daily stretching, found no
fascicle change† [@moltubakk-2021-six-month-stretching]. The strongest positive study was in
adolescent athletes, where growth and sport training confound it [@panidi-2021-adolescent-stretching].

What "tolerance" is physiologically remains unknown. Candidates include reduced sensitivity of pain
endings, central modulation and familiarity with the sensation (speculative). Neural and mechanical
changes also have different time courses: reflex changes reverted within a month of stopping, while
part of the ROM and stiffness change remained† [@guissard-2004-stretch-training].

## 6.4 Modality and dose

**Modality.** In the largest meta-analysis, static stretching (SMD −1.01) and PNF stretching
(contract–relax techniques; −1.28) gave larger chronic gains than dynamic or ballistic stretching
(−0.55, only 8 effects; p = 0.012), with no difference between static and PNF (p = 0.28)
[@konrad-2023-chronic-rom] (promising). The likely reason is time held at end range. Other syntheses
rank static above PNF† [@thomas-2018-stretching-typology] or, at the ankle only, PNF and foam rolling
above static† [@park-2026-ankle-nma], so the static-vs-PNF question is not settled. Passive static
stretching beat active static stretching (0.97 vs 0.59) [@arntz-2023-chronic-stretching-strength].

**Frequency and intensity do not matter** (promising). Weekly frequency had no effect once volume was
accounted for (g = −0.001) [@ingram-2024-stretching-dose], and neither frequency (p = 0.42) nor
intensity (p = 0.54) moderated gains in the largest all-technique analysis
[@konrad-2023-chronic-rom]. Stretching to discomfort is not needed for ROM.

**The evidence on volume is inconsistent** (speculative for any specific dose):

- One meta-analysis found gains stopped rising beyond about **10 minutes per muscle group per week**,
  but this was read by eye from a curve with sparse data at high volumes
  [@ingram-2024-stretching-dose].
- The largest all-technique meta-analysis found no relation with total duration (p = 0.73)
  [@konrad-2023-chronic-rom].
- A third found more time gave more gain [@arntz-2023-chronic-stretching-strength].
- Single trials show 2 minutes per week can work (+3.9 cm sit-and-reach over six weeks)
  [@kaczorowski-2026-low-volume], and that 30 minutes a day was not detectably better than 3
  minutes three times a week in older adults† [@luangpon-2026-older-volume].

An expert consensus recommends static or PNF stretching, 2–3 sets of 30–120 seconds daily, "to
reach the highest possible weekly volume" [@warneke-2025-delphi-consensus] (convention). Several
panel members wrote the reviews the panel relied on.

## 6.5 Resistance training as flexibility training

Resistance training improves ROM moderately against no training: SMD 0.73 (0.55 to 0.91) across 52
studies [@alizadeh-2023-rt-rom], and g = 0.63 (0.48 to 0.79) in a second meta-analysis in which no
study was at low risk of bias [@favro-2025-rt-flexibility] (promising). Body-weight-only programmes
have not been shown to improve ROM (4 studies), plausibly because exercises such as push-ups stop
short of end range. The active ingredient appears to be the range, not the load (speculative).
Trained or active people gained less than sedentary people in one analysis (0.43 vs 1.03)
[@alizadeh-2023-rt-rom], but activity level was not a significant moderator in the other
[@favro-2025-rt-flexibility].

**Against stretching, resistance training has not been shown to differ, but the comparisons are
imprecise** (contested). Pooled differences were ES −0.22 (−0.55 to 0.12), slightly favouring
stretching, across 11 trials [@afonso-2021-strength-vs-stretching], and SMD 0.08 (−0.56 to 0.73)
across 7 [@alizadeh-2023-rt-rom]. Both intervals include differences of about half a standard
deviation either way, so "no significant difference" is not equivalence. Among recent trials, two
small ones found similar gains† [@murakami-2025-ss-vs-rt] [@rosenfeldt-2024-rt-vs-stretch], and one
found a larger shoulder-flexibility gain with 15 minutes of pectoral stretching four days a week than
with resistance training (d = 0.79†) [@wohlann-2024-pec-stretch]. The only significant head-to-head
difference favours stretching. Adding stretching to resistance training did not improve ROM beyond
stretching alone (−0.001, 4 studies) [@alizadeh-2023-rt-rom].

The expert view is that full-range resistance training improves ROM "to at least a similar degree,
especially if a focus is given to the eccentric phase" [@warneke-2025-delphi-consensus] (convention).
No trial has tested whether a lifter who already trains through full range gains more by adding
stretching, or whether ROM gained under load transfers better to loaded positions than ROM gained by
stretching.

## 6.6 Stretching before lifting

**Long static stretches cost a little isolated strength** (promising). A 2024 meta-analysis of 83
studies, restricted to controlled designs, found a small loss of maximal strength after static
stretching (ES −0.21, −0.39 to −0.02). The loss was larger after bouts of at least 60 seconds per
muscle (−0.84, −1.32 to −0.37) and not detectable after shorter ones (−0.13)
[@warneke-2024-force-deficit]. Sprint, jump and throw performance were not shown to be impaired. That
paper contains several confidence intervals that do not match their p values, so its point estimates
should be read loosely. An older synthesis of 106 studies found −1.1% for stretches under 30 seconds
and −1.9% for 30–45 seconds, with larger losses only beyond about 60 seconds
[@kay-2012-acute-static-stretch].

**Inside a full warm-up, short stretches cost nothing measurable.** Adding 5- or 30-second static
stretches, or dynamic stretches, to a comprehensive warm-up did not change sprint, jump or agility
in team-sport athletes [@blazevich-2018-full-warmup]. Dynamic stretching does not reduce strength.

**Barbell evidence is thin and mixed.** Short stretching (3 × 15 seconds per muscle) did not change
bench or leg-press 1RM† [@beedle-2008-stretch-1rm], and static or PNF stretching inside a 1RM warm-up
did not change bench 1RM in trained football players† [@molacek-2010-bench-stretch]. But more
extensive passive lower-body stretching added to a warm-up reduced back-squat 1RM by 8.4%† in 17
moderately trained men, who also felt less stable† [@gergley-2013-squat-stretch].

Proposed mechanisms include reduced voluntary activation, a shift of the force–length curve toward
longer lengths and a more compliant muscle† [@behm-2021-stretch-impairment-mechanisms]
(speculative). Part of the deficit after very long stretching may be generic fatigue: calf raises to
failure reduced strength as much as 30 minutes of stretching† [@warneke-2024-rt-force-deficit].
Stretching does not reduce later muscle soreness† [@herbert-2011-stretching-soreness] (established).

The expert consensus is to avoid more than 60 seconds of static stretching per muscle before maximal
or explosive efforts [@warneke-2025-delphi-consensus] (convention).

## 6.7 Can stretching build strength or muscle?

**Strength: small gains** (promising). Chronic static stretching increased strength by d = 0.30
(0.14 to 0.46) across 42 studies [@warneke-2024-stretch] and SMD 0.21 (0.10 to 0.32) across 41
[@arntz-2023-chronic-stretching-strength], with larger effects in sedentary people. Part of this is
probably neural or test-specific. In the first quadriceps study, strength rose at one knee angle but
not another† [@siegel-2026-quadriceps-smh], and the angle that improved was the short one, the
opposite of what lengthwise growth would predict.

**Muscle: trivial to small** (contested). One meta-analysis found d = 0.20 (0.08 to 0.32; GRADE low)
[@warneke-2024-stretch]; another found SMD 0.12, with a prediction interval of −0.23 to 0.47, which
means a new study could plausibly find no effect [@arntz-2024-stretch-hypertrophy]. Programmes over
six weeks gave larger effects. Sessions of 15 minutes or more reached significance while shorter ones
did not, but the difference between them was not significant, so there is no demonstrated dose
threshold. Chapter 5 places this *stretch-mediated hypertrophy* in context: lengthening is a real but
weak growth signal compared with loading.

The trials show the cost. In 69 active adults, an hour a day of calf stretching with an orthosis
matched calf resistance training for isometric strength (+18.0% vs +13.4%), but only resistance
training grew the lateral gastrocnemius significantly (d = 0.61 vs 0.32), at up to seven hours a week
of stretching against 45 minutes of lifting [@warneke-2023-stretch-vs-rt]. MRI shows smaller effects
than ultrasound: in one trial, d = 0.16–0.30† on MRI against 0.53–0.60† on ultrasound
[@warneke-2023-long-lasting-stretch-trained]. A pectoral trial found about an hour a week of
stretching matched resistance training for strength and thickness in active adults†
[@wohlann-2024-pec-stretch], so very long doses may not be needed everywhere.

**In trained lifters, the evidence is close to nil.** The only randomised trial of stretching added
to training in resistance-trained men used 30-second loaded stretches between sets of chest work and
found no added effect on muscle thickness or bench 1RM over 8 weeks†
[@wadhi-2022-interset-stretch-trained]. Long-duration stretching on top of training has been tested
only in a single uncontrolled case study [@homer-2025-bodybuilder-case]. The consensus does not
recommend stretching as a primary strategy for strength or muscle [@warneke-2025-delphi-consensus].

## 6.8 Stretching and injury

Stretching programmes show **no detectable effect on sports-injury risk** (promising; GRADE very
low). The largest synthesis, of 15 studies and 9,002 participants, found OR 0.945 (0.828 to 1.078),
with no effect on muscle, tendon or ligament injuries, and a borderline *increase* in bone injuries
(OR 1.33, 1.00 to 1.78) [@afonso-2026-stretching-injury]. This matches earlier syntheses
(RR 0.96†, OR 0.93†) [@lauersen-2014-injury-prevention] [@thacker-2004-stretching-injury] and a
trial in 1,538 army recruits (HR 0.95†) [@pope-2000-army-stretching]. About 90% of the trials were
at high risk of bias, most participants were men, and acute stretching before activity has never been
tested as injury prevention. Strength training reduced injuries in a separate analysis (RR 0.32†)
[@lauersen-2014-injury-prevention], but that is a comparison across different trials, not head to
head.

## 6.9 Keeping the range

ROM gained is lost slowly and only partly (promising). Across 13 small studies, many in children and
adolescents, 2–6 weeks without stretching reduced ROM somewhat from its trained level (ES −0.41,
−0.73 to −0.09; GRADE low), but it stayed above baseline (0.55, 0.33 to 0.77)
[@konrad-2025-detraining]. Participants who stayed physically active showed no significant loss,
which suggests, but does not show, that ongoing activity preserves gains. By analogy, regular
full-range lifting may maintain the ranges it loads (speculative; untested). The minimum maintenance
dose is unknown.

## 6.10 Critical appraisal

- **Population and muscles.** Untrained or recreationally active young men; plantar flexors and
  hamstrings. Hips, shoulders and the thoracic spine, the ranges that matter in lifting, are barely
  studied.
- **Outcome.** Passive end ROM is bounded by tolerance. Whether passive gains transfer to active,
  loaded positions (squat depth under load, overhead lockout) is untested; Chapter 7 takes this up.
- **Duration.** Only one trial lasted six months.
- **Intensity is unmeasured,** defined by perceived discomfort in nearly every study, which blocks
  dose–response work.
- **One author network.** A small group wrote many of the meta-analyses, the stretch-hypertrophy
  trials and the consensus statement. Their conclusions are mostly sensible, but independent
  replication is thin, and several of their papers contain internal numerical inconsistencies.
- **Read level.** Many mechanism trials, the injury syntheses and all three barbell-1RM trials were
  read only as abstracts.

## 6.11 Open questions

- Does a lifter who already trains through full range gain anything by adding stretching, and at
  which joints?
- Does ROM gained under load transfer better to loaded positions than ROM gained by stretching?
- Do stiffness or fascicle changes accumulate over months or years?
- How do short stretches before heavy squats, benches and deadlifts affect 1RM in competitive lifters?
- Does long-duration stretching add muscle on top of good training in trained people?

## 6.12 Practical implications

- To gain range at a joint, stretch it: static or PNF, held positions, a few minutes per muscle per
  week or more (**established** that it works; **speculative** for any specific dose).
- Discomfort is not required; frequency can follow convenience (**promising**).
- Train through full range. It improves ROM about as well as stretching for many people, although
  stretching may still win at specific joints such as the shoulder (**contested**).
- Before heavy or explosive work, use a dynamic warm-up. Short static stretches (under about 30–60
  seconds per muscle) inside it are fine; avoid long static holds on the working muscles
  (**promising**).
- Do not stretch to build muscle or strength when you can lift instead (**promising**).
- Do not count on stretching to prevent injury (**promising**).
- Gains fade slowly; staying active, including full-range lifting, probably helps keep them
  (**speculative**).

## 6.13 Summary

Stretching reliably increases passive range, mainly by raising tolerance and partly by reducing muscle
stiffness; whether it lengthens fascicles is unresolved. Held stretches beat dynamic ones for lasting
gains, and the dose matters less than once thought. Full-range resistance training improves range
comparably, though the comparisons are imprecise. Long static holds cost a little isolated strength
before maximal efforts; short ones inside a warm-up do not. Stretching builds a little strength,
barely any muscle, and shows no detectable effect on injury risk. Nearly all of this comes from
untrained people and two muscle groups, so its application to trained lifters is inference.
