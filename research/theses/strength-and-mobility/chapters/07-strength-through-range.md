# Chapter 7. Strength through range and practitioner mobility systems

*Light first draft.*

## Abstract

Chapter 6 asked how far joints move. This chapter asks how strong a person is across that range,
and whether loaded training builds range that can be used. It covers four questions: how specific
strength is to the trained range; whether eccentric and long-length training remodel muscle
architecture; whether loaded training improves range of motion (ROM); and what evidence supports
popular mobility systems. Strength gains are specific to the trained range, but training at long
muscle lengths transfers more broadly than training at short lengths. Eccentric and long-length
training lengthen muscle fascicles, by smaller amounts than practitioners quote. Whether that
reflects added sarcomeres in humans is unsettled, and the change fades within weeks of stopping.
Eccentric and full-range resistance training improve ROM. The named mobility systems have almost
no direct trials; their component exercises map onto techniques that do have evidence.

## How to read the numbers in this chapter

Citations are evidence-record keys such as [@andrews-2025-multiscale]. **Unmarked numbers** were read
in the full text of the source. A **dagger (†)** marks a number read only in the abstract. Grades:
**established**, **promising**, **contested**, **speculative** and **convention** (practitioner
practice with no direct data). Intervals are 95% confidence intervals unless stated otherwise.

The population caveat is sharper here than in any other chapter. Every architecture, sarcomere,
ROM and mobility-system study used untrained, recreationally active or older people. The few studies
in resistance-trained participants measured muscle size, strength or muscle activity, never ROM.
"Trained" in the ROM literature usually means people who exercise weekly, not strength athletes. No
claim about trained lifters in this chapter is graded established.

## 7.1 What "mobility" should mean

The words matter because they decide what gets measured.

- **ROM is not flexibility.** Joint ROM includes things training cannot change: bone shape, joint
  geometry and soft tissue pressing against soft tissue. Flexibility is the trainable soft-tissue
  part [@afonso-2026-rom-vs-flexibility] (convention). Some differences in ROM between limbs are
  simply bony.
- **Passive ROM** is where the joint stops when moved by an examiner or by gravity, usually at the
  point of discomfort. Over short programmes it mostly measures stretch tolerance (Chapter 6)
  [@weppler-2010-extensibility].
- **Active ROM** is how far a person can move the joint under their own muscle power. It captures
  control at end range, and it can respond differently from passive ROM. In one small trial, three
  stretching methods all raised passive hip flexion, but only static stretching raised active hip
  flexion† [@lopez-bedoya-2013-active-passive] (speculative).
- **End-range strength**, the force a person can produce near the end of the range, is the most
  defensible measure of "usable range". It is rarely measured. Practitioner systems emphasise the
  gap between active and passive range, an intuitively sensible idea without validated norms.

Whether general flexibility matters at all for healthy people is disputed. One review argues that
flexibility predicts little about health or performance and should be retired as a fitness
component† [@nuzzo-2020-retiring-flexibility] (contested). For lifters, the relevant question is the
range a task needs, such as squat depth or overhead position, not flexibility in general.

## 7.2 Strength is specific to the trained range

Strength gains are largest in the range that was trained (promising).

- **Squat depth.** Over 12 weeks, deep squats raised both deep and shallow 1RM by about 20%†, while
  shallow squats raised shallow 1RM 36%† but deep 1RM only 9%† [@bloomquist-2013-squat-depth]. Deep
  squats also grew the front thigh more. In another trial, full squats raised full-squat 1RM 31.8%†
  against 11.3%† for half squats, while half-squat 1RM gains did not differ significantly
  [@kubo-2019].
- **Arms.** Full-range elbow flexion raised full-range 1RM more than partial-range training (+25.7%
  vs +16.0%†) [@pinto-2012-rom].
- **Meta-analysis.** Full ROM beat partial ROM (SMD 0.32, 0.14 to 0.49) only when the test favoured
  full ROM; when the test favoured the partial range, the difference reversed slightly (−0.12, −0.31
  to 0.07) [@wolf-2023-rom].

Transfer is asymmetric rather than one-way. Deep trainees gained more in the deep lift, but less in
the shallow lift than shallow trainees did. The likely causes are neural (activation specific to
joint angle), skill (the motor pattern of the trained depth) and muscular: deep squats load muscles
such as the adductors and gluteus maximus that shallow squats barely stretch (Chapter 5).

## 7.3 Training at long lengths transfers more broadly

Isometric training shows the length effect most clearly (promising; small trials in untrained men).
Training at a short knee angle raised force by 13.4%† only near the trained angle, and the gain
tracked muscle activity. Training at a long angle raised force across angles and grew the muscle†
[@noorkoiv-2014-isometric-angle]. In a second trial, long-length training raised strength 23.5%†
and shifted the angle of peak torque 14.6%† toward longer lengths, while short-length training
shifted it the other way† [@alegre-2014-isometric]. A systematic review concluded that long-length
isometrics give more hypertrophy and transfer better to dynamic performance [@oranchuk-2019].

The shift of peak torque toward the trained length is quick. Even a single eccentric bout moved the
optimum angle by 7.7°† [@brockett-2001-optimum-length]. The practical reading is that strength in a
long position is trainable, and that training there brings more of the range along with it than
training in a short position does.

## 7.4 Remodelling the muscle: fascicles and sarcomeres

A *fascicle* is a bundle of muscle fibres; a longer fascicle has more sarcomeres (the contractile
units) in series, or longer ones. Longer fascicles could, in principle, let a muscle produce force at
longer lengths.

**Eccentric and long-length training lengthen fascicles, but by less than practitioners quote**
(promising for direction; contested for size). In recreationally active men, Nordic hamstring curls
and hip-extension exercises both lengthened biceps femoris fascicles† [@bourne-2017-nordic-architecture].
Contraction type decides the direction: eccentric-only training lengthened fascicles and
concentric-only training shortened them† [@timmins-2016-ecc-con-architecture]. Long-range dynamic
training lengthened vastus lateralis fascicles more than short-range training (+23% vs +10%†)
[@mcmahon-2014-rom].

The size of the effect depends on how it is measured. Most studies image a small part of the fascicle
and extrapolate the rest. A meta-analysis of Nordic training split by method found effects of
g = 0.23 (−1.02 to 1.47) with manual extrapolation, 0.38 (−0.50 to 1.27) with panoramic ultrasound,
which images the whole fascicle, and 1.98 (0.52 to 3.44) with a trigonometric equation
[@yagiz-2021-nordic-fascicle]. Across all eccentric training, the panoramic estimate was 0.72 (0.17
to 1.28). Controlled estimates in footballers are similar: g = 0.43 (0.20 to 0.65) with high-volume
Nordic training [@cholp-2025-nordic-volume]. The often-quoted g ≥ 2.58 is an uncontrolled before–after
figure, with very high heterogeneity [@cuthbert-2020-nordic-volume]. In rats, ultrasound overstated
the underlying sarcomere change [@hinks-2023-ultrasound-ssn], so human ultrasound gains are best read
as upper limits.

**Whether training adds sarcomeres in humans is unsettled** (promising that fascicles lengthen;
speculative that sarcomeres are added). After three weeks of Nordic training, distal fascicles
lengthened 21% because sarcomeres got longer (+17%), not more numerous [@pincheira-2022-sarcomere].
After nine weeks, a 12-person study estimated a 25–49% rise in serial sarcomere number, with fascicles
+19–33%, muscle volume +8% and strength +40% [@andrews-2025-multiscale]. But sarcomere number was
calculated (fascicle length divided by sarcomere length), sarcomere lengths were obtained in only 6–9
participants, and there was no control group. In the same cohort, peak torque occurred at a 37%
greater knee-extension angle, with fascicles 25% longer at peak torque
[@andrews-2026-nhe-mechanics]. That is the cleanest evidence so far that the muscle learns to produce
peak force at longer lengths, but it is not independent confirmation. A 2025 meta-analysis found that
eccentric training did not add sarcomeres in animals (+1%), concluded that high force at long fibre
lengths, not eccentric contraction as such, is the trigger, and judged the human data too few to
conclude [@blazevich-2025-sarcomerogenesis].

**The change fades quickly** (promising). Fascicle gains were fully lost after 28 days of detraining
in one trial† [@timmins-2016-ecc-con-architecture] and fell 14.8%† in four weeks in another
[@carmichael-2022-isometric-eccentric-hip]. In the best-measured study they fell 12–16% in three weeks
but stayed above baseline, while muscle volume and most of the strength gain were retained
[@andrews-2025-multiscale].

Whichever exercise is chosen matters too. Hip-flexed eccentric knee flexion, which loads the
hamstrings at long length at both joints, grew the biceps femoris long head more than Nordics (+19%
vs +5%) [@maeo-2024-nordic].

## 7.5 Loaded training as flexibility training

Resistance training improves ROM against no training, and has not been shown to differ from
stretching, although the comparisons cannot rule out moderate differences either way (Chapter 6)
[@alizadeh-2023-rt-rom] [@afonso-2021-strength-vs-stretching] [@favro-2025-rt-flexibility].

**Eccentric training specifically improves ROM** (established for direction). A meta-analysis of 27
trials found g = 0.54 (0.34 to 0.74)†, though it pooled joint ROM with fascicle length
[@diong-2022-eccentric-flexibility]. Another found g = 0.86 (0.65 to 1.08)† for passive lower-limb
ROM across 17 studies [@kay-2023-eccentric-rom]. In the one direct comparison, a 10-week
within-subject trial in 11 men, eccentric hamstring training matched static stretching for ROM and
for reduced muscle stiffness, and also added strength and muscle†
[@kawama-2026-ecc-vs-stretch-hamstring] (promising). In a single session, eccentric contractions
gave more than twice the ROM gain of time-matched stretching [@kay-2026-eccentric-vs-stretch]
(speculative).

**The mechanism is partly neural.** After eccentric training of one leg, the trained leg gained
fascicle length and became *stiffer*, while the untrained leg gained ROM and stretch tolerance with
no structural change† [@kay-2024-cross-education]. So ROM gains from loading do not require a more
compliant muscle, and part of them is learned tolerance, as with stretching.

**The range, not the load, seems to be the active ingredient** (speculative). Body-weight-only
programmes did not significantly improve ROM [@alizadeh-2023-rt-rom], plausibly because many
calisthenic exercises stop short of end range. No trial has compared full- and partial-range
resistance training on ROM.

## 7.6 Practitioner mobility systems

Several popular systems claim to build "usable" range or resilient joints. A search found almost no
direct trials of them. One caution from the verification of this chapter: a phrase search of PubMed
returned zero hits for "controlled articular rotations", yet a trial with that phrase in its title
exists. Zero-hit searches are weak evidence of absence.

| System | Core claim | Direct evidence | Nearest evidence | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **FRC** (CARs, PAILs/RAILs) and its class format **Kinstretch** | Builds "usable" range and joint health [@spina-2026-frc-claims] | One single-session RCT: hip CARs raised passive internal rotation as much as static stretching (n = 28) [@head-2026-cars-vs-static]; no training trial | PAILs resemble contract–relax PNF, which matches static stretching for ROM [@konrad-2023-chronic-rom]; long-length isometrics build strength at long lengths | convention (system); speculative (CARs ≈ stretching acutely) |
| **ATG / knees over toes** | Resilient knees, range and performance [@patrick-2024-atg-program] | One 6-week RCT of letting the knees travel forward vs blocking them in bodyweight squats: no detectable difference, but it measured neither ROM nor strength and could detect only large effects [@movahhedi-2026-knees-over-toes] | Blocking knee travel raised hip torque from near zero to about 300 N·m† and cut knee torque by about a fifth† (n = 7) [@fry-2003-knee-position]; deep and long-length loading (sections 7.2–7.4) | convention (system); promising (components) |
| **Jefferson curl** (loaded spinal flexion) | Loaded flexibility, posterior-chain strength | One 18-person RCT of a program combining a modified Jefferson curl with straight-leg deadlifts: matched stretching for sit-and-reach (+6.6 vs +6.1 cm) and raised isometric hinge strength far more, at both 50% and 95% of hip range [@rosenfeldt-2024-rt-vs-stretch]; an EMG study in trained lifters [@yona-2026-jefferson-curl] | Full-range RT improves ROM | speculative |
| **Yoga** | Flexibility and strength | Many RCTs, in older or untrained people | Older adults: flexibility g = 0.50, strength 0.45 against inactive controls [@sivaramakrishnan-2019-yoga]; the strength effect fell to borderline after publication-bias adjustment† [@guo-2026-yoga-older] | promising (flexibility, older adults); contested (strength) |
| **Pilates** | Flexibility and core | Several small RCTs | No significant flexibility effect in healthy adults† [@campos-2016-pilates]; SMD 0.69 or 0.86 in women, depending on whether the paper's text or table is right [@bajcetic-2026-pilates-women] | contested |
| **Animal Flow** | Mobility and strength | One RCT against inactive controls: some active ROM gains, no strength change† [@buxton-2022-qmt] | Body-weight training weak for ROM | speculative |
| **GMB** | Mobility and control | None found | — | convention |

Two points cut across the table. First, the systems' *exercise menus* are mostly consistent with the
evidence: end-range isometrics, contract–relax stretching, deep loaded positions and eccentrics all
have support. Second, their *outcome claims* (joint health, cartilage, "bulletproof" knees, uniquely
usable range) are untested. Yoga, Pilates and Animal Flow have never been trialled in trained strength
athletes.

## 7.7 Critical appraisal

- **Population.** No ROM, architecture or mobility-system study in strength athletes.
- **Measurement.** Fascicle length is mostly extrapolated from partial images, which inflates
  effects; sarcomere number has been estimated in one small uncontrolled cohort.
- **Outcomes.** Most ROM trials use passive end range or sit-and-reach; few measure active range or
  strength at end range, the outcomes that matter for "usable range".
- **Design.** Many key trials have 6–12 participants per group, no control group, or both.
- **Read level.** The squat-depth, isometric-angle and eccentric-ROM evidence was read only as
  abstracts.
- **Search limits.** Practitioner-system searches relied partly on phrase searches, which missed one
  trial.

## 7.8 Open questions

- Does eccentric or long-length training improve ROM, or strength at end range, in trained lifters?
- Does training add sarcomeres in humans? This needs a controlled trial with direct measurement.
- Does fascicle length, as opposed to strength at long lengths, explain better ROM or performance?
- What is the minimum dose to keep architectural and ROM gains?
- Does any named mobility system beat conventional full-range training plus stretching over months?

## 7.9 Practical implications

- Train the range you need to be strong in; deep squats build more transferable strength than
  shallow ones (**promising**).
- Prefer long-length positions when choosing isometrics or partials; they transfer across the range
  better than short ones (**promising**, from small untrained trials).
- Use eccentric and long-length work to build strength in stretched positions and some range; do not
  expect large or lasting changes in muscle length (**promising**).
- Keep doing it: architectural gains fade within weeks of stopping (**promising**).
- Judge mobility by active range and strength at end range in the positions your training needs, not
  by passive flexibility (**speculative**).
- Treat named mobility systems as collections of reasonable exercises, not as proven programs; their
  outcome claims are untested (**convention**).

## 7.10 Summary

Strength is specific to the range trained, and training at long muscle lengths carries more of the
range along with it. Eccentric and long-length training lengthen fascicles and shift peak force
toward longer lengths, but the size of the architectural change has been inflated by measurement
methods, the evidence that sarcomeres are added in humans is thin, and the change fades within weeks.
Loaded training, especially eccentric work, improves ROM, partly through tolerance rather than
structure. The popular mobility systems package exercises that the evidence supports, but their own
claims are untested. Almost none of this has been studied in trained lifters.
