# Chapter 8. Connective tissue: tendon, ligament, bone, cartilage and disc

*Light first draft.*

## Abstract

Muscle is not the only tissue that has to cope with heavier loads. This chapter asks how tendon,
ligament, bone, cartilage and the intervertebral disc respond to training, how fast they do so, and
what that means for progression. Tendons stiffen within weeks, mainly through changes in material
properties rather than size. The driver is the strain the tendon experiences, which relative load
predicts poorly, and a small weekly dose is enough. The collagen core of an adult tendon, like
articular cartilage, is essentially never replaced. Bone adapts slowly and modestly, and favours
few, varied, high-magnitude loads. Evidence on ligament, cartilage and disc in lifters is thin and
mostly cross-sectional. Collagen supplements raise a blood marker of collagen synthesis, but their
effect on tendons is contested. The acute:chronic workload ratio should not guide training. Almost
none of this evidence comes from trained strength athletes.

## How to read the numbers in this chapter

Citations are evidence-record keys such as [@lazarczuk-2022-tendon-meta]. **Unmarked numbers** were
read in the full text of the source. A **dagger (†)** marks a number read only in the abstract.
Grades: **established**, **promising**, **contested**, **speculative** and **convention**
(practitioner practice with no direct data). Intervals are 95% confidence intervals unless stated
otherwise.

Three caveats apply. First, tendon training studies use small groups (usually under 15), mostly
untrained or recreational young men, for 8–16 weeks; the three cohorts of well-trained athletes in
the largest meta-analysis showed **no** change in tendon stiffness [@lazarczuk-2022-tendon-meta].
Second, the pooled tendon effect sizes are before-and-after changes within groups, not differences
from a control group. Third, much of the tendon-dose evidence comes from one Berlin group, and the
positive collagen trials mostly from one Liverpool group.

Some terms. *Stiffness* is how much force it takes to stretch a tendon a given amount. *Young's
modulus* is stiffness corrected for the tendon's size, a property of the material itself. *Strain*
is the stretch as a percentage of resting length. *CSA* is cross-sectional area.

## 8.1 Tendon: material, not size

Loading makes tendons stiffer mainly by changing what they are made of, not how big they are
(established for untrained and recreational adults over ≤14 weeks). The largest meta-analysis, of 61
studies and 763 participants, found stiffness SMD 0.74 (0.62 to 0.86), modulus 0.82 (0.58 to 1.07)
and CSA only 0.22 (0.12 to 0.33); in the studies that measured all three, modulus explained the
stiffness change and CSA did not [@lazarczuk-2022-tendon-meta]. An earlier analysis that most of these
studies overlap with found the same pattern [@bohm-2015-tendon-meta]. Resistance training was the only
training type that clearly raised stiffness (SMD 0.80); jump training raised it less (0.49).

Why stiffness matters to a lifter: a stiffer tendon stretches less under the same force, which
transmits force faster and leaves the tendon working at a lower strain.

## 8.2 Tendon: what the dose is

**Strain magnitude is the dose** (promising). In within-person trials, a leg trained at about 4.5%
Achilles strain gained stiffness, modulus and regional size, while the other leg, trained at about
2.9% with the same frequency and volume, did not† [@arampatzis-2007-strain-magnitude]. Pooled
high-strain protocols (~5%) raised stiffness with SMD 1.04 (0.65 to 1.43), and low-strain protocols
(~3%) did not, though the high-strain estimate rests on only three intervention groups, all from one
lab [@lazarczuk-2022-tendon-meta]. The idea is a band of strain, roughly 4.5–6.5%, that is anabolic:
less does nothing, and much more may cause fatigue damage (speculative).

**Relative load predicts strain poorly** (contested whether intensity matters). One meta-analysis
found high intensity (>70% of maximum) raised stiffness far more than low intensity (SMD 0.90 vs 0.04)
[@bohm-2015-tendon-meta]; its larger update found no difference (0.86 vs 0.74), and argued that
percent of maximum does not index tendon strain once exercise, joint angle and velocity vary
[@lazarczuk-2022-tendon-meta]. Direct measurement supports that. In 10 mixed-sport athletes doing
partial back squats, mean patellar tendon strain rose from 4.7% at 40% 1RM to 8.2% at 80% 1RM, but
individual values ranged from 2.6% to 10.2% [@weidlich-2023-squat-tendon-strain]. A load that
under-stimulates one person's tendon may over-strain another's.

**Contraction type does not matter** at matched high intensity: isometric (SMD 0.95),
concentric–eccentric (0.82) and eccentric (1.04) training did not differ [@bohm-2015-tendon-meta]
(promising).

**Strain duration may.** At matched strain (~6.5%) and volume, repeated 3-second contractions raised
Achilles stiffness more than 12-second holds (+57% vs +25%†), while one-legged hopping produced only
non-significant trends; tendon size gains were small and similar† [@bohm-2014-strain-rate-duration]
(promising; one unreplicated trial).

**A small weekly dose is enough** (promising; recreationally active young men). In a 16-week trial,
about 180 seconds a week of high-strain isometric loading (15 sets a week of 4 × 3 seconds at 90% of
maximum) stiffened the Achilles tendon at least as much as 240–300 seconds. Spreading it over 5
sessions (24–48 hours apart) or 2.5 sessions (48–96 hours apart) made no systematic difference, and
pooled low volume beat high volume (p = 0.04) [@tsai-2024-tendon-loading-frequency]. Normalised
stiffness rose about a third, and most of the gain came in the first 8 weeks.

## 8.3 Tendon: time course and turnover

**After a single bout**, tendon collagen synthesis rose by 6 hours, peaked at about 24 hours and was
still raised at 72 hours, in one study of prolonged kicking exercise† [@miller-2005-tendon-collagen-synthesis]
(promising). Reviews describe an earlier rise in collagen breakdown, implying a window of net loss in
the first day or so† [@magnusson-2010-tendinopathy-loading]. That window has never been measured in
tendon itself: the underlying data are blood and tissue-fluid markers after endurance running, where
the early change was mainly a dip in synthesis† [@langberg-1999-peritendinous-collagen]
(speculative).

**The tendon core is never replaced** (established). Carbon-14 from 1950s–60s nuclear tests
labelled the body's proteins at the time they were made. In 28 adults, the collagen of the Achilles
tendon core carried the atmospheric level from childhood, consistent with formation during roughly
the first 17 years of life; muscle reflected the last few years [@heinemeier-2013-tendon-14c]. The
measurable synthesis in adult tendon is probably in faster-turning peripheral and non-collagenous
pools. Whole-tendon protein synthesis is about six times slower than muscle (0.20 vs 1.17% per day),
measured in older adults with knee osteoarthritis [@houtvast-2026-tissue-protein-synthesis]
(promising). If the core is not replaced, training stiffens tendons mainly by changing the existing
material, for example through cross-linking, packing or water content, and by slow peripheral
addition (speculative; none of these has been measured in humans).

**Muscle and tendon adapt on different timescales** (contested). In 8 people training isometrically,
strength rose within 2 months but tendon stiffness only by 3 months; after stopping, stiffness
returned to baseline within 2 months while strength did not change† [@kubo-2010-time-course].
Long-term-trained men did not have larger patellar tendons than untrained men, despite much larger
muscles (cross-sectional)† [@massey-2018-tendinous-long-term]. In a season-long study of volleyball
players, strength rose without a rise in stiffness, so maximal tendon strain increased
[@weidlich-2024-personalized-tendon]. Against this, a 2026 meta-analysis found stiffness rose at
least as much as strength by the end of training (+24% vs +16%), though comparing endpoints cannot
tell which tissue adapts faster [@pang-2026-strength-stiffness]. A reasonable reading is that both
are true: stiffness keeps up over the first months of a new stimulus, while tendon size and
whole-range strain lag over years.

## 8.4 Ligament

Human data are almost absent (speculative). High-performance weightlifters had larger anterior and
posterior cruciate ligaments than controls on MRI, and size tracked how young they started more than
how long they had trained [@grzelak-2012-cruciate]. Cruciate ligament protein synthesis runs at about
0.45% per day, under half that of muscle, again in older surgical patients
[@houtvast-2026-tissue-protein-synthesis]. Most ideas about programming for ligaments come from
engineered ligaments grown in the lab (section 8.8).

## 8.5 Bone

**What stimulates bone** (promising, mostly animal data): high strain magnitude and rate, unusual
directions, and few cycles. Bone cells lose about 95% of their sensitivity after roughly 20
consecutive loading cycles and recover over 4–8 hours [@warden-2021-bone-workload]. In rats, four
short bouts of 90 cycles three hours apart strengthened bone more than one bout of 360 (ultimate force
+87% vs +64%†), with only small density changes† [@robling-2002-short-bouts]. Bone strength gains
come largely from geometry at the loaded sites, not just density.

**In adults, bone adapts slowly and modestly** (promising). In premenopausal women, an older
meta-analysis found resistance training raised lumbar spine density by about 1%† and the hip not at
all, with signs of small-study bias [@martyn-st-james-2006-rt-bmd]; a 2026 analysis of the two
recent trials found no significant effect [@jones-2026-bmd-women]. In postmenopausal women with low
bone mass, eight months of heavy resistance and impact training raised lumbar density +2.9%† against
−1.2%† in controls [@watson-2018-liftmor]. There are no trials in young trained men or women.
Cross-sectionally, lifters have more bone at the sites they load: female weightlifters had stronger
forearm bone and denser bone at the distal femur† [@heinonen-2002-female-weightlifters], and male
powerlifters had higher spine density, which tracked performance† [@tsuzuku-1998-powerlifters]. These
comparisons cannot separate training from selection or loading during growth.

**Timing and fatigue.** A remodelling cycle takes about 4 weeks of resorption, 3 months of refilling
and up to a year to mineralise fully. Resorption temporarily adds porosity, which lowers fatigue
resistance [@warden-2021-bone-workload] (established). Fatigue damage rises steeply with strain: in
laboratory estimates, about 10% more strain roughly halves the number of cycles to failure. So raising
load or impact costs bone far more than adding repetitions (promising), and the first months of a new
heavy stimulus may be a period of reduced fatigue resistance (speculative).

## 8.6 Cartilage and the intervertebral disc

**Articular cartilage collagen is essentially not replaced after skeletal maturity**†
[@heinemeier-2016-cartilage-14c] (established). Cartilage thins with unloading, but there is little
evidence that more loading thickens it, and twin data point to strong genetic control†
[@eckstein-2006-cartilage]. Professional weightlifters had thicker patellar cartilage (+14%†), and no
other differences, in a study of 7 per group† [@gratzke-2007-cartilage-weightlifters] (speculative).
Running shows a U-shaped association with hip and knee osteoarthritis: lower prevalence in
recreational runners than in sedentary people or competitive runners†
[@alentorn-geli-2017-running-oa] (promising; observational).

**The disc** is also avascular and depends on load-driven fluid flow. In healthy adults, runners had
better-hydrated lumbar discs than non-athletes (T2 signal +9–11%†), associated with the accelerations
of fast walking and slow running rather than high impact [@belavy-2017-running-disc] (promising). A
2026 meta-analysis of 39 mostly observational studies found upright loading such as running
associated with better disc measures (g = 0.31, 0.12 to 0.50; very low certainty), and combined
loading, a category including weightlifting, with higher odds of reduced disc signal (OR 2.80; low
certainty) [@samanna-2026-disc-meta]. Former elite weightlifters had the most disc degeneration on MRI
of any athlete group† [@videman-1995-athletes-spine]. There is no trial of heavy resistance training
with disc outcomes in healthy adults (speculative for any effect of lifting on the disc).

## 8.7 Collagen and vitamin C

The practice of taking collagen or gelatin with vitamin C before training rests on the idea that
supplying collagen's main amino acids (glycine, proline, hydroxyproline) when loading has switched on
collagen synthesis will help tendons.

**It raises a blood marker** (promising). In 8 men, 15 g of gelatin with vitamin C an hour before
rope skipping raised PINP, a marker of whole-body type I collagen synthesis, 153% at 4 hours against
54% with placebo [@shaw-2017-gelatin]; a replication by the same lab was not significant†
[@lis-2019-collagen-derivatives]. In 10 resistance-trained men, 30 g of hydrolysed collagen an hour
before squats raised PINP more than 15 g or none, and 15 g did not differ from none
[@lee-2024-collagen-dose]. But PINP is dominated by bone; the original authors themselves called a
tendon or ligament source "highly unlikely". No human study has shown ingested collagen raising
synthesis in tendon itself.

**Effects on tendons are contested.** Small trials, several from one Liverpool group using 30 g before
or after sessions, report larger gains in tendon stiffness or size: for example, stiffness +15.4% vs
+4.6% in 11 professional female footballers [@lee-2025-collagen-female-soccer], and greater size,
stiffness and modulus gains in 20 middle-aged men [@nulty-2025-collagen-middle-aged]. Two 14-week
trials of 5 g a day found larger tendon size but no stiffness change†
[@jerger-2022-collagen-achilles] [@jerger-2023-collagen-patellar]; the first lists an author
affiliated with a collagen manufacturer. The largest training trial with MRI, 15 g a
day in 39 men, found no effect on any tendon outcome† [@balshaw-2023-collagen-peptides]. The only
meta-analysis, searched to December 2023, pooled tendon stiffness and modulus at SMD 0.05 (−0.38 to
0.48) with very low certainty; its positive tendon-size result became non-significant when one trial
was removed [@bischof-2024-collagen-meta]. Dose and timing differ between positive and null trials,
so the nulls do not disprove the idea either.

## 8.8 The "10 minutes, 6 hours apart" rule

A popular rule says connective tissue responds to about 10 minutes of loading and then needs about 6
hours to respond again. It comes from engineered ligaments, where signalling peaked within 10 minutes
of stretch and cells stayed unresponsive for up to 6 hours; 10 minutes every 6 hours raised collagen
content more than continuous stretch† [@paxton-2012-intermittent-stretch]. Bone in rats behaves
similarly (section 8.5). In human tendon, only spacing *between* days has been tested, with no
effect [@tsai-2024-tendon-loading-frequency]; spacing within a day has never been tested. Grade:
promising for bone, speculative for tendon and ligament. The senior author of the engineered-ligament
and gelatin work declares funding from nutrition companies [@houtvast-2026-tissue-protein-synthesis].

## 8.9 Training load and injury

The **acute:chronic workload ratio** (ACWR) compares the last week's training load with the average
of the last month. It was proposed with a "sweet spot" of 0.8–1.3 and a danger zone above 1.5, from
team-sport cohorts [@gabbett-2016-paradox]. It should not guide training (established that no study
has estimated a causal effect) [@impellizzeri-2020-acwr]. In one re-analysis, dividing acute load by
fixed or random numbers reproduced the association with injury, and neither the ratio nor acute load
predicted injury much better than chance (c-statistic 0.57† vs 0.50)
[@impellizzeri-2021-chronic-workload] (promising that the association largely reflects rescaled acute
load). The latest meta-analysis found a small, extremely variable association (g = 0.35, I² 96%) that
was as strong for contact injuries as non-contact ones, which points to exposure rather than tissue
overload [@ding-2026-acwr-meta]. A better frame is tissue-level: injury follows when a tissue's load
exceeds its capacity, which whole-body load metrics do not capture† [@kalkhoven-2021-load-causal]
(promising).

**Strength training reduces sports injuries** in a small set of largely overlapping trials (RR 0.34†,
0.24 to 0.48; 6 trials, 177 injuries), mostly in youth and team sports
[@lauersen-2018-strength-injury]; broader syntheses suggest smaller effects (promising).
**Strength sports themselves have low injury rates**: 2.4–3.3 per 1,000 training hours in
weightlifting and 1.0–4.4 in powerlifting†, from mostly low-quality studies
[@aasa-2017-weightlifting-injuries].

## 8.10 Critical appraisal

- **Population.** No connective-tissue dose–response study in competitive strength athletes; the three
  well-trained tendon cohorts showed no change.
- **Single labs.** Tendon dose evidence comes mostly from one group, and positive collagen trials
  mostly from another.
- **Measurement.** Tendon stiffness and modulus are derived from ultrasound and estimated forces, and
  modulus is calculated from stiffness and size, so the two are not independent. PINP is not a tendon
  measure.
- **Overlap.** The two tendon meta-analyses, and the two strength-training injury analyses, share
  most of their studies; they are not independent confirmations.
- **Design.** Ligament, cartilage and disc evidence in lifters is cross-sectional.
- **Read level.** Many bone, cartilage, disc and injury numbers were read only in abstracts.

## 8.11 Open questions

- How do tendons respond to added loading in athletes who are already strong?
- Does lab-measured tendon strain predict injury in adults?
- Does ingested collagen raise synthesis in tendon itself? This needs a tendon tracer study.
- Does spacing loading bouts within a day help human tendon or ligament?
- Does heavy lifting change the disc, for better or worse? No trial exists.

## 8.12 Practical implications

- Heavy compound lifting already strains tendons in the adaptive band; extra tendon work matters most
  where training does not load a tendon heavily (**promising**).
- If adding tendon work, a little is enough: about 15 sets a week of 3-second heavy contractions,
  spread however is convenient (**promising**, untested in strength athletes).
- Ramp load more cautiously when strength is rising fast and after layoffs, when tendon strain is
  likely highest (**speculative**; no outcome trials).
- For bone, a few heavy, varied or impact loads beat many repetitions; raising load costs more fatigue
  life than adding repetitions (**promising**).
- Do not expect to build cartilage or disc by lifting; there is no evidence either way that heavy
  lifting harms them in healthy adults (**speculative**).
- Collagen with vitamin C is cheap and low-risk, but its tendon benefit is contested. If used, the
  pre-session protocol tested was about 30 g with vitamin C an hour before training (**contested**).
- Do not use ACWR thresholds. Avoid large, abrupt jumps in high-strain or impact loading
  (**convention**).

## 8.13 Summary

Tendons stiffen within weeks of heavy loading, mostly through changes in their material, with little
growth in size. The stimulus is strain, which percent of 1RM predicts poorly, and a small dose is
enough. The tendon core and articular cartilage are essentially permanent in adults, so adaptation
works on existing tissue. Bone responds slowly to few, varied, heavy loads. Ligament, cartilage and
disc data in lifters are thin and cross-sectional. Collagen supplements move a bone-dominated blood
marker more clearly than they change tendons. Load-ratio injury models do not hold up. Nearly all of
this comes from untrained or recreational people.
