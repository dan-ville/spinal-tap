# Chapter 10. Recovery, sleep, nutrition and monitoring

*Light first draft.*

## Abstract

Training supplies the signal for adaptation; this chapter is about what surrounds it. It asks how
sleep loss affects strength, how much protein and energy lifters need, which supplements work, which
recovery methods help or harm adaptation, and which readiness tests are worth using. One night of
lost sleep costs about 3% of maximal strength, more in the afternoon, and degrades lifting quality
more than quantity; whether chronic short sleep blunts gains is untested. Extra protein adds a small
amount to training, with most of the benefit captured by about 1.6 g/kg/day. Creatine reliably adds
strength, less than headline figures suggest, and only a little directly measured muscle. Caffeine
gives a small acute boost. Regular cold-water immersion after lifting probably blunts gains; massage
and compression help comfort without affecting adaptation. Subjective wellness scores and bar velocity
are the most useful monitors, but no readiness tool has been shown to improve long-term results.

## How to read the numbers in this chapter

Citations are evidence-record keys such as [@craven-2022-sleep]. **Unmarked numbers** were read in
the full text of the source. A **dagger (†)** marks a number read only in the abstract. Grades:
**established**, **promising**, **contested**, **speculative** and **convention** (practitioner
practice with no direct data). Intervals are 95% confidence intervals unless stated otherwise.

Three caveats. First, almost every trial here was run on young men, mostly untrained or recreationally
active, over 4–12 weeks; sleep studies are mostly one night. Second, much of the mechanistic evidence
is acute muscle protein synthesis (MPS), which often fails to predict long-term growth. Third, DXA
"lean mass" includes water and glycogen, which matters for creatine (it draws water into muscle) and
for dieting (which depletes glycogen). Where direct imaging disagrees with DXA, imaging is preferred.
Several of the creatine and protein papers have authors with industry ties, noted where relevant.

## 10.1 Sleep

**One night of lost sleep costs a little strength** (established for a small pooled drop). Across 25
studies, acute sleep loss lowered strength by 2.85% (−4.47 to −1.23), more for lower-body (−3.42%)
than upper-body tasks (−1.63%, not significant) [@craven-2022-sleep]. Afternoon tasks were
consistently affected and morning tasks generally were not (promising), and after early waking the
loss grew by about 1% for each hour awake before the task.

**Which kind of sleep loss matters most is contested.** In that meta-analysis only total deprivation
reached significance (−3.00%); early waking had a larger but non-significant estimate (−4.45%, −9.30
to 0.41) [@craven-2022-sleep]. A second meta-analysis, testing mostly isometric and grip force, found
the opposite: early waking reduced force in athletes (SMD −0.57, −0.94 to −0.19) and total deprivation
did not (−0.16, −0.39 to 0.06) [@kong-2025-sleep-deprivation]. Both agree that afternoon testing is
more affected. A review of 17 studies found one sleepless night had little effect, but consecutive
nights of restriction reduced force in multi-joint lifts† [@knowles-2018-sleep-strength].

**Quality suffers more than quantity** (promising). In 10 resistance-trained women, nine nights of 5
hours in bed reduced volume load by less than 1%† (though significantly), but slowed lower-body
compound lifts by up to 15%† and raised session effort ratings by 11%†; upper-body lifts were
unaffected [@knowles-2022-sleep-restriction-females]. In 24 resistance-trained adults, 4 hours or no
sleep reduced bar velocity at 50% 1RM by 7–13%† in men [@del-val-manzano-2026-sleep]. Higher effort at
the same load suggests a central contribution (motivation, perceived effort, sleep pressure), which
would also explain why afternoon and multi-joint tasks suffer most (promising; not isolated directly).

**Mechanisms.** In 13 young adults who did not exercise, one night of total deprivation lowered
protein synthesis after a meal by 18% (0.072 vs 0.059% per hour), raised cortisol 21% and lowered
testosterone 24% [@lamon-2021-sleep-mps]. Five nights of 4 hours in bed lowered mitochondrial protein
synthesis and glucose tolerance, and high-intensity interval exercise during the restriction prevented
both [@saner-2021-sleep-restriction] (promising).

**Whether chronic short sleep blunts training gains is untested** (speculative). No randomised trial
exists in trained lifters. The only training study compared untrained middle-aged men who habitually
slept about 5.8 hours with adequate sleepers, using elastic-band training and arm muscle area
estimated from circumference and skinfolds; gains did not differ [@borba-2024-habitual-sleep]. Sleep
extension and naps are the best-supported sleep interventions for athletes' performance, though few
studies measured strength† [@cunha-2023-sleep-interventions]. A consensus statement recommends an
individual sleep target based on perceived need rather than a fixed 7–9 hours†
[@walsh-2021-sleep-consensus] (convention).

## 10.2 Protein and energy

**Extra protein adds a small amount to training** (established). Across 49 trials and 1,863
participants, protein supplementation added 2.49 kg (0.64 to 4.33) to 1RM gains, about 9% of the 27
kg that training itself produced, and 0.30 kg of fat-free mass (0.09 to 0.52) [@morton-2018-protein].
The benefit was larger in resistance-trained people. A GRADE-rated meta-analysis of 74 trials found
lean mass SMD 0.22 (0.14 to 0.30), moderate certainty [@nunes-2022-protein].

**About 1.6 g/kg/day captures most of the benefit** (promising for the range). The widely quoted break
point of 1.62 g/kg/day had a wide interval (1.03 to 2.20), and its model was not statistically
significant (p = 0.079) [@morton-2018-protein]. In adults under 65, only intakes of 1.6 g/kg/day or
more gave a significant lean-mass benefit (SMD 0.30 vs 0.15 at 1.2–1.59), though these are
between-study comparisons that are not consistent across outcomes [@nunes-2022-protein]. A spline
analysis of 105 trials found the curve flattens above about 1.3 g/kg/day but does not plateau, and
resistance training markedly reduced the flattening; its authors are employees of a dairy company
[@tagawa-2021-protein-dose]. In practice: about 1.6 g/kg/day for most trained people, and up to about
2.2 g/kg/day to cover individual variation.

**Per-meal ceilings and timing are secondary** (contested). After whole-body exercise, 100 g of milk
protein raised muscle protein synthesis about 40% more than 25 g in the 4–12 hours afterwards, and
muscle was still incorporating dietary amino acids at 12 hours [@trommelen-2023-protein-dose]. Once
signalling is switched on, the amount and duration of synthesis seem to track amino-acid supply. But
this was one acute study in recreationally active men, using slow casein-rich protein, with a dairy
employee among the authors; critics argue it does not justify dismissing protein distribution†
[@witard-2024-protein-commentary]. Protein timing around the session showed no effect on strength or
hypertrophy once total daily protein was accounted for statistically [@schoenfeld-2013-protein-timing].
Three to five protein meals of about 0.3–0.5 g/kg is a reasonable convention, not a requirement.

**Energy deficit** (promising). Resistance training in a deficit impaired lean-mass gains (ES −0.57†,
p = 0.02) but did not significantly impair strength gains (ES −0.31†, p = 0.28, few trials)
[@murphy-2022-energy-deficit]. That is "not shown to be impaired", not "spared". A deficit of about 500
kcal/day prevented lean-mass gains in a cross-study regression (speculative). Strength depends heavily
on neural adaptation, which needs little energy, and DXA exaggerates apparent muscle loss as glycogen
and water fall. Higher protein helps in a deficit. In overweight young men on a 40% deficit, 2.4
g/kg/day gained 1.2 kg† of lean mass against 0.1 kg† at 1.2 g/kg/day [@longland-2016-deficit-protein].
In trained, energy-restricted people, fat-free mass rose with protein intake scaled to fat-free mass,
with no clear plateau [@refalo-2025-protein-deficit] (promising for direction). Contest-prep guidance
of 2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass, losing 0.5–1% of body mass a week, is convention†
[@helms-2014-contest-nutrition].

**Energy surplus** (speculative). In trained lifters, faster weight gain over 8 weeks mainly added fat,
with little evidence of more muscle† [@helms-2023-energy-surplus].

## 10.3 Supplements

**Creatine adds strength** (established for direction; promising for size). With resistance training,
creatine added 4.43 kg (3.12 to 5.75) to upper-body and 11.35 kg (8.44 to 14.25) to lower-body strength
gains in adults under 50 [@wang-2024-creatine-strength]. In a 2026 three-level meta-analysis the effect
was about g = 0.55, but tests for small-study bias were significant and adjustment cut it to about
0.35 [@wang-2026-creatine]. The effect is clear in men and unclear in women, from only 2 female-only
studies. Several authors of these analyses declare creatine-industry ties.

**Creatine adds only a little directly measured muscle** (promising for a small effect). DXA and
similar methods show fat-free mass gains of about 1.4 kg (1.07 to 1.70)
[@ashtary-larky-2025-creatine-training-status]. But a meta-analysis of studies that imaged muscle
directly found SMD 0.11 (−0.02 to 0.25), with every site estimate's interval including zero
[@burke-2023-creatine-hypertrophy]. The gap is plausibly partly intracellular water, but no study has
separated water from muscle (speculative). The mechanism: supplementation raises muscle creatine and
phosphocreatine by 20–40%, which speeds energy recovery between efforts and plausibly allows more
training work [@kreider-2017-creatine]. Standard dosing is about 20 g a day for 5–7 days then 3–5 g
a day, or 3 g a day from the start; the society position finds no compelling evidence of harm in
healthy people, though its authors declare industry funding [@kreider-2017-creatine]
(established for efficacy and dosing; promising for long-term safety).

**Creatine and cognition: no credible benefit in healthy young adults.** Memory effects concentrate in
older adults (SMD 0.88 vs 0.03 in young people) [@prokopidis-2023-creatine-memory]. Both pooled
meta-analyses double-counted non-independent outcomes; one reportedly lost its overall effect on
reanalysis, and the other has the same error [@citherlet-2026-creatine-commentary]
[@xu-2024-creatine-cognition] (contested overall). Benefits under sleep deprivation are speculative†
[@gordji-nejad-2024-creatine-sleep-deprivation].

**Caffeine gives a small acute boost** (established). It raised 1RM strength by SMD 0.20 (0.03 to
0.36) [@grgic-2018-caffeine], with moderate certainty for strength in an umbrella review†
[@grgic-2020-caffeine-umbrella], and bar velocity by SMD 0.42 (0.19 to 0.65), more in low habitual
users [@xiao-2025-caffeine-velocity]. The usual dose is 3–6 mg/kg, possibly as little as 2 mg/kg;
taking it 60 minutes before is common practice rather than a tested optimum [@guest-2021-caffeine].
It works mainly by blocking adenosine receptors in the brain, raising drive and lowering perceived
effort. The same mechanism disturbs sleep, so evening sessions bring a trade-off.

**Weaker or harmful options.**

- The IOC classes only caffeine, creatine, some buffering agents and nitrate as having good
  performance evidence, and warns of contamination with banned substances†
  [@maughan-2018-ioc-supplements]. Buffers and nitrate were not reviewed here for strength.
- HMB showed no effect on lean mass or strength in young adults† [@jakubowski-2020-hmb].
- High-dose vitamins C and E blocked markers of mitochondrial adaptation to endurance training†
  [@paulsen-2014-antioxidants], but showed no detectable effect on resistance-training outcomes (SMD
  −0.07†) [@clifford-2020-antioxidants] (promising for no meaningful harm).
- **Anti-inflammatory drugs: contested.** High-dose ibuprofen (1,200 mg a day) roughly halved
  quadriceps growth in young untrained adults (+3.7%† vs +7.5%†) [@lilja-2018-nsaid]. A 2025 trial in
  trained men found diclofenac *increased* growth (+8.6%† vs +3.9%†) without changing strength
  [@mallinson-2025-nsaid]. The effect may depend on drug, dose and training status.

## 10.4 Recovery methods

**Regular cold-water immersion probably blunts gains** (promising). A Bayesian meta-analysis found that
cold-water immersion after resistance training reduced gains by SMD −0.22 (−0.47 to 0.04), with a 96%
probability of some harm [@pinero-2024-cwi]. Limb-only immersion reduced strength gains (−0.31†), while
whole-body immersion's estimate was small and uncertain (−0.08†) [@grgic-2023-cwi-strength]; that
concerns strength only and should not be read as safe for muscle growth. An umbrella review found
immersion reduces soreness for up to 96 hours and speeds power recovery, but impairs strength and
hypertrophy gains when used repeatedly, at low certainty [@berjisian-2026-cwi-umbrella]. A network
meta-analysis ranked passive recovery best for strength in resistance training† [@yu-2026-cwi-nma].

The mechanism is the best supported in this chapter. Twelve weeks of immersion after strength sessions
removed the gains in type II fibre size (+17% with active recovery only) and myonuclei (+26%), and
blunted satellite-cell and growth signalling [@roberts-2015-cwi]. Cooling one leg to 8 °C reduced the
uptake of dietary amino acids into muscle, acute protein synthesis (0.058 vs 0.072% per hour) and
two-week synthesis (1.48 vs 1.67% per day) [@fuchs-2020-cwi-mps]. Cold constricts vessels and slows
enzymes, cutting amino-acid delivery, and damps the inflammatory and satellite-cell response that
supports remodelling. Immersion therefore trades long-term gains for short-term comfort and power
recovery: defensible in competition, counterproductive in a building phase.

**Massage and compression help comfort, not adaptation** (promising). Massage did not improve strength
(SMD 0.17, −0.08 to 0.42) or other performance, but reduced soreness (SMD 1.13, 0.44 to 1.82)
[@davis-2020-massage]. Compression garments aided strength recovery in the hours to days after a bout
(ES 0.62†) [@brown-2017-compression]; a placebo is impossible. Both act mainly on perception and fluid
shifts, not on the signals that drive adaptation, and neither has been tested for long-term effects.
Heating the trained thigh did not change hypertrophy in one small trial† [@stadnyk-2018-heat]
(speculative).

**The biggest lever is the session itself.** In trained men, sets taken to failure slowed the recovery
of jump height and bar velocity by 24–48 hours compared with volume-matched sets stopped halfway†
[@moran-navarro-2017-recovery] (promising). How close sets go to failure changes recovery needs more
than any recovery method does (Chapter 3).

## 10.5 Monitoring readiness

- **Subjective wellness questionnaires** tracked training load more sensitively and consistently than
  blood markers or heart rate [@saw-2016-subjective] (promising), mostly in endurance and team sports
  and with validated multi-item tools. Single-item scores have largely unestablished measurement
  properties [@duignan-2020-wellness] (contested).
- **Bar velocity at a fixed load** tracked recovery after failure versus non-failure sets†
  [@moran-navarro-2017-recovery] and detected sleep restriction more sensitively than volume load
  [@knowles-2022-sleep-restriction-females] (promising). It is the most specific objective readiness
  signal for lifters. Linear position transducers are the most accurate commercial devices
  [@weakley-2021-devices]. Caffeine, time of day and warm-up all shift velocity, so test conditions
  need standardising.
- **Jump height**: the average of several jumps was more sensitive to fatigue than the best jump†
  [@claudino-2017-cmj], but jumps are less specific to heavy, slow or upper-body lifting.
- **Heart rate variability** needs rolling averages, not single-day readings† [@plews-2013-hrv].
  HRV-guided training gave at most small, non-significant performance benefits in mostly
  endurance-training trials (SMD 0.20) [@manresa-rocamora-2021-hrv-guided]. It responds little to local
  neuromuscular fatigue, which limits it for strength athletes (speculative).

No readiness tool has been shown to improve long-term strength or hypertrophy compared with good fixed
programming or in-session effort ratings (Chapter 4).

## 10.6 Critical appraisal

- **Population.** Young men, mostly untrained; one-night sleep studies; few women, especially in
  creatine, cold-water and sleep research.
- **Acute vs chronic.** Protein dose, sleep and supplement mechanisms rest heavily on short-term
  protein synthesis, which often fails to predict growth.
- **Measurement.** DXA confounds creatine and dieting studies.
- **Industry ties.** Common among creatine, protein and supplement authors, including some
  meta-analyses cited as independent checks.
- **Read level.** Key numbers from the sleep-restriction trial in women, the energy-deficit
  meta-analysis, the ibuprofen trial and several cold-water and compression analyses were read only in
  abstracts.

## 10.7 Open questions

- Does weeks of 5–6 hours' sleep blunt strength or muscle gains in trained lifters?
- Where is the protein plateau in trained lifters, measured with imaging rather than DXA?
- How much of creatine's fat-free mass gain is muscle?
- How long after training must cold-water immersion wait to avoid blunting gains?
- Does any readiness test beat in-session effort ratings for long-term outcomes?

## 10.8 Practical implications

- Protect sleep, especially the end of the night, and schedule key heavy sessions earlier in the day
  after poor nights (**promising**).
- Eat about 1.6 g/kg/day of protein, up to about 2.2; more when dieting, scaled to fat-free mass
  (**promising**).
- Spread protein over three to five meals if convenient; total daily intake matters most
  (**convention**).
- When cutting, keep the deficit moderate and protein high; expect strength to hold better than
  lean mass (**promising**).
- Take 3–5 g of creatine a day; expect more strength and a little muscle, not a cognitive boost
  (**established** for strength).
- Use 2–6 mg/kg of caffeine before important sessions, mindful of sleep (**established**).
- Avoid regular cold-water immersion after lifting in building phases; use it in competition if it
  helps (**promising**).
- Use massage or compression for comfort, not for gains (**promising**).
- Monitor with a short wellness questionnaire and bar velocity at a standard warm-up load
  (**convention** built from promising parts).

## 10.9 Summary

Sleep loss costs a few percent of strength and more of lifting quality, while its effect on long-term
gains is untested. Protein adds a small amount to training, with most of the benefit by about 1.6
g/kg/day, and more matters when dieting. Creatine adds strength, less than the headlines suggest, and
little directly measured muscle; caffeine gives a small acute boost. Recovery methods that blunt the
signals of adaptation, above all regular cold-water immersion, can cost gains, while massage and
compression help comfort without affecting them. Wellness scores and bar velocity are the most useful
monitors, but none has been shown to improve long-term outcomes.
