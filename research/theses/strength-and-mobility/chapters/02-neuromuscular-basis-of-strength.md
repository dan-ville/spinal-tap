# Chapter 2. The neuromuscular basis of strength

## Abstract

Maximal strength is the greatest force or torque a person can express in a specified task. This
chapter asks what sets that number and what changes when it rises with training. Two systems are
involved. The muscle supplies the force-generating tissue: its volume, its physiological
cross-sectional area, its architecture and the leverage it acts through. The nervous system decides
how much of that tissue is used, how fast, and in what coordination with synergists, antagonists
and stabilisers. The chapter traces the century-old idea that early strength gains are "neural"
from its first indirect evidence to modern motor-unit decomposition, and argues for four
conclusions. First, strength rises faster than muscle size in the first weeks of training, and the
excess is neural and learned. That much is established, but where in the nervous system the change
happens is still contested. Second, across people with years of training, accumulated muscle size
is the main determinant of strength differences. This is promising evidence, drawn mostly from one
laboratory's cross-sectional work in men. Third, strength is strongly task-specific: dynamic
training improves the trained lift about twice as much as an untrained isometric test. Fourth,
whether adding muscle causes additional one-repetition-maximum (1RM) strength in already-trained
lifters, and by how much, is unresolved. The chapter explains why the two sides of that debate
disagree, the most important reasons being how growth is measured and how long trials last. Almost
all mechanistic evidence comes from short studies of untrained young men performing single-joint
isometric tasks, and the chapter states that limitation wherever it matters.

## How to read the numbers in this chapter

Citations use evidence-record keys, such as [@maden-wilkinson-2020]. Following the thesis
conventions (Chapter 1), only numbers read in the full text of a source may be presented as
verified.

- **Unmarked numbers** were read in the full text.
- A **dagger (†)** marks a number read only in the abstract.
- A **section sign (§)** marks a number that the chapter's researcher read in full text but the
  independent verifier could not reach. The record is held at abstract level, so the number is
  unverified.
- A **double dagger (‡)** marks content known only from a secondary source.

Preprints are named as such. Intervals are 95% confidence intervals unless stated otherwise.

The grades are those used throughout the thesis:

- **established**: consistent evidence from several good syntheses;
- **promising**: good but limited or indirect evidence;
- **contested**: credible evidence points different ways;
- **speculative**: plausible but thinly supported;
- **convention**: practitioner practice with no direct data.

One more reading rule matters in this chapter more than in most. Several of the studies discussed
come from a small number of laboratories that reuse their participants. Where two papers report on
the same people, the chapter says so: agreement between them is consistency, not replication.

## 2.1 Introduction: an old question with new instruments

Ask a strength coach why a novice's squat climbs ten kilograms in a month while their thighs look
no different, and the answer will be "neural adaptation". The phrase is one of the most widely
repeated ideas in exercise science. It is also one of the least precisely understood. It names a
residual, the part of the strength gain that muscle growth does not explain, rather than a
mechanism. A good part of this chapter is about turning that residual into something measurable.

The question has a long history. In 1979 Moritani and deVries partitioned strength gains into
"neural" and "muscular" components. They compared the slope of the relation between surface
electromyography (EMG: the electrical activity of muscle recorded from the skin) and force before
and after eight weeks of elbow-flexor training. Their conclusion, that neural factors dominate the
first three to five weeks and hypertrophy dominates thereafter, became a textbook figure
[@moritani-1979]. The partition rested on assumptions about EMG that no longer survive scrutiny
(Section 2.3), but the phenomenon it described has held up. In 1986 Rutherford and Jones made the
point more sharply. Trainees who lifted progressively heavier loads in a dynamic leg extension
roughly tripled the load they could lift (about +200%†), yet their isometric force in the same
muscles rose only 15-20%† [@rutherford-1986]. No plausible change in muscle could explain a gap of
that size. Something about the *task* had been learned.

For the next three decades the field accumulated indirect evidence of this kind. Folland and
Williams's 2007 review drew it together and concluded that the behavioural signs of neural
adaptation are robust, while its site remained unknown [@folland-2007]. Fourteen years later,
Škarabot and colleagues reached essentially the same verdict with far better instruments
[@skarabot-2021-review]. What changed in between was the arrival of tools that can follow
individual motor units through a training programme. High-density surface EMG decomposition is the
chief of them. In parallel, a second debate gained force. If neural factors explain early gains,
does muscle growth explain later ones, or is the link between size and strength looser than
assumed? That debate, conducted in large part between the Loenneke-Buckner-Dankel group on one side
and the Folland-Balshaw group and their allies on the other, is the chapter's most consequential
open question for trained lifters.

The chapter is organised around five questions:

1. What determines how much force a person can produce? (Section 2.2)
2. How can the nervous system's contribution be measured, and what are the instruments' blind spots?
   (Section 2.3)
3. What do we know about neural adaptations: whether they exist, what they consist of and where
   they happen? (Sections 2.4 to 2.9)
4. How do the neural and muscular contributions change over a training career? (Section 2.10)
5. How specific is strength, and does added muscle cause added strength in people who are already
   trained? (Sections 2.11 and 2.12)

Questions of dose (how much volume, load and frequency) are left to Chapter 3. Questions of how
muscle grows are left to Chapter 5.

## 2.2 A working model: what determines force

Before asking what training changes, it helps to have a model of what determines force in the first
place. A useful one has two halves.

**The muscle half.** The force a muscle can produce depends first on how many contractile units
(sarcomeres) are arranged *in parallel*, pulling side by side. The best anatomical index of this is
the **physiological cross-sectional area (PCSA)**, the area cut perpendicular to the muscle fibres.
In a pennate muscle, whose fibres run at an angle to the line of pull, PCSA can be much larger than
the **anatomical cross-sectional area (ACSA)** seen on a single scan slice. **Muscle volume** is
easier to measure than PCSA and is closely related to it. **Architecture** (fascicle length and
pennation angle) affects both how much force the muscle can produce and how fast it can shorten.
**Specific tension** is force per unit of PCSA, the "quality" of the tissue as a force generator.
Finally, the force a muscle produces reaches the outside world as **torque** about a joint, and the
**moment arm** (the perpendicular distance from the joint centre to the tendon's line of pull)
converts one into the other. Fibre type matters less than is often assumed. In humans, single-fibre
specific tension is broadly similar across fibre types [@erskine-2011], so a larger area of type II
fibres contributes to maximal force mainly through size. (Type II fibres matter more for speed, a
point returned to in Section 2.6.)

**The nervous half.** A muscle is controlled in packets called **motor units**: one motoneuron in
the spinal cord and all the muscle fibres it innervates. The nervous system grades force in two
ways. It can **recruit** more motor units, normally in order of increasing size: small,
low-threshold units first and large, high-threshold units last. This ordering is the size
principle. It can also raise the **discharge rate** (rate coding) of units already active. Each
action potential produces a twitch, and twitches fired close together summate along a sigmoidal
force-frequency relation until force plateaus. The largest, high-threshold units contribute the most
force per unit, so maximal strength requires that they be recruited and driven at high rates.

Beyond the single muscle, a maximal lift is a coordination problem. Prime movers and synergists
must be activated in the right sequence and proportion. Antagonists must be restrained, since their
coactivation subtracts from net joint torque. The trunk and limbs must be stabilised so that force
reaches the bar rather than leaking into unwanted movement. None of this is captured by any single
muscle's capacity.

**Voluntary activation** links the two halves. It is the proportion of a muscle's force capacity
that a person can drive voluntarily. It is usually estimated by delivering an electrical stimulus
during a maximal effort: if the stimulus adds force (the "interpolated twitch"), activation was
incomplete.

### A worked example: decomposing a 60% strength advantage

One cross-sectional study lets us put numbers on this model. Maden-Wilkinson and colleagues compared
men with about four years of knee-extensor training against untrained men. The trained men produced
60% more isometric knee-extension torque. Their quadriceps volume was 56% larger, their PCSA 41%
larger, their fascicles 11% longer, their specific tension 9% higher (8% in the paper's Discussion)
and their patellar-tendon moment arm 4% longer [@maden-wilkinson-2020].

Multiplying the three force-relevant ratios gives 1.41 (PCSA) x 1.09 (specific tension) x 1.04
(moment arm) = 1.60, exactly the observed torque ratio. This should not be over-read. In vivo
specific tension is conventionally *calculated* as estimated muscle force (torque divided by moment
arm) per unit PCSA, so the product reproduces the torque largely by construction. What the
arithmetic does show is the *partition*. Of a 60% advantage, the great majority is accounted for by
more contractile tissue in parallel. Only a small part comes from leverage or from the tissue being
intrinsically "better". Nothing is left over for a large difference in neural drive at the
quadriceps level. That is consistent with the EMG findings in Section 2.3, which come from
overlapping participants.

The caveats are as instructive as the result. The study is cross-sectional and the trained group
small (16 men), so selection cannot be excluded: people with large quadriceps may be the ones who
take up and persist in training. The task was isometric single-joint knee extension, which has
little coordination demand. And the participants overlap with those of other studies from the same
laboratory (Section 2.13). The example shows what a mature, highly trained muscle looks like. It
does not show how it got that way.

## 2.3 Measuring the nervous system: what each window shows

The neural side of strength is hard to study because no instrument observes "neural drive"
directly. Each method opens a different window, and each has a characteristic blind spot. Much of
the disagreement in this literature is disagreement between windows.

| Method | What it indexes | Main limitation | Key sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Surface EMG amplitude | Gross neural drive to a muscle | Signal cancellation; confounded by muscle size, subcutaneous fat and fibre membrane properties; conclusions change with normalisation | [@skarabot-2021-emg]; [@del-vecchio-2026] |
| Interpolated twitch | Voluntary activation | Ceiling near 90-100% in healthy people, so changes are small | [@nuzzo-2017]; [@jenkins-2017] |
| H-reflex and V-wave | Spinal reflex excitability; descending drive to motoneurons during maximal effort | Presynaptic inhibition, axonal excitability and polysynaptic contributions; task dependence | [@siddique-2020]; [@skarabot-2021-review] |
| Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) | Corticospinal excitability (motor evoked potentials, MEPs), intracortical inhibition, silent period | Cannot separate cortical from spinal sites alone; weakly linked to behaviour; test task rarely matches the training task | [@kidgell-2017]; [@siddique-2020]; [@gomez-feria-2023] |
| Cervicomedullary stimulation | Transmission from corticospinal axons to motoneurons | Few studies; usually at rest or low force | [@nuzzo-2017] |
| Motor-unit decomposition (high-density surface or intramuscular EMG) | Recruitment thresholds, discharge rates, estimated persistent inward currents, conduction velocity of individual units | Mostly submaximal, isometric, single-joint tasks; tracking at maximal effort only from 2026; cannot identify the source of the input | [@del-vecchio-2019]; [@skarabot-2021-review]; [@lecce-2026-preprint] |
| Startle (StartReact) paradigms | Indirect index of reticulospinal contribution | The corticospinal tract also contributes; the direction of group differences is open to interpretation | [@tanel-2025] |

Two methodological lessons from recent work deserve to be carried through the rest of the chapter.

**Surface EMG amplitude is confounded by muscle size between people.** For decades, the observation
that trained lifters produce larger EMG signals was taken as evidence that they "activate more" of
their muscle. Škarabot and colleagues compared long-term trained and untrained men. The trained men
had 66-71%† greater maximal torque, 54-56%† larger muscles and 29-60%† larger maximal M-waves (the
compound electrical response to supramaximal nerve stimulation, which reflects how much muscle lies
under the electrode and how it conducts). Trained men had higher absolute EMG only in the knee
extensors. In the elbow flexors there was no difference even before normalisation (P = 0.195†).
Once EMG was normalised to the maximal M-wave, no group difference remained for any muscle
(P ≥ 0.652†) [@skarabot-2021-emg]. Larger EMG in a trained lifter therefore partly reflects more
muscle under the electrode, not more drive to it.

**Within a person, over a short intervention, absolute EMG is more informative.** A 2026 analysis
from the Rome-Imperial group found that changes in absolute high-density EMG amplitude tracked
motor-unit changes within individuals. The repeated-measures correlations were 0.56† with discharge
rate and -0.55† with recruitment threshold. EMG normalised to maximal voluntary contraction did not
track them [@del-vecchio-2026]. The two findings are compatible. Absolute EMG is usable within one
person over a few weeks, when muscle size and membrane properties barely change. It misleads when
comparing people whose muscles differ greatly in size. The general lesson is that a normalisation
choice is a modelling decision, and it can create or erase an apparent neural difference.

A third, broader caution comes from Hortobágyi and colleagues. Across activation, stimulation and
imaging studies, they found that measured neural changes and measured performance changes are often
uncoupled. The people whose TMS or EMG measures change most are not reliably the people who gain
most strength [@hortobagyi-2021]. That critique does not deny neural adaptation. It denies that the
current instruments have shown any particular neural change to be the *cause* of a strength gain.

## 2.4 The behavioural case for neural adaptation

Whatever the instruments say, the behavioural evidence that the nervous system contributes to
strength gains is strong. It comes from four observations, each hard to explain by muscle alone.

**Strength outpaces size early.** Four weeks of isometric dorsiflexion training raised maximal force
about 14% in young men (289 to 329 N§), with no change in controls [@del-vecchio-2019]. Muscle size
changes little in that time. In a more striking comparison, untrained men trained the leg extension
to failure at either 80% or 30% of 1RM for six weeks. Gains in muscle thickness were similar, yet
1RM rose 27.7% with heavy loads against 9.5% with light ones [@jenkins-2017]. Equal growth with
unequal strength is hard to explain except through the nervous system, or through practice with
heavy loads, which is itself a neural phenomenon.

The evidence on early hypertrophy reinforces the point. Whole-muscle growth can be detected
surprisingly early: quadriceps cross-sectional area rose 3.5-5.2%† by day 20 of flywheel training
in a study of seven people, while maximal voluntary contraction rose 38.9%† over 35 days
[@seynnes-2007]. But Damas and colleagues showed that early increases in cross-sectional area
coincide with signs of muscle swelling. Area was up 2.7%† at week 3 and 10.4%† at week 10, and
ultrasound echo intensity, a marker of oedema, was elevated at week 3 [@damas-2016]. Some of what
looks like hypertrophy in the first three or four weeks is therefore fluid. That makes the
early-strength excess *more* likely to be neural, not less. The tidy Moritani picture, in which
neural gains come first and hypertrophy second, is a simplification. Growth begins early, but the
early strength gain outruns it [@folland-2007].

**Strength is specific to the trained task.** The Rutherford and Jones result above, and the modern
meta-analyses in Section 2.11, show that training improves the trained movement far more than an
untrained test of the same muscles. A change in muscle would show up on any test of that muscle; a
change in how the movement is controlled would not.

**Cross-education.** Training one limb strengthens the untrained opposite limb, without detectable
growth in it (Section 2.8). Because the untrained limb's muscle is unchanged, the gain must arise in
the nervous system.

**Learning curves.** The rapid improvement in a newly introduced exercise, which every lifter
experiences, is a learning curve in the ordinary motor-learning sense [@carroll-2001].

These observations put the existence of neural adaptation beyond reasonable doubt (**established**).
What they do not reveal is its anatomy. Both major integrative reviews make exactly this
distinction [@folland-2007; @skarabot-2021-review]. The next five sections examine the candidate
sites in turn, from the motor unit upward.

## 2.5 Motor units: recruitment and rate coding

If the nervous system produces more force from the same muscle, it can do so in only a few ways at
the level of the motor unit. It can recruit units earlier or recruit units that were not previously
used. It can drive them at higher rates. Or it can change the muscle fibres' response to the same
drive. Motor-unit decomposition makes it possible to test these alternatives directly. It does so by
separating the EMG signal into the spike trains of individual units, which can then be tracked
across a training programme.

### 2.5.1 The best direct evidence

The landmark study is Del Vecchio and colleagues' 2019 trial in recreationally active young men.
After four weeks of isometric dorsiflexion training, motor units tracked longitudinally in the
tibialis anterior discharged faster at the force plateau, by 3.3 ± 2.5† pulses per second
(P < 0.001†). They were also recruited at lower relative forces: from 26 ± 5% to 21 ± 5% of maximal
force§ [@del-vecchio-2019]. The estimated input-output gain of the motoneurons was unchanged. The
authors therefore located the adaptation in greater net synaptic input, or in altered intrinsic
motoneuron properties, rather than in the muscle. They also noted, honestly, that the absolute
threshold changes may lie within measurement error.

The same laboratory has published further analyses with the identical protocol. A 2026 paper
reported discharge rates about 11%† higher and recruitment thresholds about 10%† lower after the
same four weeks, with the same ~14%† force gain [@del-vecchio-2026]. In 13 people, from the same
group and with the same protocol and outcome, this is very probably a reanalysis of the 2019 cohort
or a subset of it, not a second cohort. The same is probably true of a 2020 study that found a 3.0%†
rise in motor-unit conduction velocity, largest in high-threshold units [@casolo-2020]. Conduction
velocity is a property of the fibre membrane, so this is a *peripheral* change, and it also inflates
surface EMG. These papers add measurements, not independent confirmations.

A 2026 study with the same protocol in older adults (about 70 years) adds genuinely new information
[@casolo-2026]. Maximal force rose 17.6%. Discharge rate rose 8.2% at recruitment and 11.3% at the
force plateau. But recruitment thresholds did *not* change. Compared with 12 young trainees from the
2019 cohort, the older adults showed qualitatively similar but attenuated adaptations, carried by
rate coding rather than recruitment.

The study also estimated **persistent inward currents (PICs)**. These are depolarising currents in
the motoneuron's dendrites, switched on by serotonin and noradrenaline released from brainstem
neurons, that amplify and prolong the cell's response to synaptic input. They are estimated
indirectly as "delta-F", the difference in the discharge rate of a reference unit at the recruitment
and derecruitment of a test unit. Delta-F rose by 1.0 pulse per second, a small-to-moderate change
(d = 0.43), and in 10 of 13 trainees. Its change tracked the change in plateau discharge rate
(repeated-measures r = 0.61, P = 0.021), and the discharge-rate change in turn tracked the force
gain (r = 0.54-0.57). No direct correlation between delta-F and force was reported. The chain "more
PIC, so faster discharge, so more force" is therefore a two-step inference from indirect estimates
in older, untrained adults (**speculative**).

### 2.5.2 What the pooled evidence says, and does not

The only meta-analysis of motor-unit firing after resistance training is easily misread
[@elgueta-cancino-2022]. Seven trials in 167 men were reviewed, but the discharge-rate meta-analysis
pooled only four studies with 82 participants, one of them the 2019 trial above. The pooled
standardised mean difference (SMD) was 0.70 in favour of training: a moderate effect in size, but
not statistically significant. The paper reports P = 0.38 in its Results and P = 0.43 in its
abstract and Discussion, an internal inconsistency. Heterogeneity was extreme (I² = 91%), and GRADE
certainty was very low for both discharge rate and recruitment threshold. The search ended in June
2021, before most longitudinal tracking studies.

This result is sometimes cited as showing that discharge rates do not change with training. It
shows nothing of the kind. A moderate point estimate with a wide interval from four small,
methodologically mixed trials is *imprecision*, not evidence of absence. No newer meta-analysis
exists. The honest pooled summary is that the direction of effect favours increased discharge rate
and the certainty is very low (**contested** at the pooled level).

### 2.5.3 Rate coding at maximal effort

Nearly all of the decomposition work above used submaximal contractions, typically 35-70% of maximal
force. This is a serious limitation for a chapter on *maximal* strength, because the units that
matter most for a 1RM are the high-threshold units recruited only near maximum. Until recently they
could not be reliably tracked there.

A 2026 preprint reports the first attempt to track motor units during maximal contractions across a
training programme [@lecce-2026-preprint]. After four weeks of unilateral elbow-flexor training,
maximal force rose 16%† in the trained arm and 8%† in the untrained arm. Higher discharge rates and
faster conduction velocities in both arms were strongly associated with force gains (R² > 0.7†). The
largest discharge-rate increases were in high-threshold units. If it survives peer review, this is
the most direct evidence yet that rate coding of high-threshold units carries early strength gains.
Until then it is **speculative**. An earlier, peer-reviewed study from the same group found lower
recruitment thresholds and higher net discharge rates in both the trained and the untrained arm
after unilateral training [@lecce-2024].

### 2.5.4 Does the type of training change the motor-unit response?

Two small studies suggest that load and proximity to failure shape *which* units adapt, even when
strength outcomes are similar. In untrained men, heavy-load training raised the firing rates of
lower-threshold units. Combining heavy and light loads instead enlarged the action potentials of
higher-threshold units, a sign of fibre hypertrophy, and lowered their firing rates. Yet 1RM and
maximal isometric gains were similar despite muscle cross-sectional area rising 21%† versus 4%†
[@olmos-2026]. In 19 resistance-trained men and women, five weeks of squat, bench press and deadlift
training at 0-1 versus 4-6 repetitions in reserve produced equal 1RM gains but different shifts in
the relation between firing rate and recruitment threshold [@ruple-2023]. These studies are small
and their motor-unit findings are difficult to interpret functionally (**speculative**). The
second is one of the very few motor-unit studies in trained lifters.

### 2.5.5 What the motor-unit data can and cannot tell us

A higher discharge rate at the same relative force means that the motoneuron is receiving more net
excitatory input, or is amplifying that input more (through PICs). A lower recruitment threshold
brings large units into play earlier. Both would raise force. But motor-unit data cannot reveal
*where the extra input comes from*. The candidates include the motor cortex, the reticulospinal
tract, brainstem neuromodulation, reduced inhibition, and altered sensory feedback from the
muscle; all of them converge on the same motoneurons [@skarabot-2021-review]. The overall
judgement: in untrained people, in single muscles, in isometric tasks, short-term training raises
discharge rates and probably lowers recruitment thresholds. The direction is **promising**. The
evidence comes almost entirely from one laboratory group, with no independent replication. Whether
the same happens in trained lifters performing multi-joint lifts is unknown.

## 2.6 Rate of force development: a different question

Maximal strength is the peak force a person can produce. **Rate of force development (RFD)** is how
quickly force rises in the first few hundred milliseconds of an explosive effort. The two are
related but not the same. Early RFD (the first 50-100 ms) depends heavily on neural drive,
especially the initial discharge rates of motor units, and on the muscle's intrinsic contractile
speed. Later RFD increasingly reflects maximal strength itself.

The classic short-term finding is Aagaard and colleagues' 2002 study of untrained men. Fourteen
weeks of heavy training raised maximal torque from 291 to 339 N·m† and RFD across the first 200 ms,
for example from 1,601 to 2,020 N·m/s† at 30 ms. Early-phase EMG rose 22-143†%, and RFD *relative*
to maximal torque rose 15%† in the earliest phase [@aagaard-2002]. The neural interpretation rested
on surface EMG.

Long-term cross-sectional data tell a different story. Men with at least three years of
maximum-strength training had 41-64% higher absolute explosive torque over 25-150 ms than untrained
men. But relative to their maximum, their explosive torque was 11-16% *lower* from 100 ms onward,
and their electrically evoked contractions were slower (octet time-to-peak torque 8% longer).
Explosive EMG normalised to the maximal M-wave did not differ (effect size 0.03 at 0-50 ms)
[@balshaw-2022]. A companion study from the same laboratory found that chronically trained men did
discharge their motor units faster at the onset of rapid contractions. Initial rates were 74 [68,
80] versus 56 [50, 63] pulses per second in the biceps (d = 0.86) and 102 [90, 115] versus 76 [63,
90] in the vastus lateralis. Yet their relative RFD was no better [@skarabot-2024]. The groups were
small (13 versus 12, and 11 versus 10).

One interpretation reconciles the two. Long-term heavy-strength training builds a larger muscle that
is slower-contracting *relative to its own maximum*. Any extra early neural drive offsets this
slowing rather than exceeding it. That reading is plausible, but both long-term studies are
cross-sectional, so selection cannot be excluded and the causal wording "training produces" is not
licensed. The short-term and long-term data also point in opposite directions for relative early
RFD. That disagreement should be stated, not resolved by fiat (**promising** for the cross-sectional
pattern; the cause is unknown). For a lifter whose sport demands rapid force, the practical point is
that maximal-strength training alone may not maximise *relative* explosive capacity (Section 2.15).

## 2.7 Where in the nervous system? Cortex, spinal cord and brainstem

If neural drive to the muscle increases, the change must happen somewhere: in the motor cortex, in
the descending pathways, in the spinal circuits around the motoneuron, or in the motoneuron itself.
This is the most methodologically difficult question in the field, and the evidence is genuinely
conflicting.

### 2.7.1 What TMS meta-analyses agree and disagree on

Transcranial magnetic stimulation over the motor cortex produces a motor evoked potential (MEP) in
the target muscle. Its size indexes corticospinal excitability. Paired-pulse protocols measure
**short-interval intracortical inhibition (SICI)**, an index of GABA-mediated inhibitory circuits in
the cortex. The **cortical silent period**, a pause in ongoing EMG after a stimulus delivered during
contraction, also reflects inhibition.

A 2017 meta-analysis of 19 studies, mostly in untrained people, found that strength rose by SMD 0.84†
(0.55 to 1.13†). SICI fell (SMD -1.00†, -1.84 to -0.17†) and the silent period shortened (SMD
-0.66†, -1.00 to -0.32†). Corticospinal excitability rose only borderline (SMD 0.27†, 0.00 to 0.54†)
[@kidgell-2017]. A 2020 meta-analysis of 30 randomised trials (623 participants), in which decreases
were coded as positive, found the following [@siddique-2020]:

- MEPs rose during contraction (SMD 0.55†, 0.27 to 0.84†) but not at rest (0.49†, -0.68 to 1.66†).
- The silent period shortened (0.65†, 0.29 to 1.01†).
- SICI fell when measured during contraction (0.68†, 0.14 to 1.23†) but not at rest (0.26†, -0.29 to
  0.81†).
- The V-wave, an index of descending drive to motoneurons during maximal effort, rose (0.62†, 0.14
  to 1.10†).
- The H-reflex did not change, and neither did TMS-assessed voluntary activation.

On this evidence the obvious conclusion, and the one reached by an influential 2021 review
[@skarabot-2021-review], is that reduced intracortical inhibition is the most reproducible central
change. That conclusion no longer stands without qualification. Kidgell 2017 and Siddique 2020 come
from the same research group and draw on overlapping primary studies, so they are not two
independent confirmations. A 2023 multivariate meta-analysis found *no* significant effect on SICI
[@gomez-feria-2023]. It found that the silent period shortened with every training modality, but
that the change did not predict strength. It also found that corticospinal excitability rose after
metronome-paced training and correlated with strength gain (its numerical estimates could not be
retrieved; abstract only). A 2026 meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials (244 adults) found that
corticospinal excitability *rose* overall: SMD 0.59† (0.10 to 1.08†), I² = 69%†
[@li-2026-corticospinal]. And a 2026 two-week study found corticospinal excitability *decreased* in
young adults [@siddique-2026].

The one point of agreement is that the **cortical silent period shortens** after strength training.
SICI and corticospinal excitability are **contested**. Measures taken during contraction tend to
reveal changes that resting measures miss. That fits the broader principle of task specificity: the
nervous system adapts in the state in which it was trained. It also means that a TMS study testing
at rest, or in a task unlike the training task, may miss real change [@skarabot-2021-review].

Why do the meta-analyses disagree? Probably for three reasons. First, TMS protocols vary
enormously: stimulus intensity, contraction state, target muscle, and timing relative to the last
session. Second, the trials are small, so a few studies can swing a pooled estimate. Third, the
analyses differ in how they handle the dependence among multiple outcomes from one study. None of
the meta-analyses is clearly wrong. The literature is simply not yet precise enough to support a
confident statement about the cortical site.

### 2.7.2 The spinal level

One elegant study tried to bypass the cortex. Stimulation at the cervicomedullary junction activates
corticospinal axons directly, below the cortex. If training increased the efficacy of
corticospinal-to-motoneuron transmission, or motoneuron excitability, these responses should grow.
After four weeks of isometric elbow-flexor training, strength rose 12.8%†, EMG 27.8%† and voluntary
activation 4.7† percentage points, but cervicomedullary responses were unchanged [@nuzzo-2017]. The
gain therefore seemed to lie upstream of the motoneuron or in other pathways. The study is small,
though: 21 people were randomised, only 10 trained (7 of them women), and the responses were
recorded only in the resting biceps. "Corticomotoneuronal transmission does not change" is the
conclusion of one small study at rest, and it should carry that weight.

### 2.7.3 The reticulospinal hypothesis

The most intellectually exciting idea in the field is that strength training up-regulates the
**reticulospinal tract**, a phylogenetically old descending pathway from the brainstem's reticular
formation. The corticospinal tract is specialised for fine, fractionated control, such as moving
individual fingers. The reticulospinal tract projects bilaterally and diffusely to many muscles. It
is well suited to carrying a gross "how hard" signal to the whole limb or body.

The evidence comes from primates. Two macaques trained a unilateral weighted pull, progressing to
about 6 kg†, near their body weight, over 8-9 weeks. Muscle responses to stimulation of the motor
cortex and of the reticulospinal tract both increased. Responses to direct stimulation of the
corticospinal tract did not. After a further three months, spinal input-output gain was higher on
the trained side for reticulospinal but not corticospinal inputs [@glover-2020]. The authors
concluded that there were adaptations in the reticulospinal tract "as well as intracortical
circuits" in the motor cortex. So the cortex did change; what did not change was the direct
corticospinal path. A separate recording study, of force coding rather than training, found that
reticular-formation neurons increased their firing almost uniformly with force, while
pyramidal-tract neurons in the cortex were heterogeneous [@glover-2022].

The hypothesis is attractive because it would explain several things at once. A bilateral pathway
would explain cross-education. A diffuse pathway would explain the whole-body, "gross effort"
character of maximal lifts. And it would explain why corticospinal measures change inconsistently.

The human evidence, however, is entirely indirect. The main tool is the StartReact paradigm. A loud,
startling sound speeds reaction time, apparently by engaging reticulospinal circuits; the size of
that speed-up indexes the reticulospinal contribution. In a 2025 study, 15 long-term strength-trained
athletes were compared with 18 untrained adults [@tanel-2025]. The untrained adults benefited *more*
from the startle cue: the group difference in "reticulospinal gain" was P = 0.003. But there was no
overall group difference in reaction time (P = 0.073). The difference arose because the trained
athletes were already faster to the quiet, non-startling cue (129.8 versus 148.7 ms, P = 0.016). The
trained athletes also had larger MEPs at high stimulation intensities (MEP area at 180% of active
motor threshold, P = 0.020) and shorter silent periods (P = 0.035). The paper's abstract swaps two of
these P values relative to its Results; the Results are given here.

The authors read this as a cortico-reticulospinal system already primed, so that a startle adds
little. That is plausible. But a smaller startle benefit could equally mean less reticulospinal
reserve, and the StartReact effect itself has corticospinal contributions. Other recent human
studies are shorter still. One found startle-cued reaction time and RFD improved after two weeks of
training, more in older adults [@siddique-2026]. Another found startle-cued early RFD rose 60%†
after a single session [@akalu-2026]. A third found muscle-specific startle differences in
strength-trained men performing a standing curl [@inubashiri-2026]. No method exists to measure
reticulospinal output directly in humans, and no longitudinal study in trained lifters exists.

The reticulospinal hypothesis is mechanistically coherent and supported in primates. In humans it
is **speculative**. It should be presented to students as a live hypothesis worth testing, not as an
explanation of how lifters get stronger.

## 2.8 Cross-education: strength without muscle

Cross-education is the transfer of strength from a trained limb to its untrained counterpart. It is
the cleanest natural experiment in the field, because the untrained limb's muscle is not trained and
does not detectably grow. Any strength gain there must therefore be neural.

The effect is consistent across syntheses:

- **Pooled percentage gain.** A 2017 meta-analysis of 31 randomised trials (785 participants) found
  that contralateral strength rose 11.9%† (9.1 to 14.8†) [@manca-2017]. The gain was 9.4%† in the
  upper limb and 16.4%† in the lower. Eccentric training gave the largest point estimate (17.7%†),
  and isometric training the smallest (8.2%†). Risk of bias was high, and contraction types were not
  formally compared with one another.
- **Lower limb.** A 2026 meta-analysis of randomised trials against control found 1RM SMD 0.59 (0.22
  to 0.97, very low certainty, from six studies) and maximal isometric strength SMD 0.55 (0.16 to
  0.94, low certainty) [@altheyab-2026]. In subgroup analysis only eccentric training significantly
  raised contralateral isometric torque (SMD 0.37, 0.13 to 0.61). Concentric training's estimate was
  similar (0.33) but its interval crossed zero (-0.09 to 0.74). This shows a wider interval, not a
  demonstrated difference between contraction types. Contralateral muscle thickness did not change
  (SMD 0.01, -0.44 to 0.46; three studies).
- **Upper limb.** A 2026 meta-analysis of 31 trials (1,214 participants) found isometric strength
  SMD 0.42† (0.12 to 0.72†) and 1RM SMD 0.36† (0.04 to 0.69†). It found no contralateral change in
  muscle thickness (SMD 0.14†, -0.23 to 0.51†). Certainty was low to very low
  [@hernando-garijo-2026].

"No hypertrophy" means none was detected. The intervals do not exclude a moderate effect. And not
every trial finds cross-education at all: Rutherford and Jones found no significant contralateral
change after unilateral isometric training [@rutherford-1986]. Even so, the overall picture is
consistent. The untrained limb gains roughly half as much as the trained one, without measurable
growth.

The mechanism is debated. Two families of explanation compete [@manca-2021]. "Cross-activation"
holds that unilateral effort activates motor areas on both sides of the brain, so the untrained
side's circuits are trained too. "Bilateral access" holds that the learned motor programme is stored
where both limbs can use it. At the motor-unit level, the untrained arm shows lower recruitment
thresholds and higher net discharge rates after unilateral training [@lecce-2024]. The 2026 preprint
found higher discharge rates in the untrained arm at maximal effort [@lecce-2026-preprint]. A
bilaterally projecting reticulospinal tract would fit, but that link is conjecture.

One trial hints that transfer depends on skill or load. In 151 adults, six weeks of 1RM practice and
of traditional training raised trained-arm 1RM equally (about 2.4 kg†). But only the 1RM-practice
group increased untrained-arm 1RM (+1.5 kg†) [@dankel-2020].

An expert Delphi panel recommended high-intensity and eccentric contractions and at least 13-18
sessions to maximise transfer [@manca-2021]. That is reasonable advice, but it rests on expert
opinion over a low-certainty evidence base (**convention**). The grade overall is **established**
that cross-education occurs, with low to very low certainty about its size.

## 2.9 Coordination: antagonists, synchrony and skill

### 2.9.1 Antagonist coactivation

When the knee extensors contract maximally, the hamstrings also contract slightly. This
coactivation stabilises the joint, but it subtracts from net extension torque. Reducing it is an
obvious route to greater net strength.

The classic evidence is Carolan and Cafarelli's 1992 study of sedentary young men. Eight weeks of
isometric knee-extension training raised maximal voluntary contraction by 32.8%† without any change
in maximal agonist EMG. Hamstring coactivation fell about 20%† within the first *week*. In the
untrained leg, maximal contraction rose 16.2%† and coactivation fell 13%† after two weeks
[@carolan-1992]. Cross-sectionally, long-term trained men showed 32-38% lower hamstring EMG during
the middle and late phases of explosive knee extension (P = 0.026 at 100-150 ms) [@balshaw-2022]. A
synthesis of 20 cross-sectional studies found generally lower antagonist co-contraction in trained
people, depending on their training background [@santos-2023]. That synthesis is graded
**contested** because of the EMG methods involved: antagonist EMG is especially vulnerable to
crosstalk from neighbouring muscles.

How much does this matter for strength? Over 12 weeks, the change in antagonist EMG did *not*
predict individual strength gains (r = 0.298†, P = 0.123†) [@balshaw-2017]. Reduced coactivation is
real and appears early, but it is probably a small contributor to net joint torque (**promising**,
small).

### 2.9.2 Motor-unit synchronisation

**Synchronisation** is the tendency of different motor units to fire at nearly the same moment, more
often than chance would predict. It was once a favoured candidate mechanism. The evidence is
unconvincing. Trained men showed more synchronisation on one index but not on two others
[@fling-2009]. Modelling suggests that synchronisation does not increase maximal force, though it
might matter for rapid force [@behm-1993]. It is **contested** as a phenomenon and not a
load-bearing mechanism for 1RM.

### 2.9.3 Intermuscular coordination and skill

A maximal squat is not a maximal contraction of one muscle. It requires bracing the trunk,
sequencing hip and knee extension, controlling the bar path, activating stabilisers and restraining
antagonists, all at an intensity the lifter rarely experiences. Much of what is called "neural
adaptation" is motor learning of this coordination [@carroll-2001; @rutherford-1986].

The behavioural signature is everywhere. It appears in the gap between gains in the lifted load and
gains in isometric force, and in the specificity data in Section 2.11. It also appears in the
finding that practising the 1RM test alone yields 1RM gains comparable to conventional training in
novices (Section 2.11.2). What is missing is direct measurement. Almost no study has quantified
intermuscular coordination changes in a multi-joint lift over a training programme. This is one of
the clearest gaps in the field. Coordination is **established** as a phenomenon, and its mechanism
in maximal multi-joint lifts is unmeasured.

## 2.10 From nerve to muscle over a training career

### 2.10.1 The time course

The textbook model says that neural factors dominate the first weeks, hypertrophy then takes over,
and neural contributions are exhausted early. Part of this survives scrutiny. In the first weeks,
strength rises faster than muscle size, and the excess is neural and learned (**established**).
Beyond that, the model needs refining.

Hypertrophy begins early (Section 2.4). And not everyone accepts that neural adaptation stops early.
Pearcey and colleagues argued, from cross-sectional comparisons, that neural plasticity continues
with chronic training [@pearcey-2021] (**speculative**). The Loughborough cross-sectional programme
weighs against this. Trained-untrained differences in that work are mostly morphological. Trained
men's biceps motor units behave like untrained men's at the same relative submaximal force (15-70%
of maximum) [@casolo-2021]. Normalised EMG does not differ [@skarabot-2021-emg; @balshaw-2022]. The
one documented exception is higher initial discharge rates in rapid contractions [@skarabot-2024].

These studies all test single-joint isometric tasks. They cannot detect continuing improvement in the
coordination of a competition lift, which is exactly where an advanced lifter's remaining neural
gains would be expected.

### 2.10.2 Between people: size dominates

Among 52 recreationally active men, MRI quadriceps volume explained about 60% of the variance in
isometric knee-extension strength (r = 0.773†) [@balshaw-2021]. Two refinements of the size measure
did not help. Effective PCSA, which incorporates architecture, predicted strength slightly *less*
well (r = 0.685†). Single-slice anatomical cross-sectional area depended heavily on where along the
thigh the slice was taken (r = -0.04† to 0.72†). The second finding is a warning for any study that
measures "muscle size" at one site.

The trained-untrained comparison of Section 2.2 extends this. Across trained and untrained men
pooled (68 men), quadriceps volume alone explained 81% of strength variance in a stepwise model
[@maden-wilkinson-2020]. That figure is inflated by the two-group spread: within one population the
comparable figure is about 60%. And the 52 untrained men are very probably the same men as in the
2021 study, so the two are not independent. Trained biceps tell the same story. In athletes with
65%† more isometric strength and 72%† larger biceps than untrained men, motor units were recruited
and discharged identically at the same relative force, while trained muscle produced about 67%† more
absolute force per unit increase in discharge rate [@casolo-2021]. The same neural command drives
more tissue.

The grade is **promising** rather than established. The evidence is cross-sectional, in men, for
isometric single-joint strength, and from one laboratory. But its direction is consistent with
everything else known about muscle physiology.

### 2.10.3 Within people: a statistical trap

Between-person data show that bigger people are stronger. They do not show that *growing* makes a
person stronger, or that those who grow most gain most. For that we need within-person data, and
here the evidence is weaker than it first appears.

In 12 weeks of isometric training in untrained men, a regression explained about 60%† of the
variance in individual strength gains. Change in agonist EMG contributed 30.6%†, change in muscle
volume 18.7%† and baseline strength 10.6%† [@balshaw-2017]. Neural drive was the largest single
predictor. The same laboratory later reported a 15-week study of 39 untrained men
[@marques-2025]. Its headline numbers were striking. Repeated-measures correlations between MRI
volume growth and strength gain were 0.92 for isometric strength and 0.89 for 1RM, against 0.56-0.58
for EMG change. Mixed models gave standardised coefficients of 0.88-0.94 for growth and 0.13-0.17
for activation. The title announced that muscle growth is "very strongly correlated" with strength
gains.

But the *between-person* correlations in the same data were much weaker: r = 0.60 for volume versus
isometric strength gain, and r = 0.35 for 1RM. The gap between the two kinds of correlation is the
crux.

**Worked example: why two time points inflate a repeated-measures correlation.** A repeated-measures
correlation removes each person's average and correlates the remaining within-person variation. With
only two time points, each person's data reduce to one change in size and one change in strength.
The repeated-measures correlation then equals the *uncentred* correlation of the change scores: the
correlation computed without subtracting the group mean change. When nearly everyone grows and gets
stronger, that quantity is dominated by the shared mean increase.

The paper's Table 1 gives the numbers. Volume rose 12.7 ± 7.1% and isometric strength 21.6 ± 9.2%.
If the between-person correlation of the changes were *zero*, the expected uncentred correlation
would be:

(12.7 x 21.6) / √[(12.7² + 7.1²)(21.6² + 9.2²)] = 274 / √(212 x 551) ≈ 0.80

Plugging in the reported between-person r of 0.60 adds 0.60 x 7.1 x 9.2 ≈ 39 to the numerator,
giving about 0.92, the published value. The same calculation for 1RM (28.6 ± 12.9%, r = 0.35) gives
about 0.87, close to the published 0.89. The independent verifier reproduced these figures.

The study therefore shows that muscle growth and strength gain *occur together*, which nobody
disputes. It does not show that people who grew more gained proportionally more strength. That
question is answered by the between-person r of 0.35-0.60. This analysis is the chapter
researcher's and the verifier's, not the authors'. The grade for "growth explains individual
differences in gains" is **contested**. The study's participants also overlap with another
discussed in Section 2.12.3, and it was part-funded by a collagen industry body.

Formal causal mediation analysis is the better design. In a large randomised trial of 175 untrained
older women, 10.6%† of the 1RM gain was mediated by growth of the anterior thigh (average causal
mediation effect 0.74 kg†, 0.20 to 1.35†, of a total effect of 7.02 kg†). The lateral-thigh model
was not significant [@kassiano-2026-mediation]. Growth was measured by ultrasound, which, as Section
2.12.3 explains, probably makes this an underestimate. A related reanalysis of an elbow-flexor
dataset found that the variance in strength explained by hypertrophy ranged from 0 to 24%†,
depending on the size measure and the model, and that between-person correlations understate
within-person relations [@vigotsky-2018].

### 2.10.4 Training age: diminishing returns

Gains shrink as lifters advance, and the remaining gains seem to track muscle:

- **Strength athletes versus non-athletes.** Over 21 weeks, 8 strength athletes gained 3.9%†
  isometric force with no increase in quadriceps cross-sectional area (-1.8%†). Eight non-athletes
  gained 20.9%† and 5.6%† [@ahtiainen-2003].
- **Elite weightlifters.** Nine elite lifters improved their competition total by 2.8%† over two
  years, with non-significant changes in EMG, force and fibre area [@hakkinen-1988].
- **Professional rugby players.** Over two years, 20 players' 1RM gains of 6.5-11.5%† were smaller in
  the stronger players (r = -0.57† to -0.71†). Only the bench press gains were statistically
  significant; the squat gains were not. Lower-body gains tracked changes in lean mass
  (r = 0.69† to 0.88†) [@appleby-2012].

These samples are tiny and observational. "Gains are much smaller in advanced lifters" is supported;
"training age caps gains" is too strong (**promising**). Separately, individual responsiveness has a
biological basis. In untrained adults who completed two identical 10-week training periods separated
by detraining, each person's gains in muscle size and leg-press 1RM were reproducible (r = 0.67-0.76)
[@rantila-2025]. That bears on individual response (Chapter 11), not on training age.

### 2.10.5 Morphology beyond size

**Architecture.** Fascicle length and pennation change within weeks [@seynnes-2007]. In long-term
trained men, the extra size is mainly added in parallel (PCSA +41%) rather than in series (fascicle
length +11%) [@maden-wilkinson-2020]. Adding architecture did not improve the prediction of strength
from volume [@balshaw-2021].

**Specific tension.** After nine weeks of training, in vivo specific tension rose about 20%†:
maximal contraction rose 31%† while PCSA rose only 6%† [@erskine-2010]. In a related analysis it
rose 17%† with *no* change in the specific tension of single fibres taken by biopsy. The authors
favoured improved lateral force transmission between fibres as the explanation [@erskine-2011]
(**speculative**). Yet after about four years of training, trained men differ from untrained men by
only 8-9% [@maden-wilkinson-2020]. The likeliest reading is that early apparent rises in specific
tension partly reflect residual neural activation and measurement artefacts (PCSA is hard to measure
precisely), and do not accumulate (**promising**).

**Fibre type.** Resistance training shifts type IIx fibres toward IIa and hybrid fibres. Shifts from
slow to fast types are inconsistent and depend on method [@plotkin-2021]. In trained lifters, nine
weeks of high- or low-load training changed neither fibre cross-sectional area nor fibre type
[@cumming-2025].

**The quality of hypertrophy.** Not all growth is contractile. In trained men, six weeks of very
high-volume training increased fibre cross-sectional area 23%†, while actin and myosin
concentrations fell about 30%† [@haun-2019]. The statistics were borderline (P = 0.052-0.055† for
the main effect). The pattern suggests expansion of the non-contractile sarcoplasm, which would add
little force. This "sarcoplasmic hypertrophy" interpretation is **contested**. But the broader point
that some growth is non-contractile, so that hypertrophy need not translate one-to-one into force,
is well grounded [@reggiani-2020]. Chapter 5 takes up the mechanisms of growth.

## 2.11 Specificity: strength belongs to a task

### 2.11.1 The evidence for specificity

If strength were simply a property of muscle, it would transfer equally to any test of that muscle.
It does not. The most comprehensive evidence is a 2025 meta-analysis of 43 studies (1,660
participants, 72 interventions) of dynamic training that tested both dynamic and isometric strength
[@saeterbakken-2025]. Dynamic strength improved by SMD 0.98 (0.91 to 1.06; I² = 67%). Untrained
isometric strength improved by only 0.42 (0.35 to 0.49; I² = 4%). The gap held in resistance-trained
participants (0.75 versus 0.29). Neither hypertrophy nor change in muscle activity predicted either
outcome in meta-regression (P ≥ 0.222).

Two cautions apply. The SMDs are within-group, pre-to-post effects, and the most common unmet
quality criterion was the lack of a control group, so the absolute values are inflated. The
dynamic-versus-isometric contrast, measured in the same participants, is not affected by this. And a
study-level meta-regression is a weak test of mechanism. Its null result for hypertrophy says little
about whether hypertrophy matters for individuals (**contested**).

The same pattern recurs along every dimension that has been tested:

| Dimension | Finding | Grade | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mode: dynamic vs isometric | Dynamic test SMD 0.98 vs untrained isometric 0.42 | established | [@saeterbakken-2025] |
| Equipment: free weight vs machine | Free-weight training better on free-weight tests (ES 0.655†, 0.269 to 1.041†); machine training better on machine tests (ES -0.784†, -1.223 to -0.344†); no difference on neutral tests or hypertrophy | established | [@heidel-2022] |
| Limb configuration | Bilateral better for bilateral strength (ES 0.56†, 0.16 to 0.96†); unilateral better for unilateral strength (ES -0.65†, -0.93 to -0.37†); hypertrophy similar | promising | [@kassiano-2025-unilateral] |
| Load | High vs low load SMD 0.60 (0.38 to 0.82) for 1RM; hypertrophy load-independent with sets to failure | established (mostly untrained) | [@lopez-2021-load] |
| Range of motion | Full ROM beat partial ROM for strength (ES 0.56†) tested mostly at full ROM; isometric joint-angle specificity greater at short muscle lengths | promising | [@pallares-2021]; [@oranchuk-2019] |
| Velocity and intent | Gains greatest near the training velocity; intent to move fast may drive adaptation | promising (old evidence) | [@behm-1993] |
| Contraction type | Eccentric training gains more mode- and velocity-specific | promising | [@roig-2009] |

Range of motion is treated in depth in Chapters 5 and 7, and its effect on hypertrophy is more
disputed than its effect on strength.

### 2.11.2 Load specificity and the practice effect

Load deserves a closer look, because it shows how specificity can masquerade as a better muscle. A
network meta-analysis found that with sets taken to failure, high loads beat low loads for 1RM (SMD
0.60, 0.38 to 0.82). Moderate loads beat low loads (0.34). High loads had only a borderline edge
over moderate loads (0.26, -0.02 to 0.54; P = 0.068). Hypertrophy did not differ by load
[@lopez-2021-load]. Three quarters of the included studies were in untrained people and none in
highly strength-trained people. The funnel plot was asymmetric (P = 0.014), suggesting small-study
effects.

But 1RM is a heavy-load task, and the heavy-load group practised it. When strength was tested on an
*unpractised* isometric or isokinetic task, the high-load advantage roughly halved and became
uncertain: d = 0.32 (-0.08 to 0.72), with a prediction interval of -0.45 to 1.1, from 10 studies (9
of 10 in untrained people) [@hammert-2026-nonspecific-strength]. The point estimate is still about
half the 1RM advantage, and the interval includes a moderate effect. So heavy loading probably
confers some non-specific advantage, but much of its 1RM advantage is practice with heavy loads.
Hammert and colleagues belong to the Buckner-Loenneke group, which holds a specificity-based view,
but the analysis stands on its data.

What is the neural correlate? In the Jenkins trial, only the 80% group increased voluntary
activation and maximal EMG by week 6. The 30% group, however, also increased voluntary activation at
week 3, and the heavy group's advantage appeared on its unpractised isometric test as well (+28.0%
versus +13.4%) [@jenkins-2017]. One trial of 13 per group cannot establish voluntary activation as
*the* mechanism (**speculative**).

For the thesis population, one result complicates the picture. In 14 trained lifters using a
within-subject leg design, nine weeks of 3-5RM and 20-25RM training produced equal multi-joint 1RM
gains (+21%† in both) and equal growth. Only single-joint 1RM favoured heavy loads (+9%† versus
-3%†) [@cumming-2025]. The multi-joint result is surprising. It may reflect transfer between the
legs in a within-subject design, where both legs take part in a bilateral multi-joint test. One
small trial should not overturn a meta-analysis, but it is a reminder that the load data come almost
entirely from novices. Chapter 3 treats load as a dose variable in full.

### 2.11.3 The skill component of the 1RM

If tested strength contains a learned component, then practising the test alone should improve it
even without the stimulus for growth. Several trials confirm this, at least in novices.

- **Untrained, 8 weeks.** Adults who only performed 1RM attempts gained as much 1RM as those doing
  four sets to failure: the difference was -1.1 kg† for the upper body (90% CI -4.8 to 2.4†) and
  1.0 kg† for the lower body (-0.7 to 2.8†). Isometric and isokinetic changes were also similar,
  despite less growth in the 1RM group [@mattocks-2017-practice-test].
- **Untrained, 6 weeks.** In 151 adults, both 1RM practice and traditional training gained about
  2.4 kg† 1RM. True individual variability was present in the 1RM gains [@dankel-2020].
- **Trained, 8 weeks.** In 25 trained people using a within-subject biceps-curl design, 1RM practice
  matched traditional training for 1RM, while traditional training grew more (muscle thickness
  +0.14-0.17 cm† more). In a second phase, a prior hypertrophy block did not clearly raise 1RM after a
  four-week strength block. The Bayesian evidence was inconclusive, with a mean difference of
  0.72 kg† [@buckner-2021].

Two dose-response findings fit the same picture (Chapter 3). In a meta-regression of 67 studies,
volume-adjusted training frequency had a clear positive effect on strength: 3.27% per additional
weekly session (95% credible interval 2.74 to 3.84). Its effect on hypertrophy was negligible and
uncertain. Strength also showed much more pronounced diminishing returns to volume than hypertrophy
[@pelland-2026-volume-frequency]. More exposures to the task matter for strength beyond total work.
This is *consistent with* a practice component of 1RM, though it does not demonstrate one, and the
authors themselves describe the curves only as distinct. Similarly, in trained men, higher volume
increased hypertrophy over eight weeks but not squat or bench 1RM
[@schoenfeld-2019-volume-trained]. That finding is often cited for "size does not equal strength".
But eight weeks is probably too short for size-driven gains in a trained lift to appear.

The mechanism is not mysterious. A maximal lift is a coordination task performed at an intensity
that is rarely experienced. Practising at that intensity refines the motor programme without any
change in muscle. The skill component of 1RM is **promising** in untrained people, and plausible but
less tested in trained ones.

### 2.11.4 Transfer from accessory and hypertrophy work

The practical corollary concerns transfer. A lifter does accessory exercises (split squats, leg
presses, rows) and hypertrophy-oriented work to improve a competition lift. How much of the
accessory gain reaches the main lift?

The specificity evidence suggests a clear principle. The *neural and coordination* component of the
accessory gain stays with the accessory [@saeterbakken-2025; @heidel-2022]. What transfers is the
*general* adaptation, chiefly added cross-sectional area in the shared prime movers. Transfer is
therefore real but partial, slow, and mediated by size. It needs time in the main lift for the new
tissue to be used.

That principle is **promising**. Direct evidence in trained lifters is almost nonexistent. No
controlled trial in trained lifters measures how much a given accessory gain contributes to a
competition 1RM. Any ratio of main-lift to accessory work is **convention**.

## 2.12 Does adding muscle add strength in trained lifters?

This is the question that matters most to an advanced lifter, and the one on which the literature is
most divided.

### 2.12.1 The "separate phenomena" position

Buckner, Loenneke, Dankel, Mattocks and colleagues have argued since 2016 that the role of muscle
growth in strength gain has been assumed rather than demonstrated [@buckner-2016]. Their argument
has three parts. First, changes in size correlate only weakly with changes in strength. Second,
strength is maintained while size is lost during detraining. Third, low- and high-load training grow
muscle equally but produce divergent strength. In a hypothesis paper, the group stated that there is
"no experimental evidence" that exercise-induced hypertrophy is a mechanism for strength gain
[@loenneke-2019-mehy]. They put the case in a 2019 Sports Medicine debate
[@loenneke-2019-sportsmed]‡.

Their experimental support includes the practice-test trials [@mattocks-2017-practice-test;
@dankel-2020; @buckner-2021], the non-specific strength meta-analysis
[@hammert-2026-nonspecific-strength], and an independent laboratory's finding that adding light-load
work quintupled growth without adding strength [@olmos-2026].

Their best point deserves to be stated fairly. Most evidence that "size causes strength" is
correlational, and it is confounded by specificity. Programmes that grow more muscle usually also
differ in load and in how much the test is practised. Disentangling the two requires a design that
holds practice constant while manipulating growth, and few studies have done that.

### 2.12.2 The "contributory cause" position

The opposing camp includes Taber, Vigotsky, Nuckols and Haun, as well as Folland, Balshaw and the
Loughborough group. They argue that myofibrillar hypertrophy is a contributory cause of strength.
Weak whole-muscle correlations, on this view, partly reflect non-contractile growth that adds no
force [@taber-2019]‡ [@haun-2019], and partly reflect statistics: between-person correlations
underestimate within-person relations [@vigotsky-2018]. Their positive evidence includes the
cross-sectional MRI data [@maden-wilkinson-2020; @balshaw-2021], the within-person analysis (with
the caveat of Section 2.10.3) [@marques-2025] and the Folland-Balshaw debate contribution
[@folland-2021]‡. It also includes the mechanistic point that the same neural input yields more force
in a larger trained muscle [@casolo-2021], and the mediation trial attributing about 11% of a 1RM
gain to growth [@kassiano-2026-mediation].

### 2.12.3 Adjudication: why the camps disagree

The disagreement is not a matter of one side misreading data. It arises from four identifiable
features of the evidence.

**1. How growth is measured.** Most of the trials that found a weak or absent link used ultrasound
muscle thickness. A 2026 study compared ultrasound with MRI in 39 untrained men over 15 weeks of
training [@balshaw-2026]. Thickness was measured at 11 sites (two or three per muscle), so the
comparison was not against a single-site measurement. On average, ultrasound underestimated MRI
growth by more than half for the whole quadriceps, vastus lateralis and vastus medialis: the mean
offset was -7.3% against MRI cross-sectional area and -7.8% against volume. The underestimate was not
significant for vastus intermedius or rectus femoris. Part of the percentage underestimate is
geometric, since thickness scales with radius while area scales with its square.

The substantive finding is different. *Individual* changes in thickness were uncorrelated with
individual MRI growth: r = -0.152 to -0.015 for the whole quadriceps, and non-significant for every
constituent muscle. Before training, thickness was still moderately related to MRI size (r =
0.455-0.525). So ultrasound can tell big muscles from small ones, but it cannot tell who grew more.

The consequence is statistical. A predictor measured with that much noise attenuates any correlation
towards zero, a phenomenon called *regression dilution*. Studies that correlate ultrasound-measured
growth with strength gain, or use it in mediation analysis, are therefore biased toward finding no
relation. Group-mean comparisons are less affected; thickness did detect group differences in growth
in the practice-test trials. The finding has limits. It comes from one cohort of untrained men (the
same men as [@marques-2025]), studied in the quadriceps. Whether it applies to the biceps, used in
several Buckner and Dankel trials, is untested. It is **promising**, and it is the most important new
fact in this debate.

**2. Time scale.** New muscle probably needs time and practice in the specific task before it shows
up in a 1RM. The trials in trained people that found no strength benefit from extra growth lasted
eight weeks [@schoenfeld-2019-volume-trained; @buckner-2021]. The cross-sectional data that favour
size reflect years [@maden-wilkinson-2020; @balshaw-2021]. The two literatures may be measuring
different things.

**3. The specificity confound cuts both ways.** Comparisons of low versus high loads, or of 1RM
practice versus traditional training, pit growth against practice. When test specificity is
removed, the differences shrink [@hammert-2026-nonspecific-strength; @saeterbakken-2025]. That
undermines claims that heavy training produces a "better muscle". But it equally means that the
strength value of hypertrophy will show best on non-specific tests, or after the new tissue has been
practised in the target lift.

**4. What has never been done.** No trial has randomised trained lifters to added hypertrophy versus
a matched control while holding specific practice constant, continued it for six to twelve months
or longer, and measured MRI muscle volume together with a competition-lift 1RM. Mediation analyses
in trained populations do not exist.

### 2.12.4 A defensible statement

In trained lifters, muscle size is the main *between-person* determinant of strength (promising).
Added muscle very probably raises the ceiling for the specific 1RM, but the gain appears only once
the new tissue has been trained in the specific task. How much each unit of hypertrophy adds is
unquantified. It is very probably less than one-to-one, because some growth is non-contractile,
because of architectural and moment-arm limits, and because a fixed neural and skill component does
not scale with muscle. The overall grade is **contested**. The direction of the effect is promising;
its magnitude is speculative.

The two camps are closer than their rhetoric suggests. Both accept that specificity and practice
matter enormously, and that hypertrophy is not the only route to strength. They differ on how much
growth contributes over the time scales that matter to an athlete, and on which measurements to
trust in answering that.

## 2.13 Critical appraisal of the evidence base

The conclusions above are only as good as the studies behind them. This literature shares several
weaknesses, and a reader should hold them in mind when applying any of it to trained lifters.

**Populations.** Almost all mechanistic evidence comes from untrained or recreationally active young
men. The thesis is about trained adults, and the extrapolation is large. Neural adaptations measured
in someone's first four weeks of training may tell us little about a lifter in their tenth year. The
handful of studies in trained people are either cross-sectional [@maden-wilkinson-2020;
@balshaw-2022; @skarabot-2024; @tanel-2025] or short and small [@ruple-2023; @buckner-2021;
@cumming-2025]. Women are under-represented: the motor-unit meta-analysis included only men
[@elgueta-cancino-2022], as did the Loughborough cohorts. Older trained lifters are almost absent,
though one study examined older untrained adults [@casolo-2026] (see Chapter 11).

**Tasks.** Motor-unit decomposition is overwhelmingly performed in submaximal, isometric,
single-joint tasks: dorsiflexion, elbow flexion, knee extension. A competition squat or deadlift is
maximal, dynamic and multi-joint. The gap between the task studied and the task of interest is not a
detail; it is the central limitation of the mechanistic literature. The first tracking of motor
units at maximal effort is a 2026 preprint [@lecce-2026-preprint].

**Sample sizes.** Mechanistic trials typically have 10-15 people per group. Studies of that size can
detect only large effects and produce unstable estimates. Several key findings rest on a single
trial of this size [@jenkins-2017; @nuzzo-2017; @del-vecchio-2019], and the primate reticulospinal
work rests on two animals [@glover-2020].

**Duration.** Most training studies last four to fifteen weeks. That is ideal for studying early
neural adaptation and useless for studying how hypertrophy contributes over a career. The questions
that matter most to trained lifters are therefore the ones the trial designs are least able to
answer.

**Non-independence.** Much of the literature comes from a few laboratories that reuse participants.
Three clusters stand out. The Rome-Imperial tibialis anterior cohort underlies
[@del-vecchio-2019], [@casolo-2020] and [@del-vecchio-2026], and its young group is reused in
[@casolo-2026]. Overlapping groups of Loughborough men underlie [@balshaw-2021],
[@maden-wilkinson-2020], [@skarabot-2021-emg], [@balshaw-2022], [@casolo-2021] and
[@skarabot-2024]. And one 15-week Loughborough trial underlies both [@marques-2025] and
[@balshaw-2026]. The TMS meta-analyses [@kidgell-2017] and [@siddique-2020] share authors and
primary studies. Agreement within a cluster shows that a finding is consistent, not that it
replicates.

**Measurement validity.** Every window on the nervous system is partial (Section 2.3). Surface EMG
is confounded by muscle size between people [@skarabot-2021-emg]. TMS measures are sensitive to
protocol and contraction state, and only weakly linked to behaviour [@hortobagyi-2021]. Startle
paradigms are ambiguous [@tanel-2025]. On the muscle side, ultrasound thickness, the most common
measure of growth, cannot track individual MRI growth [@balshaw-2026]. Single-slice cross-sectional
area depends heavily on slice location [@balshaw-2021]. The 1RM itself is a skill-laden outcome that
favours whichever group practised it.

**Statistics.** Several influential analyses use methods that answer a different question from the
one posed. A two-time-point repeated-measures correlation mainly reflects the shared mean change
(Section 2.10.3). Study-level meta-regression cannot test individual-level mechanisms
[@saeterbakken-2025]. Non-significant pooled estimates from a handful of small, heterogeneous studies
are sometimes read as evidence of no effect [@elgueta-cancino-2022]. Pre-to-post SMDs without
control groups inflate absolute effect sizes.

**Publication bias and reporting.** The load meta-analysis showed funnel-plot asymmetry
[@lopez-2021-load]. Most small mechanistic studies that found "no change" in some neural measure may
never have been published. Several primary sources contain internal inconsistencies that the
chapter has reported rather than smoothed over: two P values for one pooled estimate
[@elgueta-cancino-2022], swapped P values between abstract and Results [@tanel-2025], and two values
for specific tension [@maden-wilkinson-2020].

**Read levels.** Many numbers in this chapter were read only in abstracts (marked †), including the
TMS and cross-education meta-analyses and several key primary trials. The primary data for the
reticulospinal hypothesis and for the 2019 motor-unit trial were not independently verified in full
text. A reader should weigh these numbers accordingly.

**Allegiance.** The two camps in the size-strength debate each produce much of the evidence for
their own position, and both are open about it. Their designs tend to reflect their priors. The
specificity camp favours short trials with practised tests and ultrasound. The size camp favours MRI,
cross-sectional comparisons and isometric single-joint tests. Neither design is neutral.

## 2.14 Open questions and promising directions

1. **Does added muscle add 1RM in trained lifters, and how much?** The decisive trial would
   randomise trained lifters for six to twelve months or longer. It would manipulate hypertrophy
   while holding specific practice constant, and it would measure MRI volume and competition-lift
   1RM, with formal causal mediation analysis. No such trial exists. It is the single most valuable
   study the field could run.
2. **Motor units in real lifts.** Motor-unit tracking at maximal effort has only just become possible
   [@lecce-2026-preprint]. Extending it to dynamic, multi-joint tasks, and to trained lifters followed
   longitudinally, would test whether the rate-coding story holds where it matters.
3. **Where is the neural adaptation?** The cortical evidence is contested, and the reticulospinal
   hypothesis lacks a direct human test. Progress probably requires testing in the trained task and
   contraction state, pre-registered TMS protocols, and better methods for isolating brainstem
   contributions.
4. **Neural adaptation in advanced lifters.** All existing data are cross-sectional, so training is
   confounded with selection and genetics. Longitudinal neural measurement across years of training
   is lacking, and so is direct measurement of intermuscular coordination in competition lifts.
5. **What makes a high strength responder?** Individual responses are reproducible [@rantila-2025],
   but whether high responders differ neurally or morphologically is unknown in trained people.
6. **Transfer from accessory work.** Controlled data on how accessory and hypertrophy work contribute
   to competition lifts in trained lifters essentially do not exist.
7. **Measurement.** Validated, practical measures of individual muscle growth are needed (MRI is
   expensive; ultrasound is not adequate for individual tracking), as are EMG normalisation methods
   valid between people.
8. **Women and older lifters.** The mechanistic evidence base is almost entirely young men.

## 2.15 Practical implications for trained lifters

These implications follow from the evidence above, with grades attached. Most rest on evidence from
untrained people, extrapolated with caution. Programming specifics belong to Chapters 3 and 4.

**Expect early gains in a new lift to be largely neural and learned.** When you introduce a new
exercise or variation, the rapid early improvement mostly reflects learning and neural changes, not
new muscle. Do not read it as evidence of growth, and do not expect it to continue at that rate
(**established** for the phenomenon).

**To get stronger at a lift, practise that lift, heavy.** Strength is specific to the task, the
load, the equipment and the limb configuration. Gains in one test transfer only partially to others
(**established**). Heavy loads produce larger 1RM gains than light loads, mostly through practice
with heavy loads (**established** in mostly untrained people; **promising** in trained people, given
one trial in trained lifters that found equal multi-joint gains [@cumming-2025]). Frequent exposure
to the target lift helps strength beyond total volume (**promising**; see Chapter 3).

**Expect accessory work to transfer through muscle, slowly.** The strength you gain in an accessory
lift largely stays with that lift. What carries over to the main lift is added muscle in the shared
prime movers, and that probably needs time in the main lift to be expressed (**promising** for the
principle). Any particular ratio of main-lift to accessory work is **convention**.

**Over a career, building muscle is the main route to a higher ceiling.** Between trained people,
muscle size is the main determinant of strength (**promising**). Whether, and by how much, adding
muscle increases a given lifter's 1RM is **contested**. The best current reading is that it does,
less than one-to-one, and with a delay.

**After a hypertrophy phase, allow time to convert size into strength.** If added muscle shows up in
a 1RM only after it has been practised in the lift, a period of specific heavy practice after
hypertrophy-focused training is sensible. The principle is plausible (**speculative**). The
timing and structure of such phases are **convention**; see Chapter 4.

**Judge progress with more than the competition lift.** Because 1RM mixes skill and muscle, a lifter
who wants to know whether they are building capacity, not just skill, can also track a less
practised test of the same muscles. This is **convention**; no study has validated it as a
monitoring practice (see Chapter 10).

**Interpret measurements carefully.** EMG amplitude comparisons between people largely reflect muscle
size (**promising**). Ultrasound thickness cannot reliably track an individual's muscle growth over
a training block (**promising**). Neither should be used to judge whether an individual lifter
"activates more" or "grew more".

**If rapid force matters, train it specifically.** Long-term heavy-strength-trained men have more
absolute explosive force but less explosive force relative to their maximum (**promising**,
cross-sectional). Velocity- and intent-specific training is the principled response (**promising**,
older evidence [@behm-1993]).

**Expect gains to slow with training age.** Advanced lifters gain much less per unit of training, and
their remaining gains seem to track muscle (**promising**). Small gains are the norm for advanced
lifters, not a sign that a programme has failed.

**Training one limb benefits the other.** When only one limb can be trained, training it maintains
or builds some strength in the other, about half as much, without muscle growth (**established** that
it occurs; size of effect low certainty). High-intensity and eccentric contractions are recommended
by expert consensus (**convention**).

**Be sceptical of "nervous system training" claims.** Methods marketed as specifically training the
reticulospinal tract or "the CNS" rest on a hypothesis that is **speculative** in humans. Proximity
to failure appears to change motor-unit behaviour without changing 1RM gains in trained lifters
[@ruple-2023] (**speculative**). Its practical role is covered in Chapter 3.

## 2.16 Concluding summary

Strength is the product of muscle and its control, and both change with training, on different time
scales and with different degrees of certainty.

That the nervous system contributes to strength gains is beyond doubt. Early gains outrun growth;
strength is specific to the trained task; and training one limb strengthens the other. Motor-unit
decomposition has added direct evidence that, in short-term single-muscle isometric training,
discharge rates rise and recruitment thresholds probably fall. Where in the nervous system these
changes originate is unresolved. The once-favoured conclusion that reduced intracortical inhibition
is the key central change is now contested by newer meta-analyses. The reticulospinal hypothesis is
coherent and supported in primates but speculative in humans.

Over a career, the balance shifts. Between trained people, muscle size explains most strength
differences, with modest contributions from specific tension, leverage and, apparently, little
difference in normalised activation. But "bigger people are stronger" is not the same as "growing
makes you stronger", and the within-person evidence is weaker than its headline numbers suggest. The
size-strength debate turns on measurement (ultrasound cannot track individual growth), on time scale
(eight weeks is not a career), and on specificity (programmes that grow more muscle usually also
practise differently). The decisive long-term trial in trained lifters has not been run.

Through all of this runs specificity. Strength belongs to a task, and much of what training builds,
especially early, is skill in that task. For the trained lifter, the evidence points to a
two-pronged model. Practise the target lift heavily and often to express capacity. Build muscle to
raise that capacity, accepting that the conversion is partial and delayed. Chapter 3 turns to how
much of each kind of training to do, and Chapter 5 to how muscle grows.
