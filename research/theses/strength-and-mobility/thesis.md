# The science of building strength and mobility

**An evidence review for healthy, trained adults**

Literature searched through November 2026 · First complete draft, 25 November 2026

Research, verification and writing by AI research agents (Claude), to a plan and conventions set by
the thesis owner. See Chapter 1 for the method and its limits.

## Abstract

People who lift want to get stronger and to move well through a full range. This thesis asks what the
research literature, searched through November 2026, supports on both, for healthy adults who already
train. Ten literature chapters cover the neuromuscular basis of strength, training dose, programming,
hypertrophy, flexibility, strength through range, connective tissue, concurrent training, recovery and
nutrition, and individual differences; a synthesis chapter draws them together. Each chapter was
researched from the primary literature and then independently checked against full texts, and every
source has a structured evidence record that says whether its numbers were read in full or only in
the abstract. Every claim carries one of five grades, from established to convention.

The evidence supports a simple model. Muscle grows from tension on fibres in sets taken close to
failure, across a wide range of loads, with diminishing returns to weekly volume. Maximal strength
grows from heavy, frequent practice of the target task, and over a career from added muscle, though
how much added muscle raises a given lifter's maximum is unresolved. Gains are specific to the task
and the range trained, and training at long muscle lengths transfers more broadly. Range of motion
improves mainly through tolerance, by stretching or by loading through full range, and usable range
means strength near its ends. Tissues adapt on different clocks: nerves and tendon material in weeks,
muscle in months, bone over a year, while the adult tendon core and cartilage are essentially
permanent. Protein, creatine and caffeine help at the margins; regular cold-water immersion and hard
conditioning close to lifting can blunt gains; and many popular claims, from hormone spikes to
genetic testing and menstrual-cycle programming, are not supported. The main limitation is the
evidence itself: most trials are short studies of untrained young men, so most conclusions for
trained lifters are extrapolations, graded accordingly.

## Reading conventions

- **Grades.** **Established**: consistent evidence from several good syntheses. **Promising**: good
  but limited or indirect evidence. **Contested**: credible evidence points different ways.
  **Speculative**: plausible but thinly supported. **Convention**: practitioner practice with no
  direct data. A grade applies to the population studied.
- **Citations** are evidence-record keys, such as `[@morton-2018-protein]`, each a file in
  `/evidence/sources/`.
- **Read level.** Unmarked numbers were read in the full text of their source. A dagger (†) marks a
  number read only in the abstract. Chapter 2 also uses § (read in full by its researcher but not by
  its verifier) and ‡ (known only secondhand).
- **Intervals** are 95% confidence intervals unless a chapter says otherwise.

## Glossary

- **Anabolic resistance.** A blunted muscle-building response to exercise or protein, typical of older
  age.
- **Autoregulation.** Adjusting load or volume during training from effort ratings or bar velocity
  rather than fixed percentages.
- **Biarticular muscle.** A muscle that crosses two joints, such as the rectus femoris or hamstrings, so
  its length depends on both.
- **Concurrent training interference.** A smaller gain in strength, power or muscle when endurance
  training is added to resistance training.
- **Cross-education.** A strength gain in an untrained limb after training the opposite limb.
- **Deload.** A planned period of reduced training.
- **Eccentric contraction.** A muscle producing force while lengthening, as when lowering a weight.
- **Fascicle.** A bundle of muscle fibres; its length reflects the sarcomeres in series.
- **Flexibility.** The trainable soft-tissue component of range of motion.
- **Hypertrophy.** Growth of muscle tissue.
- **Lengthened partials.** Partial repetitions in the stretched part of an exercise's range.
- **Mechanical tension.** The force borne by muscle fibres, the primary stimulus for hypertrophy.
- **Mobility.** Used in this thesis for usable range: range a person can control and produce force in.
- **Motor unit.** A motor neuron and the muscle fibres it controls; recruitment and discharge rate set
  force output.
- **Myonuclear domain.** The volume of fibre served by one nucleus.
- **Periodization.** Planned sequencing of training periods across a season; distinct from programming,
  the session-to-session prescription.
- **Proximity to failure.** How many more repetitions could have been done when a set ended.
- **Sarcomere.** The basic contractile unit of muscle.
- **Satellite cells.** Muscle stem cells that can donate new nuclei to growing fibres.
- **Smallest worthwhile change.** The smallest change that matters in practice, used with a test's
  typical error to judge individual progress.
- **Stiffness (tendon).** The force needed to stretch a tendon a given amount; Young's modulus is
  stiffness adjusted for size.
- **Stretch tolerance.** How much stretch sensation, and so how much passive torque, a person accepts at
  end range.
- **Taper.** A short reduction in training volume before competition.
- **Training volume.** Usually counted as hard sets per muscle per week.
