# Chapter 7 research notes: Strength through range and practitioner mobility systems

Researcher: chapter 7 literature researcher. Searched 2026-11-24 (PubMed E-utilities, PMC full text,
Europe PMC when available, web search for practitioner sources and SportRxiv). Population of interest:
healthy, trained adults. Almost all mechanistic and architectural trials below used untrained or
recreationally active people; that indirectness applies throughout and is flagged where it matters.

Read levels: **FT** = full text read; **AB** = abstract only; **SEC** = secondary (search extract or
automated page summary). Grades: established | promising | contested | speculative | convention.
Records shared with chapters 5 and 6 are cited by their existing ids; this chapter added its findings
and `cited_in`. DOIs were corrected against PubMed in five shared records (wolf-2025-lengthened-partials,
havers-2025, wolf-2026-longitudinal, pedrosa-2026-review, van-der-pijl-2026).

## Bottom line for the writer (the load-bearing claims)

1. **Training through range improves range.** Resistance training raises joint ROM by a moderate amount
   (SMD 0.73) and about as much as stretching (SMD 0.08 to -0.22, CIs spanning zero; GRADE moderate)
   [@alizadeh-2023-rt-rom; @afonso-2021-strength-vs-stretching]. Eccentric training specifically
   raises ROM (g 0.54, 27 trials; g 0.86 for passive lower-limb ROM)
   [@diong-2022-eccentric-flexibility; @kay-2023-eccentric-rom]. **Established** (RT-ROM), **promising**
   (eccentric superiority). Smaller in trained people (0.43 vs 1.03).
2. **Eccentric and long-length training lengthen fascicles, but the size of the effect is mostly a
   measurement artefact of single-image extrapolation.** Nordic fascicle effects range from g = 0.38
   (panoramic, CI crosses zero) to 1.98 (trigonometric) [@yagiz-2021-nordic-fascicle]; the widely
   quoted g >= 2.58 is a within-group pre-post figure from mixed methods [@cuthbert-2020-nordic-volume].
   Direction: **promising**. Magnitude: **contested**.
3. **Serial sarcomere addition after eccentric training is now directly shown in humans, but only in
   one 12-person uncontrolled study**; early fascicle lengthening (3 wk) is sarcomere stretching, not
   addition [@andrews-2025-multiscale; @pincheira-2022-sarcomere]. **Promising.**
4. **Architectural gains reverse within 3-4 weeks of stopping; strength is retained longer**
   [@timmins-2016-ecc-con-architecture; @carmichael-2022-isometric-eccentric-hip; @andrews-2025-multiscale].
   **Promising.**
5. **Strength is range- and angle-specific; training at long lengths transfers more broadly.**
   Shallow squats barely raise deep-squat 1RM (+9% vs +36% for the trained range); deep squats raise
   both [@bloomquist-2013-squat-depth]; strength tests biased to full ROM favour full ROM (SMD 0.32)
   [@wolf-2023-rom]. Long-length isometrics give gains across angles and shift peak torque to longer
   lengths; short-length isometrics give narrow, neural, angle-specific gains
   [@noorkoiv-2014-isometric-angle; @alegre-2014-isometric]. **Promising** (consistent, small trials).
6. **Full vs partial ROM for hypertrophy depends on which partial**: short-length partials are worse,
   long-length partials roughly equal full ROM, including in trained lifters and in a 297-person
   multi-site trial [@wolf-2023-rom; @wolf-2025-lengthened-partials; @gschneidner-2025-lengthened-partials].
   **Promising.** (Chapter 5 covers this in depth.)
7. **Mechanism of ROM gain is mostly sensory plus reduced stiffness, with a structural component only
   for loaded/eccentric work.** Static stretching raises stretch tolerance (g 0.74) and reduces stiffness
   but does not lengthen fascicles [@ingram-2025-stretching-mechanisms; @panidi-2023-fascicle-meta];
   eccentric training adds a structural component in the trained limb, but its cross-education ROM gain
   in the untrained limb is neural [@kay-2024-cross-education]. **Promising.**
8. **No peer-reviewed outcome trial exists for FRC (CARs, PAILs/RAILs), Kinstretch, ATG as a system,
   or GMB.** PubMed returns zero records for these terms. Their component exercises map onto evidence
   classes that do work (PNF-style contract-relax, loaded end-range training, eccentrics). **Convention**
   for the systems; component-level grades vary.
9. **The single training trial of the 'knees over toes' cue found no difference** between letting knees
   travel and blocking them over 6 weeks of bodyweight squats [@movahhedi-2026-knees-over-toes];
   blocking knee travel shifts load to the hip about tenfold [@fry-2003-knee-position].
   **Speculative** (trial) / **promising** (biomechanics).
10. **Jefferson curl: one tiny RCT and one EMG study.** A programme including a modified Jefferson curl
    matched stretching for sit-and-reach and beat it for end-range hinge strength (n = 6/group)
    [@rosenfeldt-2024-rt-vs-stretch]; the Jefferson curl recruits erectors and biceps femoris more than an
    RDL at the same load [@yona-2026-jefferson-curl]. **Speculative.**
11. **Yoga and Pilates improve flexibility in untrained or older people by moderate amounts; strength
    effects are small, inconsistent and fragile to publication-bias adjustment**
    [@sivaramakrishnan-2019-yoga; @guo-2026-yoga-older; @bajcetic-2026-pilates-women; @campos-2016-pilates].
    No trial in trained strength athletes. **Promising** (flexibility, untrained); **contested** (strength).
12. **Measurement: ROM is not flexibility, and passive ROM is not usable range.** Active and passive ROM
    can respond differently [@lopez-bedoya-2013-active-passive]; ROM includes non-modifiable bony limits
    [@afonso-2026-rom-vs-flexibility]; flexibility scores predict little in healthy people
    [@nuzzo-2020-retiring-flexibility] (contested). End-range strength (isometric force at a long joint
    angle) is the most defensible "mobility" outcome but is rarely measured. **Convention/speculative.**

## RQ1. Does eccentric or long-length training remodel muscle architecture (fascicle length) and improve ROM? How robust is the evidence?

### Findings: fascicle length

- **Nordic hamstring exercise (NHE) lengthens biceps femoris long-head (BFlh) fascicles.** Bourne 2017
  RCT (n = 30 recreationally active men, 10 wk): NHE and 45-degree hip extension both lengthened BFlh
  fascicles vs baseline (post d = 1.77-2.17), not different between exercises; hip extension grew BFlh
  more (d = 1.03) [@bourne-2017-nordic-architecture] (AB, promising). Alonso-Fernandez 2018 (n = 23,
  uncontrolled, 8 wk + 4 wk detraining): fascicle length d = 2.28, reversed with detraining (d = 1.79)
  [@alonso-fernandez-2018-nordic] (AB, promising). Contraction mode matters: eccentric-only isokinetic
  training lengthened (d 2.65-2.98) and concentric-only shortened (d -0.96 to -1.62) BFlh fascicles
  [@timmins-2016-ecc-con-architecture] (AB, promising). Eccentric, not isometric, hip extension
  lengthened fascicles (+19.7%, d 1.57) [@carmichael-2022-isometric-eccentric-hip] (AB, promising).
- **Pooled magnitude: highly method-dependent (the key robustness point).**
  - Cuthbert et al. 2020 (authorship: Cuthbert M first, McMahon JJ third; *not* "McMahon et al."):
    13 studies, very large within-group pre-post effects: eccentric strength g 2.12 (high volume) and
    2.28 (low volume), fascicle length g >= 2.58, pennation reduction g >= 1.31; I2 88% for architecture;
    no volume trend; ~6 wk minimum [@cuthbert-2020-nordic-volume] (FT, contested). These are pre-post,
    uncontrolled effect sizes; mean TESTEX 6/15; publication bias via fail-safe N only; criticised in a
    published comment (Alt et al. 2020, PMID 31820377, not read).
  - Yagiz et al. 2021: 8 RCTs vs controls, split by method. Eccentric training: g 0.29 (95% CI -0.26 to
    0.85) manual linear extrapolation; 0.72 (0.17 to 1.28) panoramic ultrasound; 2.20 (0.99 to 3.41)
    trigonometric equation. NHE only: 0.23 (-1.02 to 1.47), 0.38 (-0.50 to 1.27), 1.98 (0.52 to 3.44).
    GRADE very low to low [@yagiz-2021-nordic-fascicle] (FT). The method most prone to overestimation
    (single-image trigonometric) produces the "large" effect; panoramic (closest to direct) does not show
    a significant NHE effect.
  - Cholp & Zemkova 2025 (11 soccer studies, controlled g): high-volume NHE fascicle length g 0.43
    (0.20 to 0.65), thickness 0.48; low-volume NHE raised eccentric strength (g 0.46) but not
    architecture [@cholp-2025-nordic-volume] (FT, speculative). Contradicts Cuthbert's "volume doesn't
    matter"; the discrepancy is metric (controlled vs pre-post) and population.
  - Systematic review 2026 (published version of the 2024 SportRxiv preprint): in 8 longer- vs
    shorter-length RT studies, 7 used linear extrapolation; no human study had measured serial sarcomere
    number after long- vs short-length training [@wolf-2026-longitudinal] (FT by chapter 5; established
    as a statement about the literature).
- **Long-length and full-ROM dynamic training also lengthens vastus lateralis fascicles.** Full ROM vs
    TUT-matched short partial: fascicle length +4.9% with full ROM, PCSA +17.7% with partial
    [@valamatos-2018-rom] (AB); long vs short ROM: fascicle length +23% vs +10% [@mcmahon-2014-rom] (AB);
    lengthened partials > full ROM and short partials for fascicle length [@mcmahon-2026] (AB). Isometric
    training at long vs short knee angles: fascicle length +5.4% in both groups [@noorkoiv-2014-isometric-angle]
    or unchanged [@alegre-2014-isometric]. Static stretching alone: trivial resting fascicle change
    (SMD 0.17, 0.01 to 0.33), only with high volume and intensity [@panidi-2023-fascicle-meta]; no
    significant effect in the larger 65-study synthesis [@ingram-2025-stretching-mechanisms].
- **Machine lengthened-state eccentrics vs NHE**: hip-flexed (lengthened) eccentric knee flexion grew
  hamstrings 18% vs 11% and BFlh 19% vs 5% (MRI), i.e. the NHE, done at short hamstring length at the hip,
  is not the best long-length stimulus for BFlh [@maeo-2024-nordic] (FT by ch5, promising).

### Findings: sarcomeres (the 2022-2026 advance)

- 3 weeks NHE (n = 10, uncontrolled): distal fascicle +21% explained by sarcomere length +17%; serial
  sarcomere number unchanged [@pincheira-2022-sarcomere] (FT, promising).
- 9 weeks NHE (n = 12, uncontrolled; microendoscopy + 3D ultrasound): fascicle length +19% central / +33%
  distal; serial sarcomere number +25% / +49%; BFlh volume +8%; strength +40%; 3 weeks detraining:
  fascicle -12% / -16% with sarcomere-number loss [@andrews-2025-multiscale] (FT, promising). First direct
  human evidence of sarcomerogenesis from training.
- Same cohort, during the NHE: peak torque occurred at 37% more knee extension and 4% longer MTU; fascicles
  operated 25% longer at peak torque; estimated active sarcomere length unchanged, i.e. the muscle can
  produce peak force at longer lengths without operating further down the descending limb
  [@andrews-2026-nhe-mechanics] (FT, promising). This is the cleanest mechanistic basis for "strength at
  long length".
- Rat casting: ultrasound fascicle length overestimated serial sarcomere change (soleus +11% by
  ultrasound vs +6% sarcomere number; gastrocnemius ultrasound changes with no sarcomere change)
  [@hinks-2023-ultrasound-ssn] (FT, promising). Human ultrasound "fascicle" gains should be read as upper
  bounds on structural lengthening.

### Findings: ROM from eccentric training

- O'Sullivan 2012: 6 RCTs, all positive, no pooling [@osullivan-2012-eccentric-flexibility] (AB,
  promising; superseded).
- Diong 2022 update: 27 trials, 911 participants: g 0.54 (0.34 to 0.74), I2 50%, GRADE low to high
  [@diong-2022-eccentric-flexibility] (AB, established for direction). Caveat: pooled joint ROM with
  fascicle length under "flexibility" and mixed comparators.
- Kay 2023: 17 studies, 376 participants, passive lower-limb ROM g 0.86 (0.65 to 1.08); dorsiflexion 1.12,
  knee extension 0.82, knee flexion 0.41; isokinetic 1.07 vs isotonic 0.77 [@kay-2023-eccentric-rom] (AB,
  promising).
- Eccentric vs static stretching head to head: in hamstrings (10 wk, within-subject, n = 11) ROM gains and
  shear-modulus reductions were similar, but only eccentrics added strength and volume
  [@kawama-2026-ecc-vs-stretch-hamstring] (AB, promising). Acutely, eccentric contractions gave more than
  twice the ROM gain of time-matched stretching, with larger stretch-tolerance gains
  [@kay-2026-eccentric-vs-stretch] (FT, speculative).
- General RT vs stretching: no difference in ROM gains (Afonso 2021: ES -0.22, -0.55 to 0.12, 11 RCTs,
  452 participants, GRADE moderate; Alizadeh 2023: RT vs control 0.73; RT vs stretching 0.08; trained
  0.43 vs untrained 1.03; body-weight-only RT not significant)
  [@afonso-2021-strength-vs-stretching; @alizadeh-2023-rt-rom; @morton-2011-rt-vs-stretching] (FT,
  established/promising).

### Mechanisms (RQ1)

- Eccentric loading imposes high force at long fascicle lengths; strain on sarcomeres beyond optimum is
  thought to trigger serial addition (animal downhill-running and casting models; human evidence now
  [@andrews-2025-multiscale]). Early on, longer resting sarcomere length (and probably altered passive
  tension or connective tissue) produces apparent lengthening [@pincheira-2022-sarcomere]; the authors'
  review frames early sarcomere stretching (which may reduce force at long lengths) and later serial
  addition (which restores it) as distinct phases, and notes strength outlasts fascicle gains in
  detraining [@andrews-2025-hamstring-review] (FT). A 2021 review concluded there was then no clear
  evidence that any strategy causes longitudinal growth in humans, with long-length loading and
  stretch-plus-activation the most plausible [@kruse-2021-longitudinal-growth] (FT). Titin N2A/MARP
  signalling via mTORC1 regulates stretch-induced longitudinal growth in rodents [@van-der-pijl-2026]
  (AB, speculative).
- Optimum angle shifts to longer lengths after even one eccentric bout (+7.7 deg) [@brockett-2001-optimum-length]
  (AB) and after long-length isometric training [@alegre-2014-isometric].
- ROM gains after eccentrics are partly neural: the untrained contralateral limb gained ROM and stretch
  tolerance with no structural change [@kay-2024-cross-education] (AB).

### Disagreements

- Magnitude of fascicle change: Cuthbert (g >= 2.58) vs Yagiz (panoramic NHE g 0.38, NS). Resolution:
  method and effect metric. Franchi's group shows single-image equations overestimate (cited in Yagiz).
- Volume: Cuthbert (no volume effect; low volume suffices) vs Cholp (only high volume changes
  architecture). Both rest on between-study comparisons.
- Whether ultrasound fascicle length tracks sarcomere number: Hinks (overestimates) vs Andrews 2025
  (direct sarcomere evidence consistent with fascicle change). Both can be true: the direction is real,
  the magnitude inflated.

### Unknowns (RQ1)

- No controlled trial measuring serial sarcomere number; no data in trained lifters; whether fascicle
  length (rather than strength at long length) causes better ROM or performance; minimal maintenance dose
  for architectural gains; upper-body and trunk muscles almost unstudied.

## RQ2. How does training range (full, partial, deep) affect strength, hypertrophy and flexibility at different joint angles?

### Strength: specific to the trained range, with asymmetric transfer

- Deep (0-120 deg) vs shallow (0-60 deg) squat, 12 wk: deep group +~20% in both deep and shallow 1RM;
  shallow group +36% shallow, +9% deep. Deep squats also gave more front-thigh CSA (4-7%) and more isometric
  strength at 75 and 105 deg [@bloomquist-2013-squat-depth] (AB, promising).
- Full vs half squat 10 wk: full squat 1RM +31.8% vs +11.3% for half-squat trainees; half-squat 1RM
  +24.2% vs +32.0% (ns); full squats grew adductors and glutes more [@kubo-2019] (AB, promising).
- Elbow flexion full vs partial: 1RM +25.7% vs +16.0% (tested full ROM) [@pinto-2012-rom] (AB).
- Meta-analyses: Pallares 2021 favoured full ROM for strength (ES 0.56) [@pallares-2021] (AB); Wolf 2023
  (Bayesian, 23 studies): strength SMD 0.14 (-0.01 to 0.29) overall, but 0.32 (0.14 to 0.49) when the
  test favoured full ROM vs -0.12 (-0.31 to 0.07) when the test favoured partial [@wolf-2023-rom] (FT by
  ch5). Schoenfeld & Grgic 2020 narrative agrees [@schoenfeld-2020-rom-review] (FT). Dynamic RT transfers
  to untrained isometric tests in a task-specific way [@saeterbakken-2025].
- Isometrics: short-length (38 deg) training produced +13.4% force only near the trained angle, linked to
  EMG; long-length (87.5 deg) training gave gains across angles with hypertrophy
  [@noorkoiv-2014-isometric-angle] (AB). 90-deg (long) training: isokinetic strength +23.5% and peak-torque
  angle shifted +14.6% toward longer lengths; 50-deg training shifted it shorter and raised strength
  non-significantly [@alegre-2014-isometric] (AB). Systematic review: long-length isometrics give more
  hypertrophy and more dynamic transfer [@oranchuk-2019] (AB). In trained lifters, long-length isometric
  knee extension grew the quadriceps about as much as full-ROM dynamic training (6 wk)
  [@varovic-2025-isometric] (AB, speculative).
- Lengthened partials: in trained people, strength at full and partial tests was similar to full ROM
  [@wolf-2025-lengthened-partials] (FT by ch5); full ROM had a slightly larger long-length isometric
  (SMD 0.24) and 1RM gain [@havers-2025] (FT by ch5, speculative).

### Hypertrophy (brief; chapter 5 owns this)

- Full > short-length partial; long-length partial ~ full ROM. Wolf 2023 muscle size SMD 0.04 (-0.17 to
  0.25); long-length partial vs full -0.28 (-0.81 to 0.16, favouring partial; exploratory)
  [@wolf-2023-rom]; Kassiano 2023 [@kassiano-2023-rom-review]; regional effect of muscle length trivial
  (SMD 0.05-0.09) [@varovic-2025-muscle-length]; trained-lifter trials and a 297-person cluster trial found
  practical equivalence [@wolf-2025-lengthened-partials; @gschneidner-2025-lengthened-partials;
  @havers-2025]; lengthened partials at lower load matched heavy full ROM for growth and exceeded it for
  fascicle length [@mcmahon-2026].

### Flexibility by range

- Resistance training through full ROM improves ROM about as much as stretching (above). The ROM gain
  appears to require reaching long muscle lengths under load: body-weight-only programmes did not raise
  ROM in Alizadeh's subgroup [@alizadeh-2023-rt-rom]. No trial compares full vs partial ROM training on
  ROM outcomes directly (gap).

### Mechanisms (RQ2)

- Range specificity of strength: neural (joint-angle-specific activation; Noorkoiv EMG correlation),
  skill (motor pattern of the trained depth), and mechanical (hypertrophy distributed to the muscles loaded
  in the trained range, e.g. adductors and glutes in deep squats).
- Broader transfer from long-length training: hypertrophy (more muscle loaded at high tension), shift of
  the force-length relationship toward longer lengths (optimum-angle shift; fascicle/sarcomere changes),
  and tendon/aponeurosis adaptation.
- Why short-length partials lose: less muscle stretch under load, less total excursion, and tests at full
  ROM penalise them.

### Disagreements

- Pallares 2021 (full ROM clearly better) vs Wolf 2023 (trivial difference). Pallares pooled mostly
  short-length partials and full-ROM-biased tests; Wolf modelled test specificity and length. Both agree
  short-length partials are worse.
- Whether long-length partials beat full ROM: early untrained trials suggested yes; trained-lifter and
  large trials suggest equivalence.

### Unknowns (RQ2)

- Very few trials in trained strength athletes; almost none on how range affects competition-lift 1RM in
  powerlifters; no trials on ROM (flexibility) outcomes of partial vs full ROM; upper-body deep vs partial
  pressing data are sparse.

## RQ3. What peer-reviewed evidence exists for ATG/Knees Over Toes, FRC, Kinstretch, Jefferson curls, yoga, Pilates, GMB and Animal Flow?

### Search result summary

PubMed (2026-11-24): "functional range conditioning"[tiab] 0; "controlled articular rotation*"[tiab] 0;
kinstretch[tiab] 0; "knees over toes"[tiab] 1 (Movahhedi 2026); "animal flow" → 3 relevant (Buxton
group); "jefferson curl" → 1 relevant (Yona 2026) plus Rosenfeldt 2024 found by web search; GMB none.
A registered FRC RCT in a clinical population (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03456050, n = 56) was found through
web search; no results were located and clinicaltrials.gov could not be fetched here (SEC).

### ATG / Knees Over Toes

- **Claims** (practitioner newsletter, SEC): knees went from "damaged and fragile to remarkably
  protected"; ATG split squats are "the fundamental reason I can now dunk and do the splits"; sled work
  lets you exert "without increased injury risk"; no peer-reviewed studies cited
  [@patrick-2024-atg-program] (convention).
- **Evidence for the premise**: restricting forward knee travel in a parallel squat lowered knee torque
  (150 → 117 N.m) but raised hip torque from 28 to 303 N.m (n = 7, body-weight load)
  [@fry-2003-knee-position] (AB, promising). The "1000%" figure in secondary sources is this ~10-fold hip
  torque.
- **The only training RCT of the cue**: 60 active adults, 6 wk, 720 bodyweight squats; knees-free vs
  knees-blocked vs control: no differences in the primary outcome or in 21 exploratory outcomes after
  multiplicity correction; control changed similarly [@movahhedi-2026-knees-over-toes] (FT, speculative).
  It neither supports nor refutes ATG's broader claims; no ROM or strength outcome.
- **Component evidence**: deep, long-length loading improves strength at long lengths and ROM
  (RQ1-2); Nordics lengthen fascicles; full-ROM RT ≈ stretching for ROM. So ATG's exercise menu is
  consistent with the evidence; the outcome claims (injury-proofing, "bulletproof" knees, dunking) are not
  tested. Grade the system **convention**, its components **promising**.

### FRC: CARs, PAILs/RAILs; Kinstretch (FRC's group class)

- **Claims** (SEC; official site blocked from this environment): mobility = "controllable flexibility
  across articulations (flexibility plus strength)", "USABLE motion"; FRC "based in scientific principals
  and research" [@spina-2026-frc-claims] (convention). PAILs = isometric contraction of the stretched
  muscle at end range; RAILs = contraction of the opposing muscles to pull deeper.
- **No outcome trial** of CARs, PAILs/RAILs or Kinstretch in PubMed.
- **Nearest evidence**: PAILs ≈ contract-relax PNF; RAILs ≈ agonist-contract PNF/active end-range work.
  PNF produces chronic ROM gains equal to static stretching and larger than ballistic/dynamic
  (PNF -1.283 vs static -1.005 vs ballistic/dynamic -0.550; 77 studies) [@konrad-2023-chronic-rom] (FT by
  ch6). Long-length isometrics build strength and shift peak torque toward long lengths
  [@alegre-2014-isometric; @noorkoiv-2014-isometric-angle; @oranchuk-2019]. So the parts are
  **promising**; claims of unique tissue remodelling or "joint health" from CARs are **convention**.
- FRC's "mobility = flexibility + control" definition is reasonable and overlaps the academic
  distinction between ROM and flexibility [@afonso-2026-rom-vs-flexibility], but FRC has not published a
  validated measure of "usable range".

### Jefferson curl

- **Rosenfeldt 2024 RCT** (n = 18; 6 per group; 8 wk, 3x/wk): straight-leg deadlift plus kettlebell
  "modified Jefferson curl" (hip hinge with neutral spine, then spinal flexion) from a box vs volume-matched
  static stretching vs control. Sit-and-reach: RT g 2.53, stretching g 2.44 vs control, RT = stretching
  (p = 1.00). Isometric straight-leg deadlift at 95% hip ROM: RT +145 N vs stretching -16 N (RT vs SS
  g 2.69-3.36) [@rosenfeldt-2024-rt-vs-stretch] (FT, speculative). Supports "loaded flexion/hinge through
  range improves flexibility and end-range strength" but cannot isolate the Jefferson curl. (Round 0 had
  this as "unverified, secondary"; now verified.)
- **Yona 2026 EMG** (n = 21 trained): higher upper ES (62.8 vs 44.5 %MVIC, g 0.83), lower ES (g 0.49)
  and biceps femoris (g 0.60) than an RDL at the same absolute load [@yona-2026-jefferson-curl] (FT,
  promising as description). Load was set to RPE 16 on the Jefferson curl, so the RDL was relatively easy.
- No data on adaptation of spinal tissues, injury, or dose. Grade loaded-spinal-flexion training as
  **speculative**; practitioner standards (e.g. bodyweight Jefferson curl) **convention**.

### Yoga

- Older adults, 22 RCTs: lower-body flexibility g 0.50 and lower-limb strength 0.45 vs inactive; vs active
  controls strength 0.49, flexibility 0.28 [@sivaramakrishnan-2019-yoga] (FT, promising for older adults).
- 2026 update (21 studies, 1471 older adults): strength benefit fell to borderline after trim-and-fill
  [@guo-2026-yoga-older] (AB, contested).
- Young adults: Tran 2001, uncontrolled n = 10 untrained, isokinetic strength +19-31%, trunk flexion +14%
  [@tran-2001-hatha-yoga] (AB, speculative), the source of many "yoga builds strength" claims.
- Safety: adverse events similar to usual care and other exercise (94 RCTs, 8430 participants)
  [@cramer-2015-yoga-safety] (AB, promising). (Cramer's efficacy reviews are mostly clinical populations
  and were not used, per the chapter's non-clinical scope.)
- No yoga trial in trained strength athletes measuring strength or end-range strength was found.

### Pilates

- Healthy adults, 9 RCTs: abdominal endurance MD 9.53% (2.41 to 16.43); flexibility MD 4.97 (-0.53 to
  10.47), NS [@campos-2016-pilates] (AB, promising).
- Adult women 2026, 12 studies (~540): sit-and-reach SMD 0.69 (0.43 to 0.94), I2 83%; handgrip SMD 0.04
  (-0.63 to 0.72); GRADE very low to moderate [@bajcetic-2026-pilates-women] (FT, promising for
  flexibility, null for strength).
- Alizadeh's RT-ROM meta-analysis counted Pilates among RT modalities that improved ROM
  [@alizadeh-2023-rt-rom].

### Animal Flow / quadrupedal movement training; GMB

- 8 wk Animal Flow-style QMT vs inactive control (n = 42): FMS +1.62 vs +0.33; some active ROM improved;
  no change in grip strength or push-up endurance [@buxton-2022-qmt] (AB, speculative).
- EMG: QMT movements reach 21->60% MVIC; higher deltoid/latissimus than push-ups
  [@buxton-2024-qmt-emg] (AB, speculative).
- Body-weight-only RT did not significantly improve ROM in Alizadeh's subgroup (SMD -0.23, -0.52 to 0.05)
  [@alizadeh-2023-rt-rom], a caution for purely calisthenic mobility systems.
- GMB: no peer-reviewed study found (convention).

## RQ4. Mechanisms that could make "strength through range" work, and claims that go beyond the data

### Plausible mechanisms with evidence

1. **Stretch tolerance (sensory).** The dominant driver of short-term ROM gains with stretching
   [@weppler-2010-extensibility; @freitas-2018-chronic-stretching-mtu]: chronic stretching raises maximal
   tolerated passive torque (g 0.74) [@ingram-2025-stretching-mechanisms]. Eccentric training also raises
   it, including in the untrained contralateral limb [@kay-2024-cross-education].
2. **Reduced passive stiffness.** Chronic stretching g 0.37 [@ingram-2025-stretching-mechanisms];
   eccentric training reduced hamstring shear modulus as much as stretching [@kawama-2026-ecc-vs-stretch-hamstring].
   But eccentric training can *increase* MTU and tendon stiffness while raising ROM
   [@kay-2024-cross-education], so ROM gains do not require a more compliant muscle.
3. **Longitudinal growth (serial sarcomeres).** Now directly shown after 9 wk NHE in 12 people
   [@andrews-2025-multiscale]; allows force at longer lengths without sarcomere overstretch
   [@andrews-2026-nhe-mechanics]. Reverses fast with detraining.
4. **Shift of the force-length/angle-torque relation** toward the trained length
   [@brockett-2001-optimum-length; @alegre-2014-isometric].
5. **Hypertrophy at long lengths** and of muscles loaded in deep positions (adductors, gluteus maximus)
   [@kubo-2019; @bloomquist-2013-squat-depth]; long-length loading probably adds passive tension
   (titin) and mTOR signalling (animal) [@pedrosa-2026-review; @van-der-pijl-2026] (speculative in humans).
6. **Neural, angle-specific learning** (agonist activation, antagonist co-activation, skill in the end
   position) [@noorkoiv-2014-isometric-angle].
7. **Stretch-mediated hypertrophy and strength from very long stretching** (loaded stretching): calf
   1 h/day for 6 wk matched hypertrophy training for MVC and ROM [@warneke-2023-stretch-vs-rt]; pectoral
   stretching 15 min/day 4 d/wk matched RT for strength and thickness [@wohlann-2024-pec-stretch];
   meta-analysis: hypertrophy d 0.20, strength d 0.30, larger with longer durations
   [@warneke-2024-stretch]. Expert consensus: stretching is not recommended as a primary strength or
   hypertrophy strategy; >= 15 min per muscle per session needed if used [@warneke-2025-delphi-consensus].
   Short "loaded stretch" holds of 30-60 s in practice are far below these doses.

### Claims that go beyond the data

- "Mobility work builds joint health / cartilage / capsule" (FRC CARs): no human data (convention).
- "Knees-over-toes training bulletproofs knees": no injury or outcome trial (convention); the one cue
  trial is null.
- "Nordics make hamstrings longer and therefore more flexible by g > 2.5": inflated by method; ROM gains
  are moderate and partly sensory.
- "Loaded stretching builds muscle like lifting": only at doses of 15-60 min per muscle per day in
  untrained/recreational people; trained-lifter evidence is one RCT and one case study
  [@warneke-2025-smh-applications] (speculative).
- "Strength at end range prevents injury": plausible via eccentric-hamstring literature, but this
  chapter's population is healthy trained adults and the mechanism-to-outcome link (fascicle length →
  fewer injuries) rests on prospective associations, not trials of the mechanism.
- "Active (usable) range is what matters, not passive range": reasonable but unvalidated (RQ5).

## RQ5. How should mobility be measured meaningfully?

- **ROM ≠ flexibility.** ROM includes non-modifiable factors (bone, joint geometry, soft-tissue
  apposition) and neural control; flexibility is the trainable soft-tissue component
  [@afonso-2026-rom-vs-flexibility] (FT, convention). Inter-limb ROM asymmetries can be bony.
- **Passive ROM** (examiner- or gravity-imposed end range, often with a torque or discomfort end point)
  mostly measures stretch tolerance in the short term [@weppler-2010-extensibility]. Standardising by
  torque (ROM at a fixed passive torque vs maximal ROM) separates tissue stiffness from tolerance
  [@ingram-2025-stretching-mechanisms; @freitas-2018-chronic-stretching-mtu].
- **Active ROM** (subject moves the joint by own muscle action) captures control at end range. Active and
  passive ROM can respond differently to the same programme: all three stretching methods raised passive
  hip flexion but only static stretching raised active hip flexion [@lopez-bedoya-2013-active-passive]
  (AB, speculative). Afonso's RT-vs-stretching meta-analysis found no difference by active vs passive
  outcome [@afonso-2021-strength-vs-stretching]. The "active-passive gap" central to FRC/Kinstretch is
  intuitively sensible but has no validated norms.
- **End-range strength** (isometric force or torque at a near-end-range joint angle, or the angle of peak
  torque) is the most defensible "usable range" metric: it moved strongly in Rosenfeldt (ISLDL at 95% ROM)
  [@rosenfeldt-2024-rt-vs-stretch], shifts with long-length training [@alegre-2014-isometric] and NHE
  [@andrews-2026-nhe-mechanics], and distinguishes stretching (ROM up, end-range strength flat) from loaded
  training (both up).
- **Architecture measures**: report method; prefer panoramic/extended-field-of-view or 3D ultrasound over
  single-image extrapolation; treat ultrasound fascicle length as an upper bound on structural change
  [@yagiz-2021-nordic-fascicle; @hinks-2023-ultrasound-ssn].
- **Field tests** (sit-and-reach, FMS): composite, influenced by anthropometry and spinal flexion;
  sit-and-reach was the outcome in most Pilates/yoga/Jefferson-curl trials, limiting inference.
- **Does flexibility matter for healthy people?** Nuzzo argues flexibility has little predictive or
  concurrent validity for health or performance and should be retired as a fitness component
  [@nuzzo-2020-retiring-flexibility] (AB); disputed in published comments (Kruse 2020; Mota & Marocolo
  2021). For strength athletes, the relevant question is task ROM (e.g. squat depth, bar position), not
  general flexibility.
- **Detraining**: stretching-induced ROM partly persists after detraining (pre-to-detraining ES 0.47)
  [@konrad-2025-detraining]; eccentric fascicle gains reverse in 3-4 weeks. Measurement timing matters.

## Gaps

1. **Trained strength athletes**: almost every architectural, ROM and practitioner-system study used
   untrained or recreationally active participants. No trial of eccentric/long-length training on ROM in
   powerlifters, weightlifters or strongman athletes was found.
2. **Practitioner systems**: zero controlled outcome trials of FRC, CARs, PAILs/RAILs, Kinstretch, ATG as
   a programme, or GMB; one null RCT of the knees-over-toes cue; one 18-person RCT including a modified
   Jefferson curl. The registered FRC trial (NCT03456050) has no located results.
3. **Serial sarcomere number**: one uncontrolled 12-person study; no controlled human trial; no trial in
   trained lifters; no dose-response.
4. **Fascicle measurement**: method heterogeneity (extrapolation vs panoramic vs 3D) makes pooled
   magnitudes unreliable; consensus standards are lacking.
5. **Active vs passive ROM and end-range strength**: no validated "usable range" metric or norms; few
   trials report active ROM, and fewer report isometric strength at end range.
6. **Full vs partial ROM effects on flexibility**: no direct comparison found.
7. **Loaded stretching at practical doses** (30-120 s loaded holds, inter-set stretching) in trained
   people: evidence limited; effective stretch-hypertrophy doses are impractical.
8. **Maintenance**: minimal dose to retain eccentric-induced fascicle and ROM gains is unknown.
9. **Loaded spinal flexion training**: no adaptation, dose or safety data in healthy lifters beyond EMG.
10. **Yoga/Pilates in trained athletes**: no trials measuring strength, end-range strength or task ROM.
11. Not read (could change details): Alt et al. 2020 comment on Cuthbert; Medeiros 2020 and Gerard 2020
    NHE meta-analyses (known only via Yagiz); Kruse 2020 and Mota 2021 comments on Nuzzo; the 2025
    longitudinal-growth review's full ROPE detail; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03456050 record.
