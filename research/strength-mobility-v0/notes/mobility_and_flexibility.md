# Mobility and Flexibility Training: Current Evidence (2020–2026), incl. "Strength Through Range" Methods

Method note: compiled 2026-11-24 from WebSearch results only. Direct fetches of pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov failed (host not allowlisted in this session), so every figure below comes from an abstract or a search-result snippet, not a full text. Effect sizes and CIs are as reported in abstracts; moderator details and certainty grades were not checked against full texts. Certainty labels used: **ESTABLISHED** (consistent meta-analytic evidence), **PROMISING** (some controlled human evidence, limited or mostly untrained populations), **CONTESTED** (meta-analyses disagree or are disputed), **NO DATA / MARKETING** (no peer-reviewed outcome trials found).

Applied population: trained, self-coached adult lifter returning from non-specific low back pain (NSLBP), aiming at strongman/powerlifting.

## 1. Chronic ROM gains: stretching type and dose

### Takeaway
**ESTABLISHED:** Regular stretching (≥2 weeks) reliably increases joint ROM. Static and PNF beat ballistic/dynamic stretching for long-term gains. Gains plateau at about **10 min per week of cumulative static stretching** (per muscle group/joint as studied), and stretch intensity and session frequency do not appear to matter. People who start less flexible gain more. Gains decay when training stops.

### Cited Findings
- 2023 meta-analysis (J Sport Health Sci; Konrad group, PMID 37301370, DOI 10.1016/j.jshs.2023.06.002): 77 studies, 186 effect sizes. Stretch training done regularly for ≥2 weeks chronically increases joint ROM. In subgroup analysis, **PNF and static stretching produced larger ROM gains than ballistic/dynamic stretching (p = 0.01)**, so the authors recommend PNF or static stretching to maximize long-term ROM. Females gained more ROM than males. The pooled ES was not visible in the snippets. — [PubMed 37301370](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37301370/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/371414297_Chronic_effects_of_stretching_on_range_of_motion_with_consideration_of_potential_moderating_variables_A_systematic_review_with_meta-analysis)
- Ingram, Tomkinson et al. 2024 (Sports Medicine, published 30 Nov 2024), "Optimising the Dose of Static Stretching to Improve Flexibility" (multivariate meta-regression):
  - Acute static stretching has a moderate positive effect on flexibility (Hedges' g = 0.63).
  - Flexibility gains were **maximised at a cumulative volume of ~4 min per session (acute) and ~10 min per week (chronic)**, with no extra benefit beyond that.
  - Stretch intensity, weekly session frequency, intervention length, age, sex and training status did **not** moderate the effect (p > 0.05).
  - Lower baseline flexibility predicted larger gains.
  — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02143-9); [PubMed 39614059](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39614059/); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Optimising-the-Dose-of-Static-Stretching-to-Improve-Ingram-Tomkinson/9b555dbcf0dc27858cfe40d38681aee001f19a77)
- Detraining meta-analysis (Sports Medicine – Open, Nov 2025; Konrad and Warneke among the co-authors): 13 studies, 556 participants, GRADE used. It examines how ROM gains from stretching are lost after training stops. One secondary citation of a Konrad-group 2025 paper gives an average stretch-training ES of 0.93 across 13 studies. The exact decay timeline was not retrieved. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-025-00935-5); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/396558320_Detraining_Effects_following_Chronic_Stretching_training_on_Range_of_Motion_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analysis)
- Delphi consensus of international stretching experts (Warneke et al. 2025): experts agreed that both chronic and acute stretching improve ROM, "although alternatives exist" (i.e., resistance training; see Q2). — [PMC12305623](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12305623/); [Northampton PDF](https://pure.northampton.ac.uk/ws/files/84506540/Warneke_et_al_2025_Practical_recommendations_on_stretching_exercise_a_Delphi_consensus_statement_of_international_research_experts.pdf)
- A 2026 RCT in J Clin Med compared low-volume static stretching with full-ROM resistance training for flexibility in physically active young adults. Only the title was retrieved, not the results. — [doi 10.3390/jcm15155783](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm15155783)

### Inferences
- A practical minimum effective dose for a lifter is roughly 10 min/week of static or PNF work on each restricted region, split however is convenient. Daily frequency is not required, and stretches do not need to be painful (intensity did not moderate the effect).
- Ingram 2024 refines and partly supersedes the older Thomas et al. 2018 guidance of ≥5 min/week at ≥5 days/week. Thomas 2018 was not retrieved this session, so check its figures before citing them.
- A well-trained lifter with near-normal ROM should expect small gains. The largest returns go to the most restricted joints.

### Gaps
- Exact pooled ES and CIs per technique (static vs PNF vs ballistic) in the 2023 meta-analysis were not visible.
- Thomas, Bianco, Paoli & Palma 2018 (dose/frequency meta-analysis) and Behm's 2016/2021 reviews were not retrieved. Their specific numbers are unverified here.
- The detraining meta-analysis's decay rate and retention period were not retrieved.
- No "umbrella review" by Warneke on chronic ROM specifically was found. The 2025 Delphi consensus is the closest synthesis.

## 2. Does resistance training improve ROM as much as stretching?

### Takeaway
**ESTABLISHED (moderate certainty, high heterogeneity):** Resistance training (RT) increases ROM by a moderate amount, and two independent meta-analyses found no significant difference between RT and stretching. Body-mass-only training did not improve ROM. The full-ROM vs partial-ROM question has been studied mainly for hypertrophy, not flexibility.

### Cited Findings
- Afonso et al. 2021 (Healthcare, MDPI): 11 RCTs, n = 452. RT vs stretching for ROM: ES = −0.22 (95% CI −0.55 to 0.12, p = 0.206), small and non-significant, numerically favouring stretching. Subgroups (risk of bias, active vs passive ROM, joint movement) showed no differences. Studies were highly heterogeneous. — [PubMed 33917036](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33917036/); [PMC8067745](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8067745/)
- Alizadeh et al. 2023 (Sports Medicine; online 2022): 55 studies. RT increased ROM vs control (**ES = 0.73, p < 0.001**). Body-mass-only training produced no significant gain. RT vs stretch training: **ES = 0.08, p = 0.79**. Conclusion: "stretching prior to or after resistance training may not be necessary to enhance flexibility." — [PubMed 36622555](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36622555/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01804-x)
- Favro et al. 2025 review: pooled RT effect on flexibility of about 0.63, but risk of bias judged high. This comes from a secondary search summary and was not verified. — [cris.unibo.it PDF](https://cris.unibo.it/retrieve/8b29c758-4216-4e40-b75b-7ae5a3fa44c1/00124278-202503000-00014.pdf)
- Full vs partial ROM (hypertrophy, not flexibility): Wolf et al. 2023 Bayesian meta-analysis found a trivial SMD favouring full ROM for hypertrophy (0.12; 95% CI −0.02 to 0.26). Partial ROM at long muscle lengths vs full ROM came out at −0.28 (95% CI −0.81 to 0.16): directionally favours lengthened partials, but imprecise. — [SportRxiv](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/198); [IUSCA journal](https://journal.iusca.org/index.php/Journal/article/view/182)
- A 2024–25 within-subject trial in trained individuals found that lengthened partials produced muscular adaptations similar to full ROM. — [PMC11829627](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11829627/)
- A 2025 systematic review (ScienceDirect) asks whether training at longer muscle lengths causes greater longitudinal (fascicle) growth in humans. Only the title was retrieved. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666337625000332)

### Inferences
- For a lifter, full-ROM loaded training (deep squats, RDLs, full-ROM presses, etc.) is likely to maintain or improve ROM about as well as separate stretching. Stretching is optional for ROM and useful mainly for joints or positions the lifts do not load at end range.
- Lifting with external load through end range seems to be the active ingredient. Bodyweight-only work did not improve ROM in Alizadeh 2023.
- Heterogeneity is high and most trials are in untrained or recreational people. In trained lifters already using full ROM, the extra ROM from RT will be smaller.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis directly comparing full vs partial ROM RT on **flexibility** outcomes. The 2026 J Clin Med RCT may address this, but its results were not retrieved.
- Few data in trained strength athletes.

## 3. Mechanisms: stretch tolerance vs structural change

### Takeaway
**ESTABLISHED (sensory component) / CONTESTED (structural component):** The best current meta-regression (Ingram et al. 2025) finds that chronic ROM gains are associated with both **increased stretch tolerance (higher maximum tolerated passive torque)** and **reduced overall muscle-tendon stiffness**. Tolerance is widely read as the dominant factor. **Static stretching alone does not appear to increase fascicle length.** Eccentric and long-length loading may do so (see Q6).

### Cited Findings
- Ingram et al. 2025 (Sports Medicine, DOI 10.1007/s40279-025-02204-7), "Mechanisms Underlying ROM Improvements Following Acute and Chronic Static Stretching":
  - Chronic ROM gains were significantly associated with both lower overall stiffness and a higher maximum tolerable passive resistive torque. The authors call this the first meta-regression evidence of its kind for tolerance.
  - The magnitude of these changes was not influenced by stretching intensity, intervention duration, sex or baseline flexibility.
  - Only 8 chronic studies measured both fascicle length and ROM, too few for meta-regression. The review concludes that static stretching alone does not appear to increase fascicle length.
  - Acute static stretching moderately reduces muscle-tendon stiffness (ES = −0.772), with a dose relation to total stretch duration. Moderate and high intensities reduce acute stiffness more than low intensity.
  — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-025-02204-7); [PMC12152101](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12152101/); [PDF mirror](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Mechanisms-Underlying-Range-of-Motion-Improvements-Following-Acute-and-Chronic-Static.pdf)
- PainSci's commentary on Ingram 2025 reads it as "flexibility is about sensation, not muscle length": both mechanisms contribute, with tolerance dominant over the long run. This is a secondary interpretation, and the same commentary calls much of the underlying evidence weak. — [PainSci](https://www.painscience.com/biblio/flexibility-gains-due-to-boosted-tolerance.html)
- A critical recalculation (Warneke et al., 2024) of existing meta-analyses on how stretching affects passive properties and passive peak torque argues that prior pooled estimates need re-evaluation. Only the title was retrieved. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382109417_Critical_evaluation_and_recalculation_of_current_systematic_reviews_with_meta-analysis_on_the_effects_of_acute_and_chronic_stretching_on_passive_properties_and_passive_peak_torque)
- A 2025 narrative review frames static stretching as a multilevel mechanobiological process. This is hypothesis-generating, not evidence. — [MDPI Functional Morphology](https://www.mdpi.com/2411-5142/11/3/365)

### Inferences
- Most of the ROM gained from stretching is neural/sensory (you tolerate more stretch), with some reduction in tissue stiffness. Gains are therefore **specific to position and context** and fade without maintenance.
- For a lifter, the practical point is that "usable mobility" probably needs strength and control at the newly gained range (load it), not just passive tolerance. This is a reasonable inference, not directly tested by these meta-analyses.

### Gaps
- Fascicle-length (sarcomere-in-series) adaptations to stretching in humans remain under-measured. Panidi et al. 2021 (high-volume stretching and gastrocnemius architecture) was not retrieved, so its findings are unverified here.
- Ultrasound and extrapolation methods inflate fascicle-length estimates (see Q6), which complicates structural claims.

## 4. Acute pre-exercise stretching and strength/power

### Takeaway
**ESTABLISHED:** The stretch-induced force deficit is real but **small (ES ≈ −0.21)**. It is meaningful mainly with **≥60 s of static stretching per muscle**. In the 2024 multilevel meta-analysis, explosive/speed tasks (jumps, sprints, RFD) were not significantly impaired. Short stretches (<60 s per muscle) inside a full warm-up have negligible effects. Some of the "deficit" seen in classic studies may be a control-condition artefact.

### Cited Findings
- Warneke & Lohmann 2024 (J Sport Health Sci 13(6):805–819), "Revisiting the stretch-induced force deficit": multilevel meta-analysis with robust variance estimation; 83 studies, >400 effect sizes, n = 2012.
  - Static stretching caused a small, significant loss of maximal strength (**ES = −0.21, p = 0.003**).
  - Effects were **larger for ≥60 s per bout** when compared with passive controls.
  - **Jumping, sprinting, explosive strength and RFD showed no significant reductions.**
  — [PubMed 38735533](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38735533/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2095254624000693); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c14574d2c2f7bb665f2e5d23f940fbc0288f48c3)
- The 2025 Delphi consensus cites a proposed threshold of about **60 s per muscle** for the force deficit, with no such effect shown for shorter durations. — [PMC12305623](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12305623/)
- A 2024 randomized crossover study by the Warneke group was titled "Resistance Training Causes the Stretch-Induced Force Deficit". Its framing is that a comparable acute force drop occurs after resistance exercise, i.e., the deficit is not stretch-specific (inferred from the title; the abstract was not retrieved). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380760777_Resistance_training_causes_the_stretch-induced_force_deficit_-_a_randomized_cross-over_study); [PDF](https://www.db-thueringen.de/servlets/MCRFileNodeServlet/dbt_derivate_00064076/sports-12-00145.pdf)
- Systematic review on stretch durations for athletes with high ROM demands (PubMed 42539555; date not shown): short-duration stretching (≤60 s) gave a small flexibility improvement vs control (0.79%) with trivial performance changes (+0.06% pre-post; +0.48% vs control). Longer holds caused small pre-post performance declines (−1.80%) but trivial effects vs control (−0.02%). — [PubMed 42539555](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42539555/)
- Acute and chronic stretching effects on running economy (Warneke et al. 2025, Sports Med Open) are out of scope for lifting; cited for completeness. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-025-00859-0)

### Inferences
- For a powerlifting or strongman session, brief stretches (<30–60 s per muscle) before lifting are fine if they help the lifter reach positions (e.g., squat depth). Avoid long static holds (≥60 s per muscle, repeated) immediately before max-effort attempts. The expected cost is small, but on a 1RM day small costs matter.
- Behm et al.'s 2016 consensus (short stretches within a dynamic warm-up are fine) appears to have been **reinforced, not overturned**, by Warneke & Lohmann 2024. Behm 2016 itself was not retrieved this session.

### Gaps
- Behm et al. 2016 (APNM) and Behm 2021 figures were not retrieved; unverified.
- No lifter-specific (1RM squat/deadlift) moderator data were visible.

## 5. Stretch-mediated hypertrophy and strength in humans

### Takeaway
**PROMISING but SMALL / duration-dependent:** Pooled across studies, chronic static stretching gives **small strength gains (d = 0.30)** and **small hypertrophy (d = 0.20)**. Meaningful hypertrophy appears to need very long durations (**>15 min per muscle per session**, up to 1–2 h/day with calf orthoses), mostly in **untrained** people and over ≤8 weeks. For a trained lifter it is not a practical replacement for, or a meaningful add-on to, resistance training. Animal data (much stronger effects) do not transfer directly.

### Cited Findings
- Warneke et al. 2024 (Sports Medicine – Open; DOI 10.1186/s40798-024-00706-8), "Effects of Chronic Static Stretching on Maximal Strength and Muscle Hypertrophy":
  - 42 studies, n = 1318.
  - Small stretch-mediated maximal strength increase (**d = 0.30, p < 0.001**). Stretching duration and intervention length were significant moderators.
  - Small but significant hypertrophy (**d = 0.20**).
  — [PubMed 38637473](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38637473/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-024-00706-8)
- Separate 2024 multilevel meta-analysis (Sports Medicine – Open), "Chronic Effects of Static Stretching Exercises on Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy in Healthy Individuals": hypertrophy was moderated by the number of different stretching exercises used. Pooled values were not retrieved; one secondary source notes this analysis found little or no overall hypertrophy, but that was not verified. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-024-00772-y)
- Warneke et al. (sex-differences study): 6 weeks of **daily 1 h** unilateral plantar-flexor stretching with a calf orthosis produced significant increases in maximal strength, muscle thickness and ROM. — [PMC9846774](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9846774/)
- Warneke et al. 2023 narrative review (Sports Medicine), "Physiology of Stretch-Mediated Hypertrophy and Strength Increases": human evidence is **conflicting**. Some studies with up to 2 h/session (calf orthoses or stretching boards) show hypertrophy and strength gains; others show neither. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-023-01898-x); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/372535025_Physiology_of_stretch-mediated_hypertrophy_and_strength_increases_a_narrative_review)
- 2025 review "Implications and Applications of Stretch-Mediated Hypertrophy in Therapy, Rehabilitation and Athletic Training": **high-volume stretching of >15 min per muscle group seems to be required** for hypertrophy. This rests on a handful of studies, mostly in untrained individuals, none longer than 8 weeks. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-025-02237-y); [PMC12460394](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12460394/)
- In a 6-week daily stretching study by Wohlann et al., accumulated 15 min per session produced significant hypertrophy (as cited within the Warneke 2024 meta-analysis). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-024-00706-8)
- Animal meta-analysis (2022): long-lasting stretch induces hypertrophy in animals (e.g., avian wing-loading models), with limited transfer to humans. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42978-022-00191-z)

### Inferences
- For the target lifter, **stretch-mediated hypertrophy is not a practical lever**. Doses that work (≥15 min to 1 h per muscle per day) are impractical, the evidence is mainly in untrained calves, and resistance training (especially at long muscle lengths) is far more time-efficient.
- The plausible applied use is rehab or immobilisation contexts (e.g., maintaining muscle when loading is restricted), as the 2025 review suggests. That use is speculative for NSLBP.
- "Loaded stretching builds muscle" claims in fitness media are an extrapolation from this small, duration-dependent effect. Label them **contested / overstated**.

### Gaps
- Panidi et al. 2021 was not retrieved. I found no 2025–26 RCT of stretch-mediated hypertrophy in **resistance-trained** people, so I cannot confirm or refute claims of null results in trained participants.
- Head-to-head comparisons of long-duration stretching vs RT on hypertrophy (Warneke et al. 2023, plantar flexors) were not retrieved.

## 6. Loaded stretching / end-range strength: Jefferson curl, ATG (Knees Over Toes), FRC (CARs/PAILs/RAILs)

### Takeaway
**General principle (PROMISING to ESTABLISHED):** Loading muscles at long lengths and through full ROM improves ROM (RT ≈ stretching, Q2). Eccentric training, notably the Nordic hamstring exercise, increases biceps femoris long-head fascicle length, though measurement methods inflate the size of that effect.
**Branded systems (NO DATA / MARKETING):** I found no peer-reviewed controlled outcome trials of the ATG / Knees Over Toes programme, the Jefferson curl, or FRC's CARs/PAILs/RAILs as packaged systems. Their individual exercises are reasonable applications of the general principle, but specific claims (e.g., "bulletproof knees", PAILs/RAILs uniquely "expanding usable range") are untested.

### Cited Findings
- Nordic hamstring exercise and fascicle length:
  - Yagiz et al. 2021 (PLOS ONE; 8 RCTs): pooled NHE effect on biceps femoris long-head (BFlh) fascicle length was large (g = 1.09, I² = 79%), but results were **heterogeneous by measurement method**, and trigonometric extrapolation may inflate changes. — [PubMed 34752499](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34752499/); [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0259821)
  - J Sport Rehabil 2020 meta-analysis: NHE increased BFlh fascicle length (ES ≈ 0.97, 95% CI 0.46–1.48) and eccentric strength. — [Human Kinetics](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/jsr/30/3/article-p482.xml)
  - Cuthbert et al. 2019/2020 (Sports Medicine), NHE volume: large fascicle-length effects (g ≥ 2.58), with low volumes appearing sufficient. Note the conflicting attribution: the search summary credited this paper to "McMahon et al. 2019", but the link resolves to the Sports Medicine NHE-volume review commonly cited as Cuthbert et al. Verify the authorship before citing. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-019-01178-7); [PMC6942028](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6942028/)
  - Khairunnisa et al. 2023 (conference proceedings meta-analysis): NHE increased eccentric strength but **not** BFlh fascicle length. A low-tier source, but it shows the result is not uniform. — [Atlantis Press](https://www.atlantis-press.com/proceedings/ichwb-22/125987618)
  - BFlh sarcomere and fascicle-length adaptations were seen after 3 weeks of eccentric training (bioRxiv preprint). — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.01.18.427202.full.pdf)
- Knees Over Toes / ATG:
  - The underlying lab biomechanics is a 2003 University of Memphis study. Restricting forward knee travel in the squat reduced knee stress (~22%) but sharply increased hip and lower-back torque. The magnitude is reported inconsistently across secondary sources ("~1,000%" vs "nearly ten-fold"), and I did not see the primary source. — [Biology Insights (secondary)](https://biologyinsights.com/what-is-knees-over-toes-the-atg-system-explained/); [BodySpec blog (secondary)](https://www.bodyspec.com/blog/post/knees_over_toes_exercises_for_strong_painfree_knees)
  - The ATG programme itself "has not been tested in large-scale clinical trials", while its full-ROM progressive-overload philosophy is consistent with exercise science. — [Biology Insights](https://biologyinsights.com/what-is-knees-over-toes-the-atg-system-explained/)
  - The only ATG "study" found was an informal, non-peer-reviewed 3-week internal project by Mountain Tactical Institute with self-reported knee-pain reduction. It is **not evidence**. — [Mountain Tactical Institute](https://mtntactical.com/knowledge/mini-study-lab-rats-experience-various-levels-knee-pain-reduction-following-3-week-cycle-of-atg-exercises/)
- FRC (CARs, PAILs/RAILs): all sources found were practitioner and coaching content describing isometric end-range contractions at progressively longer joint angles. Proposed mechanisms (motor-unit recruitment, tissue adaptation) are asserted without citations. No controlled trials were found. — [Sportsmith](https://www.sportsmith.co/articles/mobility-progressions-for-rehab-what-we-learn-from-functional-range-conditioning/); [TeamBuildr blog](https://blog.teambuildr.com/its-time-to-learn-what-the-hell-is-functional-range-conditioning-frc); [ThinkMovement](https://thinkmovement.net/2017/10/08/frc-101-an-elegant-framework-for-improving-mobility/)
- Related principle: PNF stretching (which, like PAILs/RAILs, uses contraction at end range) is among the most effective techniques for chronic ROM, per the 2023 meta-analysis (Q1). — [PubMed 37301370](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37301370/)

### Inferences
- PAILs/RAILs are mechanically close to contract-relax / agonist-contract PNF, which **does** have meta-analytic support for ROM. FRC's novelty claims beyond PNF are untested. A fair label: "PNF-like technique with branding; specific added value unproven."
- Deep split squats, tib raises, Nordics, reverse Nordics and back extensions are ordinary RT exercises. ROM benefit is expected from the general RT→ROM finding (Q2), and Nordics have specific fascicle-length and hamstring-injury data. The **brand** adds no evidence.
- **Jefferson curl:** no studies found. For a lifter returning from NSLBP, it is loaded spinal flexion at end range. Evidence neither supports nor contraindicates it (see Q8 on flexion fear). The decision should rest on tolerance and progression, not claimed mobility benefits.
- O'Sullivan, McAuliffe & DeBurca 2012 (BJSM; eccentric training increases flexibility) was not retrieved. Its conclusion is consistent with the Nordic literature above, but it is unverified here.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed RCTs found for ATG, Jefferson curl, CARs, PAILs/RAILs (searches limited to the web; PubMed could not be fetched directly).
- The primary 2003 Memphis squat knee-travel paper was not seen; its figures come from secondary sources and conflict.

## 7. Hip mobility and low back pain; squat depth

### Takeaway
**CONTESTED / WEAK:** The association between limited hip ROM and NSLBP rests on **very low-quality** evidence. Reduced hip **internal rotation** is the most consistent signal. External/total rotation and hip extension show no consistent link. These are cross-sectional associations, not evidence that improving hip ROM treats LBP.

### Cited Findings
- 2019 systematic review (Musculoskeletal Science and Practice), "Is there an association between hip ROM and nonspecific LBP?": **very low-quality evidence** for an association. Limited **hip internal rotation** was the only movement significantly associated with NSLBP, to be interpreted with caution. Hip extension testing should separate true joint ROM from hip-flexor muscle length. — [PubMed 31030110](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31030110); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2468781218303229)
- Lower-limb alignment and LBP meta-analysis (2024): reduced hip internal rotation in people with LBP; external and total transverse rotation not significantly different. — [PMC11466400](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11466400/)
- Elite inline hockey players: external and total hip rotation ROM were associated with (predisposed to) LBP. This is a single sport-specific cohort that partly conflicts with the above. — [PMC7369919](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7369919/)

### Inferences
- For a lifter returning from NSLBP, hip IR deficits are worth screening, but there is no trial evidence that restoring hip ROM reduces LBP recurrence. Treat hip mobility work as performance and positioning support (e.g., squat depth, hip-hinge mechanics), not a back-pain cure.
- **Squat depth:** powerlifting requires the hip crease to pass below the top of the knee (federation rule; not sourced this session). Reaching depth depends on ankle dorsiflexion, hip flexion and hip rotation/stance width, and on anthropometry. I found no studies quantifying the minimum hip ROM needed for legal depth.

### Gaps
- No RCT found testing hip-mobility interventions for LBP outcomes specifically.
- Squat-depth ROM requirements for powerlifting/strongman (e.g., hip flexion and dorsiflexion thresholds) were not researched. Federation rules were not sourced.

## 8. Lumbar spine mobility as a target for LBP and lifting

### Takeaway
**ESTABLISHED (association) / NO DATA (as a training target):** People with LBP show reduced lumbar ROM in meta-analyses. Whether that reduction is a cause, a consequence (protective guarding) or neither is not settled. I found no evidence that deliberately increasing **or** decreasing lumbar flexion ROM improves LBP outcomes or lifting performance.

### Cited Findings
- Spine kinematics meta-analysis (2023; PubMed 37595368): **lumbar mobility is significantly limited in all planes** in LBP groups. Pelvic mobility is not significantly limited. — [PubMed 37595368](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37595368/)
- Lumbopelvic kinematics meta-analysis (2022; 9 studies): reduced lumbar ROM in people with spinal pain, no difference in lordosis angle, and no significant difference in extension and rotation. Heterogeneity was significant. — [PubMed 35345658](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35345658/); [PMC8957439](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8957439/)

### Inferences
- Reduced lumbar ROM in LBP is plausibly a consequence of pain and guarding as much as a cause. Restoring normal, unguarded movement as pain allows is a reasonable rehab goal. Chasing maximal lumbar flexion ROM, or rigidly avoiding flexion, has no direct evidence base. This is my inference from the cross-sectional data.
- For lifting, a neutral-ish spine under maximal load is the conventional technical norm. Its injury-prevention value was not researched here and should be treated as a separate question.

### Gaps
- Not found: trials of lumbar flexibility training for LBP; evidence on lumbar flexion ROM and powerlifting/strongman performance; current reviews on "flexion intolerance" and spinal-flexion fear (e.g., the debate following McGill vs pain-science perspectives). These would need targeted searches (e.g., JOSPT, BJSM 2020–2026).

## 9. Mobility, flexibility and injury prevention

### Takeaway
**ESTABLISHED (for stretching = no general effect):** Stretching does not reduce overall injury risk. This holds for Lauersen 2014 (RR 0.963) and for the large 2026 Sports Medicine meta-analysis (OR 0.945, very low certainty). Strength training does reduce injuries (Lauersen 2014, 2018). **CONTESTED:** a 2024 meta-analysis claiming static stretching reduces muscle injuries (OR 0.37) has been formally disputed and conflicts with the 2026 review.

### Cited Findings
- Lauersen, Bertelsen & Andersen 2014 (BJSM), meta-analysis of RCTs: stretching had **no beneficial effect (RR 0.963, 95% CI 0.846–1.095)**. Strength training reduced sports injuries to less than 1/3 and nearly halved overuse injuries. — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/cgi/doi/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092538); [PubMed 24100287](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24100287/)
- Lauersen et al. 2018 (BJSM; separate paper): strength training is a dose-dependent, safe injury prevention method (an RR ≈ 0.338 is cited across six trials). — [BJSM 2018](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/early/2018/08/21/bjsports-2018-099078?rss=1); [PubMed 30131332](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30131332/)
- "How Effective is Stretching for Injury Prevention? A Large-Scale Systematic Review with Meta-analysis" (Sports Medicine 2026; OSF preprint July 2025):
  - 15 studies in the meta-analysis, ~9,000 participants.
  - **No preventive effect** vs all comparators (**OR = 0.945, 95% CI 0.828–1.078, p = 0.396**).
  - No effect for muscle, tendon or ligament/capsule injuries.
  - Certainty: **very low** for all outcomes except bone injury (low).
  — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-026-02559-5); [ResearchGate preprint](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/394674776_How_effective_is_stretching_for_injury_prevention_A_large-scale_systematic_review_with_meta-analysis); [Sciety/OSF](https://sciety-discovery.elifesciences.org/articles/by?article_doi=10.31219/osf.io/35vs7_v1)
- Takeuchi et al. 2024: static stretching significantly reduced muscle injuries vs control (OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.16–0.85). This was **disputed** in a 2025 comment in Sport Sciences for Health. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/381998537_Stretching_intervention_can_prevent_muscle_injuries_a_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis); [Comment, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11332-025-01324-x)
- A meta-analysis claiming active stretching during training prevents injuries was criticised in a letter: only one included study had injury data. — [J Sports Med letter](https://journalofsportsmedicine.org/full-text/785/eng); [original](https://journalofsportsmedicine.org/full-text/769/eng)
- 2025 Delphi consensus: stretch training is **not** an all-encompassing injury-prevention strategy. This comes from secondary summaries and was not verified in the full text. — [PMC12305623](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12305623/)
- A 2024–25 paper, "Do we need to stretch? Active alternatives and contrasting perspectives on current stretch-recommendations", argues for active alternatives. Only the title was retrieved. — [CISS journal](https://ciss-journal.org/article/view/13958)
- Hamstring-specific: the NHE increases BFlh fascicle length and eccentric strength (Q6). NHE injury-reduction meta-analyses were not retrieved this session.

### Inferences
- For a strength athlete, injury-risk reduction should come from progressive strength training (including eccentric and long-length work such as Nordics), not stretching. Stretch only when a specific ROM is needed for task positions.
- The 2026 Sports Medicine review supersedes older claims of stretching benefit and supports Lauersen 2014's null finding with more data, though at very low certainty.

### Gaps
- NHE and hamstring-injury meta-analyses (e.g., van Dyk 2019) were not retrieved this session.
- No injury data specific to powerlifting/strongman mobility training were found.
- The 2025 Delphi consensus full recommendations (warm-up, injury, agreement percentages) could not be read; the PMC fetch was blocked.
