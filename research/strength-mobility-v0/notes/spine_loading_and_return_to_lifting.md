# Spine Loading and Returning to Heavy Lifting after Low Back Pain (strongman / powerlifting)

> Educational notes, not medical advice. Compiled 2026-11-24 from WebSearch results (abstracts, registries, publisher pages, secondary summaries). Direct page fetching (PubMed/PMC) was **blocked** in this environment (hosts not allowlisted), so most findings rest on search-result abstracts/snippets, not full texts. Where only a secondary source (blog, Physio Network review, news piece) was available, that is flagged.
>
> **Certainty legend used throughout:** **[ESTABLISHED]** = consistent systematic-review/guideline evidence, moderate certainty or better; **[PROMISING]** = positive but small/low-certainty RCTs or meta-analyses; **[CONTESTED]** = evidence conflicts or rests on lab/animal models with disputed transfer; **[ANECDOTE]** = coach/clinician practice with no located peer-reviewed test.
>
> **Red flags (refer out, don't train through):** progressive or bilateral leg weakness/numbness, saddle anaesthesia, new bladder/bowel dysfunction (possible cauda equina syndrome — emergency); unexplained weight loss, fever, history of cancer, night pain unrelated to position, major trauma, or steroid use/osteoporosis (fracture risk). These are standard clinical-guideline screening items; no specific source was retrieved for this list in this session (see Gaps).

## 1. Direct lumbar extensor training (ILEX/MedX, 45°/horizontal back extensions, reverse hypers)

### Takeaway
Isolated lumbar extension (ILEX) reliably reduces pain versus doing nothing, but pooled effects on disability and strength are not significant and it has not been shown to beat other active exercise; it works at very low dose (≈1 set, 1×/week). For disc herniation/radiculopathy the only data are a retrospective cohort and a non-randomised trial — **[PROMISING, low certainty]**. No trials were found for 45° back extensions or reverse hyperextensions as rehab interventions specifically — **[gap]**.

### Cited Findings
- **2025 systematic review & meta-analysis (Scientific Reports):** 8 randomised studies, 381 participants with non-specific LBP. ILEX vs true (no-exercise) control: significant pain reduction (ES = −0.633, p = 0.004); **non-significant** for disability (ES = −0.292, p = 0.190) and isometric strength (ES = 0.967, p = 0.150). — [Sci Rep 2025, s41598-025-90699-5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-90699-5); [PubMed 39984628](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39984628/)
- Same evidence base summarised as: lumbar-extension-specific exercise may not differ substantially from other exercise modalities when compared against active comparison groups. — [Sci Rep 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-90699-5) (summary text in search result)
- **Steele, Bruce-Low & Smith 2015 review (PM&R)** was more positive: ILEX produced significant, meaningful improvements in pain, disability and global perceived outcomes, as stand-alone or within multimodal care; called for head-to-head comparisons with other exercise approaches. This is now partly superseded by the 2025 meta-analysis showing no clear advantage over active controls. — [PubMed 25452128](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25452128/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1934148214014646)
- **Dose:** a trial titled "One lumbar extension training session per week is sufficient for strength gains and reductions in pain in patients with chronic low back pain"; other work describes low-volume (single-set) and low-frequency (1 day/week) ILEX producing strength gains in chronic LBP. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221684130_One_lumbar_extension_training_session_per_week_is_sufficient_for_strength_gains_and_reductions_in_pain_in_patients_with_chronic_low_back_pain_ergonomics); [Steele et al., PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/6001)
- **Set volume in trained males (Steele 2015):** studied ILEX set volume in recreationally trained, healthy males (not LBP). — [PubMed 25861560](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25861560/)
- **Response variability (Steele/Fisher et al., Healthcare 2017/2018):** retrospective pooled analysis of three ILEX studies examining inter-individual variability in strength, pain and disability change; authors note variability had been poorly investigated. Responder figures could not be retrieved. — [PMC5746709](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5746709/); [Southampton eprint](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/416544/1/healthcare_05_00075.pdf)
- **Cochrane (Hayden 2021) includes a small MedX trial:** pain differences at 12 weeks not significant at 1 year. — [Cochrane CD003007 table (search snippet)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7138272/table/CD003007-tblf-0004)
- **Disc herniation/radiculopathy — Golonka et al. 2021 (J Clin Med):** retrospective analysis of 189 consecutive patients with clinically diagnosed lumbar disc herniation and *relative indications for surgery*; 9-week programme, 2×/week, ILEX **in limited range of motion**. 162/168 completers (96.4%) had significant symptom reduction; mean symptom intensity 4.2→1.9, mental-health impact 5.9→2.4 (p<0.001). Results less consistent in patients with high pain; reviewers called for an RCT. — [PMC8198576](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8198576); [Lund portal](https://portal.research.lu.se/en/publications/isolated-lumbar-extension-resistance-exercise-in-limited-range-of/); [Physio Network review (secondary)](https://www.physio-network.com/research-reviews/low-back/isolated-lumbar-extension-resistance-exercise-in-limited-range-of-motion-for-patients-with-lumbar-radiculopathy-and-disk-herniation-clinical-outcome-and-influencing-factors/)
- **2025 non-randomised controlled trial (Sci Rep):** "Isolated lumbar extension exercise alone or in a multimodal program for low back pain and radiculopathy" — exists; full results not retrievable here. — [Sci Rep 2025, s41598-025-22452-x](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-22452-x); [PMC12550044](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12550044/)
- **Posterior-chain resistance training (broader than ILEX), Tataryn et al. 2021 (Sports Med Open):** 12–16 weeks of posterior-chain RT had a significantly greater effect than general exercise on pain, disability and muscular strength, with no difference in adverse events; authors call for comparisons of specific patterns (deadlift, hip lift). — [Bond University record](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/posterior-chain-resistance-training-compared-to-general-exercise-/); [PMC7940464](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7940464)

### Inferences
- For a lifter, the practical message is that direct extensor work (back extensions/ILEX-like loading) is a reasonable, low-risk analgesic tool, but there is no evidence it is *superior* to deadlifts or general strength work; the 2025 meta-analysis supersedes the 2015 "meaningful benefit" framing in terms of comparative effectiveness.
- The very low effective dose (1 hard set, 1×/week) means extensor-specific work can sit alongside a heavy competition programme without much fatigue cost.
- Golonka's "limited ROM" approach (start in pain-free range, expand) is the only published template for radiculopathy and is the closest research analogue to the iso-hold→reps progressions coaches use.
- 45° back extensions and reverse hypers are not isolated lumbar extension (pelvis is free, so hip extensors do much of the work); ILEX evidence should not be assumed to transfer directly.

### Gaps
- No RCTs located on 45° back extensions, horizontal (Roman chair) back extensions or reverse hyperextensions as LBP interventions.
- ILEX in herniation has no RCT; the 2025 non-randomised trial's numbers could not be read.
- Inter-individual responder rates (Steele/Fisher variability paper) not retrievable.

## 2. Loaded spinal flexion (Saraceni 2020; flexion vs extension RCT 2024; Jefferson curls; Callaghan & McGill 2001; stoop vs squat)

### Takeaway
There is no good clinical evidence that lifting with more lumbar flexion causes or perpetuates LBP **[low-certainty evidence, but consistent]**; the disc-injury argument rests largely on porcine in-vitro models with high cycle counts **[CONTESTED transfer]**; trials of *training* loaded flexion (Jefferson curls) essentially do not exist **[ANECDOTE / gap]**; one small RCT favoured extension- over flexion-based exercise for chronic axial LBP at 1 year **[PROMISING, high risk of bias]**.

### Cited Findings
- **Saraceni et al. 2020, JOSPT 50(3):121–130** ("To Flex or Not to Flex?"): low-quality evidence that greater lumbar flexion during lifting is **not** a risk factor for LBP onset or persistence and does not distinguish people with vs without LBP; meta-analysis of 4 studies (1 longitudinal, 3 cross-sectional; 5 articles) using intralumbar angles found no difference in peak lumbar flexion when lifting; PROSPERO-registered. Authors: findings question the "safe lifting" paradigm. — [sciencegate DOI 10.2519/jospt.2020.9218](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.2519/jospt.2020.9218); [Physio Network review (secondary)](https://www.physio-network.com/review/to-flex-or-not-to-flex-is-there-a-relationship-between-lumbar-spine-flexion-during-lifting-and-low-back-pain-a-systematic-review-with-meta-analysis/)
- 2022 cross-sectional lab study in manual workers: the LBP group showed **less** peak intra-lumbar flexion during lifting than the no-LBP group (consistent with Saraceni). — [PubMed 35098885 (search snippet)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35098885/)
- Scoping review: "Insufficient Evidence for Load as the Primary Cause of Nonspecific (Chronic) Low Back Pain." — [PubMed 38270054](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38270054/)
- **Park et al. 2024, Sci Rep 14:2714 (NCT02938689):** 1-year follow-up of an assessor-blinded RCT, chronic axial LBP, n = 56 (flexion 27, extension 29). Mean pain at 1 year 3.78 ± 1.40 (flexion) vs 2.26 ± 2.62 (extension), difference 1.52 (95% CI 0.56–2.47; p = 0.002). Some pain subscales favoured extension; **no difference** in worst pain or function. Authors advocate lumbar extension patterns in therapeutic exercise. PEDro record: concealed allocation, subject/therapist blinding and adequate follow-up **not** met. — [PMC10834418](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10834418/); [Nature PDF](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-51769-2.pdf); [PEDro 75652](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/75652)
- **Jefferson curl — EMG study (IJSPT, 2025/26):** cross-sectional, 21 resistance-trained adults; Jefferson curl at submaximal load produced greater upper/lower erector spinae and biceps femoris activation than an RDL at the same absolute load; level 3 evidence; no established 1RM protocol or normative data; whether repeated exposure produces adaptation "remains untested." — [IJSPT](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/172559-surface-emg-characterization-of-the-jefferson-curl-at-a-submaximal-load-matched-to-the-romanian-deadlift-a-cross-sectional-study-in-resistance-traine); [PubMed 42974576](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42974576)
- An 8-week RCT (Rosenfeldt et al.) comparing static stretching vs full-ROM resistance training including a modified kettlebell Jefferson curl is cited by blogs as matching stretching for sit-and-reach gains — **primary paper not located; unverified, secondary only.** — [bodyspec blog](https://www.bodyspec.com/blog/post/jefferson_curls_spinal_flexion_mobility_guide)
- **Callaghan & McGill 2001 (Clin Biomech):** in-vitro porcine **cervical** (C3–C4) motion segments under compression plus repeated flexion/extension; herniation appeared more linked to repeated flexion-extension than to compression, "at least with younger, non-degenerated specimens." — [PubMed 11114441](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11114441/)
- Follow-up porcine protocols used ~1 Hz, 34,974–84,220 cycles and 260–1,472 N peak load (i.e., tens of thousands of end-range cycles). — [PubMed 19121880 (search snippet)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19121880/)
- Adding axial torque to repetitive flexion-extension encouraged radial annular delamination; repetitive flexion alone encouraged posterior/posterolateral nucleus tracking. — [PubMed 19815318](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19815318/)
- **Stoop vs squat — van Dieën et al. 1999 review:** net moments and compression in squat lifting were equal or somewhat higher than stoop; squat lower only when load fits between the feet (marginal validity); "the biomechanical literature does not provide support for advocating the squat technique as a means of preventing low back pain." — [PubMed 10545622](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10545622/)
- Conflicting later biomechanics: a 2007 model found larger moments, passive forces and compression/shear in stoop than squat — [PubMed 17103232](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17103232/); a 2021 study found stoop produced *lower* total/compressive lumbar loads than squat — [PubMed 34805121](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34805121/); an occupational review found squat lowers L5/S1 shear but raises muscle effort, stoop raises shear — [IJOMEH review](https://ijomeh.eu/Work-related-movement-and-lumbar-spine-direction-a-systematic-review-of-lifting-posture,226804,0,2.html); Kingma 2006: no single technique can be advised for all conditions — [Kingma Phys Ther 2006](https://research.vu.nl/ws/files/43522568/Kingma_PhysTher_2006.pdf)

### Inferences
- The flexion-fear narrative for lifters is **contested**: the mechanistic animal data are real but involve young non-degenerated porcine cervical discs and tens of thousands of cycles, whereas clinical/epidemiological data (Saraceni) find no link between lifting flexion and LBP. Both sides rest on low-quality evidence.
- A reasonable position: loaded flexion is probably tolerable when graded (low load, progressive) in a non-irritable back, but there is no evidence it is *needed* for return to lifting, and it is not a substitute for competition-specific loading.
- Park 2024 does not test loaded flexion training; it compares flexion-biased vs extension-biased exercise programmes in non-athletes, with weak methods. It should not be read as "flexion is harmful."
- Combined flexion + twist under load is the most consistently damaging pattern in the lab data — relevant to stone loading/atlas stones in strongman (round-back by necessity) — but no clinical study on stone lifting was found.

### Gaps
- Washmuth (2022) stoop-vs-squat review was not surfaced by search; not verified.
- No RCT of Jefferson curls (or any loaded flexion training) in people with LBP or disc history was found.
- No direct critique paper of Callaghan & McGill 2001 was found; the limitations listed above are inferences from the paper's own scope statement.

## 3. Resistance training for chronic LBP (Hayden 2021 Cochrane, Owen 2020, Tataryn 2021, 2022–2026 updates, deadlift programmes)

### Takeaway
Exercise is a first-line, moderate-certainty treatment for chronic LBP, but average effects are small-to-moderate and no single exercise type is clearly best **[ESTABLISHED]**; resistance/posterior-chain training ranks well in NMAs and beats general exercise in one meta-analysis **[PROMISING]**; deadlift-based programmes work for many but not for highly painful or low-endurance patients **[PROMISING, single RCT]**.

### Cited Findings
- **Hayden et al. 2021, Cochrane CD009790:** 249 trials, 24,486 people. Exercise vs no treatment/usual care/placebo: pain MD −15.2 (95% CI −18.3 to −12.2) on 0–100 at earliest follow-up, moderate certainty; function MD −6.8 (−8.3 to −5.3), below the 10-point MCID. Thresholds: 15 points pain, 10 points function. Vs other conservative treatments: pain MD −9.1, function MD −4.1 (both below MCID). Certainty downgraded mainly for heterogeneity. — [Cochrane CD009790](https://www.cochrane.org/CD009790); [PubMed 34580864](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34580864/)
- **Hayden et al. 2021 network meta-analysis (J Physiother):** 217 trials; Pilates, McKenzie therapy and functional restoration more effective than other exercise types for pain/function; flexibility exercise for function only; moderate-to-clinically-important effects vs minimal treatment. — [PubMed 34538747](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34538747/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1836955321001028)
- **Owen et al. 2020 BJSM NMA:** Pilates ranked highest for pain (SUCRA 100%); resistance training SUCRA 80% for physical function and ranked highly for pain; heterogeneity and few low-risk-of-bias studies; evidence low quality. — [PubMed 31666220](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31666220/); [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/early/2019/10/28/bjsports-2019-100886.abstract)
- **Tataryn et al. 2021:** posterior-chain RT 12–16 weeks > general exercise for pain, disability and strength; no excess adverse events. — [Bond University](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/posterior-chain-resistance-training-compared-to-general-exercise-/)
- **WHO 2023 guideline (chronic primary LBP):** first WHO LBP guideline; recommends non-surgical interventions including structured exercise and self-care education; advises against routine use of some interventions (opioids, per RACGP report). — [WHO news](https://www.who.int/news/item/07-12-2023-who-releases-guidelines-on-chronic-low-back-pain); [RACGP](https://www1.racgp.org.au/newsgp/clinical/who-releases-lower-back-pain-guideline)
- WHO-commissioned review: exercise vs no intervention probably reduces pain (8 RCTs, SMD −0.33) and functional limitation, moderate certainty; individual types mostly very low certainty (aerobic, Pilates vs no intervention; motor control vs sham). — [PMC10684665](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10684665/)
- **2025 NMA "Exercise prescription for improving chronic low back pain in adults":** Pilates ranked 9th by SUCRA (40.5) behind sling exercise (63.0) and combination exercise (61.6). Author attribution conflicts across sources (Fernández-Rodríguez vs Zhao et al. 2025) — **unverified**. — [PMC12162995](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12162995/)
- 2025 meta-analysis of 57 RCTs on core training types: Pilates best for pain (SMD 0.75), core resistance training best for function (SMD 0.76). — [Frontiers Public Health 2025 (search snippet)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1512450/full)
- **Aasa/Berglund et al. 2015 JOSPT RCT (+24-month follow-up):** 70 patients with recurrent, mechanical nociceptive LBP randomised to low-load motor control vs high-load lifting (deadlift), 12 sessions over 8 weeks with education; both groups improved in pain, strength and endurance. — [JOSPT PDF](https://www.jospt.org/doi/pdf/10.2519/jospt.2015.5021); [ResearchGate 24-mo](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/301563842_High_load_lifting_exercise_and_low_load_motor_control_exercises_as_interventions_for_patients_with_mechanical_low_back_pain_A_randomized_controlled_trial_with_24-month_follow-up); [NCT01061632](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01061632)
- **Berglund secondary analysis ("Which patients benefit from deadlift training?"):** patients with baseline pain >60 mm VAS did not benefit from deadlift training; low Biering-Sørensen endurance predicted poorer outcomes (secondary summary). — [academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/17814741/Which_patients_with_low_back_pain_benefit_from_deadlift_training); [DiVA thesis](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:925706/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- **Welch et al. 2021 (J Sport Rehabil 30(4):672):** free-weight programme including deadlifts improved pain, disability and QoL in chronic mechanical LBP; classified by a review as a **cohort** (non-randomised) study. — [Human Kinetics](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/jsr/30/4/article-p672.xml)
- NCT03326362 compares high- vs low-intensity RT (squats, deadlifts) in chronic LBP; results not found. — [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03326362)

### Inferences
- For a strength athlete, "do the lifts, graded" is supported: heavy posterior-chain work is at least as good as low-load motor control and better than general exercise, with no excess harm.
- The Berglund moderator finding gives a practical rule of thumb: when pain is high (>6/10) or extensor endurance is very poor, start with lower-load work before heavy deadlifts.
- Average effects are modest; expect individual variation, and don't expect any specific modality to be magic.

### Gaps
- "Fernández-Rodríguez 2022" NMA (recalled as J Pain 2022, Pilates/strength/core/mind-body ranked best) could not be verified in search; attribution conflict noted above.
- JOSPT 2021 LBP CPG revision content not retrieved; NICE NG59 not searched (budget).
- No RCTs in competitive powerlifters/strongmen with LBP.

## 4. LBP in powerlifters, weightlifters and strongman: epidemiology, technique, bracing, belts, load management

### Takeaway
LBP is among the most common complaints in strength sports (prevalence ~25% to 70%+ depending on definition), but time-loss injury incidence is low (~1–4.4/1000 h) **[ESTABLISHED, low-quality observational data]**; strongman has the highest injury rates but rests on essentially one survey **[PROMISING/limited]**; belts and Valsalva modestly change spinal mechanics acutely, with no outcome data on pain or injury **[CONTESTED / mechanistic only]**.

### Cited Findings
- **Updated systematic review of weightlifting and powerlifting injuries (2024, PROSPERO CRD42022382364):** powerlifting injury incidence 1.0–4.4/1000 h (11 studies, 763 lifters); low back, shoulders, knees most affected; incidence low but prevalence high when injury = painful condition impairing training/competition. — [PubMed 39650568](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39650568/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386428866_Injuries_in_weightlifting_and_powerlifting_an_updated_systematic_review)
- **Polish meta-analysis of lower-back injury in powerlifting:** pooled LBP prevalence 25.6% (95% CI 15.8–38.7%); LBP accounted for 35.4% of powerlifting injuries. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/381750867_INJURIES_OF_THE_LOWER_BACK_IN_POWERLIFTING_A_LITERATURE_REVIEW_WITH_META-ANALYSIS_URAZY_DOLNEGO_ODCINKA_PLECOW_W_TROJBOJU_SILOWYM_PRZEGLAD_LITERATURY_Z_METAANALIZA)
- **2025 scoping review of powerlifting/para-powerlifting sport science (Sports Med):** lower back reported injured in up to 67% of lifters (shoulder up to 73%, knee up to 39%). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-025-02305-3)
- **Strömbäck et al. 2018 (OJSM):** cross-sectional survey of Swedish sub-elite classic powerlifters; injury prevalence and location, sex differences, training/lifestyle associations; LBP a keyword. One summary cites a point prevalence of ~70% injured (powerlifting) — exact low-back figure not retrievable. — [PMC5954586](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5954586)
- Berglund & Strömbäck (2024): pain in low back, hips and/or knees that interferes with training is common in powerlifters even though training-stopping injuries are rare. — [PMC11368446 (search snippet)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11368446)
- **Siewe et al. 2011 (245 powerlifters):** daily training of many powerlifters affected by disorders not requiring interruption; **belt use associated with higher lumbar injury rate** (cross-sectional association, likely confounded by who chooses belts/load). — [PubMed 21590644](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21590644/)
- **Winwood et al. 2014 (JSCR 28(1):28–42), strongman:** 213 athletes, prior-year retrospective survey; 82% reported injury; lower back 24%, shoulder 21%, biceps 11%, knee 11%; muscle strains 38%, tendon 23%; 1.9× more likely to be injured during strongman implement training (exposure-adjusted). — [Bond University](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/retrospective-injury-epidemiology-of-strongman-athletes/); [SRKnowledge NZ](https://srknowledge.org.nz/research-completed/retrospective-injury-epidemiology-of-strongman-athletes)
- **Keogh & Winwood 2017 (Sports Med) review:** weight-training sports have low injury rates vs team sports, ~1–2 injuries/athlete/year in most; bodybuilding lowest, strongman and Highland Games highest — but based on only one study for those sports; limitations in design, diagnosis and exposure. — [summary via search; ECU repository](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/298)
- **Belts:** stiff belt + pre-lift inhalation reduced spinal compression ~10% in 9 weightlifters (in vivo model), attributed to the belt's own moment rather than IAP — [PubMed 17047531](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17047531/); belt raised IAP in deadlifts at 90% 1RM — [PubMed 2709981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2709981/); belt did not change max isometric lifting capacity or peak IAP but raised erector spinae pressure — [PubMed 10619094](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10619094/); belt and/or raised IAP independently increase modelled lumbar stability — [PubMed 10552322](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10552322/); squat-lift belt data "difficult to justify" prescribing belts to workers — [PubMed 2141312](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2141312/)
- **Valsalva review (2013):** brief Valsalva is unavoidable above ~80% MVC or when lifting to failure; proposed stability benefit via IAP; blood-pressure safety concerns flagged. — [PubMed 23222073](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23222073/)

### Inferences
- Competitive strength athletes should expect intermittent, training-modifying back pain as a near-normal part of the sport rather than evidence of structural damage; time-loss injuries are comparatively rare.
- Strongman implement work (stones, yoke, carries, odd objects) is where risk concentrates relative to barbell training — a load-management lever when returning.
- Belt/brace advice is biomechanically plausible (small compression reduction, more stiffness), but no study shows belts reduce LBP; the Siewe association with *more* injury is likely confounding. Treat belts as a performance/comfort tool, not protection.

### Gaps
- No prospective return-to-lifting outcome studies in powerlifters or strongmen after an LBP episode were found.
- 2020s literature on technique coaching (e.g., deadlift lumbar flexion in competition lifts) and LBP outcomes in lifters was not located.
- Exact low-back prevalence from Strömbäck 2018 not retrieved.

## 5. Pain monitoring and graded exposure (Silbernagel model, CFT/RESTORE)

### Takeaway
The pain-monitoring model (pain up to ~5/10 during exercise that returns to baseline by next morning) has trial support in Achilles tendinopathy, but its application to LBP is borrowed/extrapolated **[PROMISING by analogy, untested in LBP]**; CFT (RESTORE) produced durable reductions in disability and pain out to 3 years vs usual care **[ESTABLISHED for chronic disabling LBP, one large high-quality RCT]**.

### Cited Findings
- Pain-monitoring model as applied to exercise: pain allowed to reach 5/10 during exercise, expected to return to pre-exercise level by next day; if not, regress to an easier level. Origin and supporting trial: Achilles tendinopathy. — [PMC5988063 (search snippet)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5988063)
- A systematic-review protocol (Syst Rev 2020) set out to be the **first** synthesis of pain-level-differentiated exercise advice for LBP, indicating evidence for LBP was not yet established. — [Springer Syst Rev 2020 PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/s13643-020-01412-8.pdf)
- **RESTORE (Kent et al., Lancet 2023; 401(10391):1866–1877):** 492 adults with chronic disabling LBP randomised to usual care, CFT, or CFT + movement-sensor biofeedback; CFT more effective than usual care for activity limitation at 13 weeks, retained at 52 weeks. — [PubMed 37146623](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37146623/); [Macquarie news](https://lighthouse.mq.edu.au/article/may-2023/new-treatment-provides-lasting-relief-from-back-pain-clinical-trial)
- **RESTORE 3-year follow-up (Hancock et al., Lancet Rheumatology 2025):** sustained reductions in disability and pain at 3 years; RMDQ vs usual care: CFT −3.5 (95% CI −4.9 to −2.0), CFT + biofeedback −4.1 (−5.6 to −2.6). (A news piece said "over 300 adults" — likely completers; 492 randomised.) — [Epocrates summary (secondary)](https://epocrates.com/online/article/cognitive-functional-therapy-may-provide-lasting-relief-for-chronic-low-back); [GMA news](https://www.goodmorningamerica.com/wellness/story/new-personalized-therapy-provide-long-term-relief-chronic-124346657)
- RESTORE was also reported as cost-effective ("pays off"). — [Australian Physiotherapy Association](https://australian.physio/node/3308)

### Inferences
- A practical lifting translation (inference, not tested): use RPE/load progressions where session pain stays ≤3–5/10, does not escalate across sets, and is back to baseline by the next morning; if not, regress volume/intensity/ROM for the next session. This mirrors the Silbernagel rules and the Berglund finding that high-pain patients don't do well jumping straight to heavy deadlifts.
- CFT's core ingredients — reducing threat beliefs, relaxed/less-guarded movement, graded exposure to feared tasks, lifestyle — map naturally onto returning to feared lifts (e.g., rounded-back pulls, stones). It argues *against* teaching rigid "never flex" avoidance to a lifter with fear.

### Gaps
- No trial testing the pain-monitoring model specifically in LBP or in lifters.
- RESTORE 3-year primary text not read (numbers from secondary synopsis).

## 6. Hip function and LBP (ROM/strength deficits; hip-focused interventions)

### Takeaway
Limited hip internal rotation is the most consistent hip finding in LBP, but evidence is low quality and cross-sectional **[PROMISING association, causation unproven]**; adding hip strengthening gives at most a small, very-low-certainty short-term benefit and one RCT found no added effect **[CONTESTED]**.

### Cited Findings
- Avman et al. systematic review: hip IR was the only movement significantly associated with non-specific LBP; very low-quality evidence. — [Open Research Newcastle](https://openresearch.newcastle.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Is_there_an_association_between_hip_range_of_motion_and_nonspecific_low_back_pain_A_systematic_review/28989926)
- Pizol et al. 2024 (BMC MSK, CRD42020213599): LBP patients differ from healthy controls in hip ROM, task execution, muscle activation and strength; too heterogeneous to pool. — [BMC MSK 2024](https://bmcmusculoskeletdisord.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12891-024-07463-5); [PubMed 38807086](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38807086/)
- 2015 literature review: asymmetric and limited hip IR common in LBP (esp. rotational-sport athletes); no studies reported limited ER. — [PubMed 26751745](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26751745/)
- Single-blind RCT (n = 80): motor control vs motor control + progressive hip strengthening — similar efficacy; adding hip strengthening did not improve pain outcomes. (This appears to be Kendall et al. 2015, but authorship not shown in the result.) — [PubMed 25467200](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25467200/)
- Ceballos-Laita et al. 2023 meta-analysis: very low certainty that hip strengthening alone or with low-back exercise improves short-term pain and disability; prior reviews conflicted. — [PMC10120300](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10120300); [uvadoc PDF](https://uvadoc.uva.es/bitstream/handle/10324/63597/The-effectiveness-of-hip.pdf?sequence=1)
- Santamaría et al. 2023 (Sports): hip strengthening significantly improved pain and disability. — [PMC10536491](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10536491/)
- RCT-only review across trunk/lower-limb conditions: uncertain evidence that hip strengthening enhances short-term effects of other active interventions for LBP. — [PMC9776732](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9776732/)

### Inferences
- For a lifter, hip work (split squats, hip-hinge strength, hip IR/flexor mobility) is low-cost and plausibly helpful for positions (sumo/conventional setup, squat depth), but should not be sold as an LBP treatment on current evidence.
- If a lifter has asymmetric/limited hip IR, addressing it is reasonable for performance; any LBP benefit is speculative.

### Gaps
- Bade et al. 2017 (hip-focused vs lumbar-focused treatment RCT) not surfaced; not verified.
- No trials of split squats or hip-flexor-specific work for LBP found.

## 7. Trunk endurance / motor control (McGill Big 3, endurance norms and ratios) vs strength

### Takeaway
Trunk endurance (including McGill tests/ratios) does not predict future LBP in prospective data — strong evidence of *no* relation per one systematic review **[ESTABLISHED negative, though older]**; flexor/extensor endurance ratios come from cross-sectional data **[CONTESTED as screening tool]**; motor control exercise is roughly as effective as other exercise but not superior **[ESTABLISHED, from NMAs/Cochrane above]**.

### Cited Findings
- Hamberg-van Reenen et al. (Pain 2007) systematic review: **strong evidence of no relation** between trunk muscle endurance and future LBP risk; inconclusive for trunk strength and lumbar mobility. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304395906006336)
- Lee et al. 5-year prospective study (1999): LBP group had a lower extension/flexion *peak torque* ratio; imbalance (extensors weaker than flexors) proposed as a possible risk factor. — [PubMed 9921591](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9921591/)
- McGill-style endurance ratio data are cross-sectional: e.g., flexion:extension endurance 0.64 in people without LBP vs 0.93 with LBP. — [PMC4886801 (collegiate athletes)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4886801/)
- McGill tests are among the most reliable/widely used trunk endurance tests (2022 Roman-chair comparison study). — [PMC9745951](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9745951/)
- Low Biering-Sørensen endurance predicted poorer response to deadlift training (Berglund secondary analysis) — i.e., endurance may moderate treatment response even if it doesn't predict onset. — [academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/17814741/Which_patients_with_low_back_pain_benefit_from_deadlift_training)
- WHO review: motor control exercise reduced pain vs sham, very low certainty. — [PMC10684665](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10684665/)
- Aasa/Berglund RCT: low-load motor control and high-load deadlift both improved; neither clearly superior overall. — [JOSPT PDF](https://www.jospt.org/doi/pdf/10.2519/jospt.2015.5021)

### Inferences
- McGill Big 3 (curl-up, side plank, bird-dog) is a reasonable low-irritability starting point, but endurance norms/ratios should not be used as gatekeeping criteria for return to heavy lifting; there is no prospective evidence that hitting them reduces recurrence.
- In lifters, extensor endurance may still be worth building because it predicts who tolerates heavy deadlift rehab.

### Gaps
- No 2020–2026 prospective study of endurance ratios in strength athletes found.
- No RCT directly comparing McGill Big 3 vs heavy strength training in lifters.

## 8. Disc herniation natural history and implications for loading; return to sport

### Takeaway
About two-thirds to 70% of lumbar disc herniations show spontaneous resorption with conservative care, highest for sequestrations/extrusions, mostly within ~3–6 months **[ESTABLISHED, observational]**; athletes return to sport at similar rates (~80%) with or without surgery, faster without **[PROMISING, low-quality]**.

### Cited Findings
- Zhong et al. 2017 meta-analysis (11 cohorts, 587 patients): overall spontaneous resorption 66.66% (95% CI 51–69%). — [Pain Physician PDF](https://www.painphysicianjournal.com/current/pdf?article=NDAwNA%3D%3D&journal=101); [PainSci summary](https://www.painscience.com/biblio/at-least-half-of-herniated-discs-spontaneously-de-herniate.html)
- Later meta-analysis (31 studies, 2,233 conservatively treated patients): pooled resorption 70.39%; sequestration 87.77%, extrusion 66.91%, protrusion 37.53%, bulge 13.33%; ruptured > contained; resorption mainly within 6 months. — [Clin Spine Surg (Ovid)](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/jspinaldisorders/abstract/10.1097/bsd.0000000000001490~incidence-of-spontaneous-resorption-of-lumbar-disc?redirectionsource=fulltextview); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Incidence-of-Spontaneous-Resorption-of-Lumbar-Disc-Zou-Liu/c216581fd0378972e0b1fed8059920546c9ea827)
- 2024 systematic review: average spontaneous resorption time likely 3–6 months. — [Orthopedic Reviews 2024](https://orthopedicreviews.openmedicalpublishing.org/article/121399-prevalence-clinical-predictors-and-mechanisms-of-resorption-in-lumbar-disc-herniation-a-systematic-review)
- No RCTs in the resorption literature; data "barely enough for meta-analysis." — [PainSci](https://www.painscience.com/biblio/at-least-half-of-herniated-discs-spontaneously-de-herniate.html)
- **Return to play — 2021 meta-analysis of elite athletes (Sports Health):** 20 studies; RTP ~83% surgical vs ~81.5% non-operative; time to return ~5.2 vs ~4.1 months. — [PMC8404721](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8404721); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1941738121991782)
- **Reiman et al. 2016 BJSM review:** no significant RTS difference surgery vs conservative; returners often below pre-injury level; studies heterogeneous, low quality, none high quality. — [PubMed 26491033](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26491033/)
- Discectomy in athletes: 75–100% returned to elite competition; performance 64–103% of baseline; post-op career 2.6–4.8 years. — [PMC4419017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4419017)
- ILEX in limited ROM in herniation with relative surgical indications: 96% improved (retrospective). — [PMC8198576](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8198576)

### Inferences
- A lifter with a herniation (without red flags or progressive deficit) has good odds of resorption and return to sport with conservative care; a ~3–6-month horizon is a realistic planning window before full competition loading, with graded training throughout.
- Larger/extruded herniations, counter-intuitively, resorb more often; imaging size alone shouldn't dictate a bleak prognosis.
- RTP data come from team/elite sports (not strength sports); performance may not fully return in some.

### Gaps
- No return-to-sport studies specific to powerlifters/strongmen after herniation found.
- No evidence on whether heavy axial loading during the resorption window helps or harms resorption.

## 9. Evidence for Low Back Ability (Backstrom) and ATG (Ben Patrick) back-extension / Jefferson-curl standards

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed study was found testing Low Back Ability's progression (iso holds → reps → single-leg → weighted; 2-minute hold and 30-rep standards) or ATG's back-extension/Jefferson-curl standards — these are **[ANECDOTE]**, though their general shape (start isometric/limited range, progress to dynamic and loaded) is consistent with published ILEX limited-ROM and graded-exposure principles.

### Cited Findings
- Search for Low Back Ability's specific protocol returned no primary or peer-reviewed source; no source set a 2-minute hold or 30-rep target as a validated standard. — [search returned only generic sources, e.g. Isometric back extension endurance tests review, PubMed 11208223](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11208223/)
- Coaching sources describe the generic progression: isometric holds first, then dynamic reps, then light load (2.5–5 kg plate) once bodyweight is easy. — [ATG Substack (Ben Patrick, primary coach source)](https://kneesovertoesguy.substack.com/p/atg-every-regression-counts-lower)
- The Biering-Sørensen test (prone isometric extensor hold to failure) is the research analogue of iso-hold standards; reviewed in Moreau et al. 2001. — [PubMed 11208223](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11208223/)
- Normative back-extension rep data are sport-specific (e.g., rowers ~25–32 reps), not rehab targets. — [Topend Sports](https://www.topendsports.com/testing/tests/back-extensions.htm)
- Jefferson curl: only cross-sectional EMG data; adaptation and outcome effects untested; no established 1RM or norms. — [IJSPT 2025/26](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/172559-surface-emg-characterization-of-the-jefferson-curl-at-a-submaximal-load-matched-to-the-romanian-deadlift-a-cross-sectional-study-in-resistance-traine)
- Commentary: no strong trial evidence that Jefferson curls prevent back injury in powerlifters. — [nextcultbrand blog (secondary, low quality)](https://nextcultbrand.com/blogs/gym-workout-fitness/jefferson-curls-for-powerlifters-useful-mobility-or-back-risk-hype)
- Supporting principle from research: ILEX in limited ROM progressed over 9 weeks (2×/week) was associated with improvement in herniation patients (retrospective). — [PMC8198576](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8198576)

### Inferences
- LBA- and ATG-style back-extension progressions are defensible as *applications* of graded exposure, low-dose extensor loading (ILEX evidence) and posterior-chain RT (Tataryn), but their specific numerical standards (2-min hold, 30 reps, Jefferson-curl bodyweight targets) are arbitrary benchmarks without validation for return-to-lifting or recurrence prevention.
- The McGill (flexion-avoidance, endurance-first) and ATG/LBA (load-through-range, including flexion) camps both lack direct outcome trials in lifters; the clinical evidence (Saraceni, Hayden, Tataryn, RESTORE) leans toward graded, confidence-building loading over strict avoidance, while lab disc data keep a caution on high-volume loaded flexion+twist.

### Gaps
- Low Back Ability's official materials were not reachable via search; protocol details are from the assignment brief, not verified.
- No outcome data, case series or registered trials for either programme found.
