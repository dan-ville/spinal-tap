# Hypertrophy science and muscle-length / contraction-type methods for a returning strength athlete

Scope note: web page fetching was blocked in this environment (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov failed DNS through the proxy), so every finding below comes from search-engine abstracts and snippets, not full texts. Exact numbers that could not be checked against a primary abstract are flagged. Evidence labels: **ESTABLISHED** (several meta-analyses agree), **PROMISING** (positive RCTs or meta-analyses with wide intervals), **CONTESTED** (meta-analyses disagree), **THIN** (few small studies).

## Volume dose-response for hypertrophy (Schoenfeld 2017, Pelland 2024–2026, Baz-Valle 2022, fractional sets, diminishing returns)

### Takeaway
ESTABLISHED: more weekly sets produce more hypertrophy, with diminishing returns. In the newest and largest analysis (Pelland et al., published in *Sports Medicine* 2026), the hypertrophy curve flattens more slowly than the strength curve. Counting indirect sets as half a set ("fractional") predicts outcomes best. Per-session volume also appears to plateau, at roughly 11 fractional sets per muscle per session according to the press release; that figure is unverified. For a lifter coming back from back pain, total volume (including volume taken from low-spinal-load exercises) is the main hypertrophy lever. The upper limit is uncertain.

### Cited Findings
- Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017 (J Sports Sci; meta-regression of 15 studies, 34 treatment groups) found a graded dose-response: more weekly resistance-training volume gives more hypertrophy, with a later summary noting favourable results above about 9 weekly sets per muscle. The often-quoted "+0.37% per set" figure was not confirmed in snippets. — [Schoenfeld et al. 2017 (ResearchGate PDF)](https://researchgate.net/profile/Brad-Schoenfeld/publication/305455324_Dose-response_relationship_between_weekly_resistance_training_volume_and_increases_in_muscle_mass_A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis/links/59dc0269458515e9ab4527d6/Dose-response-relationship-between-weekly-resistance-training-volume-and-increases-in-muscle-mass-A-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis.pdf)
- Baz-Valle et al. 2022 (J Hum Kinet) compared low (<12), moderate (12–20) and high (>20) weekly sets per muscle in trained men. Moderate and high volume did not differ for the quadriceps (p = 0.19) or the biceps (p = 0.59). The authors suggested 12–20 weekly sets per muscle as the standard recommendation for young trained men. — [Baz-Valle et al. 2022 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8884877)
- Pelland, Remmert, Robinson, Hinson, Zourdos et al. ran a Bayesian meta-regression of weekly volume and frequency (SportRxiv preprint 2024; the preprint describes 67 studies). They scored indirect sets as 1 ("total"), 0.5 ("fractional") or 0 ("direct"). The **fractional method best predicted both hypertrophy and strength**. Strength shows more pronounced diminishing returns than hypertrophy, and frequency had consistently identifiable effects only on strength, not hypertrophy. — [Pelland et al. preprint v2 (SportRxiv)](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460); [BibBase record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/joshuapelland-jacobremmert-zacrobinson-sethhinson-michaelzourdos-theresistancetrainingdoseresponsemetaregressionsexploringtheeectsofweeklyvolumeandfrequencyonmusclehypertrophyandstrengthgain-2024)
- The peer-reviewed version appeared as *Sports Medicine* 56(2):481–505 (2026), DOI 10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w. Its abstract repeats the main findings: different volume responses for hypertrophy and strength, more pronounced diminishing returns for strength, and frequency effects identifiable only for strength. — [SPONET record of Pelland et al. 2026](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)
- An October 2026 reanalysis on Preprints.org describes Pelland et al. as having modelled 220 hypertrophy effects from 35 studies, which conflicts with the "67 studies" figure. That figure may count strength and hypertrophy studies together; unresolved. — [Preprints.org 202610.1022](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202610.1022)
- Per-session volume: the FAU press release on the companion analysis reports that benefits for muscle growth levelled off at about **11 fractional sets per muscle per session**. The Remmert et al. per-session preprint reports a 100% posterior probability that the per-session volume slope exceeds zero for both strength and hypertrophy, and cites Pelland for strength gains being undetectable beyond about 3 fractional sets per session. — [FAU news release](https://fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/build-muscle-gain-strength-study.php); [Remmert et al. per-session preprint (SportRxiv)](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)
- A 2023 Bayesian network meta-analysis (Currier et al., BJSM) found that "all prescriptions comparably promoted muscle hypertrophy", which supports flexibility in how volume is delivered. — [Currier et al. 2023 BJSM PDF](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/57/18/1211.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because indirect sets count about 0.5, compound lifts done for the legs (squats, leg press) also give partial credit to the glutes and adductors. However, the lumbar erectors probably shouldn't be credited as if they were the prime mover (see the lumbar section).
- Diminishing returns are steeper for strength than for hypertrophy. For a strongman or powerlifting goal, that argues for moderate, sustainable volume on the competition lifts plus extra hypertrophy volume on low-spinal-load accessories. That is where extra sets still pay off.
- Volume can be shifted from spinally loaded movements to supported or machine alternatives without losing the dose, provided the sets are hard. Effort (next section) moderates how much a set counts.

### Gaps
- Exact Pelland 2026 numbers (marginal gains per set, posterior probabilities beyond about 20–30 weekly sets, the trained vs untrained moderator) could not be read. Full text was not fetchable.
- The "~11 fractional sets per session" figure comes only from a press release. A Remmert snippet appears to contain a typo ("~31").
- No data on whether volume tolerance differs during return from back pain.

## Proximity to failure (Refalo 2023/2024, Robinson 2024): how close is close enough?

### Takeaway
ESTABLISHED/PROMISING: Hypertrophy improves as sets end closer to failure (Robinson 2024 meta-regression). Strength is largely insensitive to proximity to failure over a wide range. However, a well-controlled trial in trained lifters found no difference between true failure and 1–2 RIR (Refalo 2024), and an earlier meta-analysis found no clear benefit of failure (Refalo 2023). Practical consensus: about 0–3 RIR on hypertrophy work. Going to true failure adds little growth but more fatigue, and that fatigue matters more on spinally loaded lifts.

### Cited Findings
- Robinson et al. 2024, *Sports Medicine* 54(9):2209–2231 (meta-regressions, about 55 studies per press coverage): **strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR, while hypertrophy improves as sets end closer to failure**. The authors caution that, given how RIR was estimated, the exact relationship remains unclear. — [PubMed 38393985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/); [FAU news release](https://www.fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/muscle-growth-strength-study); [ScienceDaily summary](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/07/240731140409.htm)
- Robinson 2024: the RIR–hypertrophy relationship held regardless of whether volume was adjusted through sets or reps (secondary report). — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/07/240731140409.htm)
- Refalo et al. 2023, *Sports Medicine* 53(3):649–665 (15 studies) found no evidence that training to momentary failure beats non-failure training for hypertrophy. The pooled advantage across failure definitions was trivial, and the momentary-failure subgroup showed no advantage. The authors suggest a possibly non-linear proximity-to-failure–hypertrophy relationship. — [PubMed 36334240](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y)
- An earlier meta-analysis (authors not confirmed from the snippet; probably Grgic et al. 2022) found no overall difference between failure and non-failure training for strength or hypertrophy. It did find a significant hypertrophy effect of training to failure in trained subjects. Non-volume-equated studies favoured non-failure for strength. — [PMC9068575](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9068575)
- Refalo et al. 2024, *J Sports Sci* 42(1):85–101: 18 trained participants (12 men, 6 women) trained leg press and leg extension twice weekly for 8 weeks, one leg to momentary failure and the other at 2 RIR (leg press) and 1 RIR (leg extension). Quadriceps thickness gains were **~0.181 cm vs ~0.182 cm, essentially identical**. Failure caused more acute fatigue (repetition loss and velocity loss). — [AUT repository record](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/dbe641c7-a606-4e00-94bc-bb2fbe253afa)
- A 2025 single-set trial (*Med Sci Sports Exerc*, n = 42, "Without Fail") found several hypertrophy measures tended to favour failure, but absolute differences were modest. — [PubMed 40249908](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40249908/)

### Inferences
- Robinson's continuous meta-regression and Refalo's 0-vs-1–2 RIR trial can be reconciled. The gain per RIR is probably small near failure and larger further away (for example, 5+ RIR is clearly worse). "Close enough" is plausibly about 1–3 RIR.
- For this lifter: keep heavy spinally loaded lifts (deadlift, squat, stones, yoke) at 2–4 RIR for strength, which the evidence says costs little strength. Take low-spinal-load machine or isolation work to 0–1 RIR, where the risk is low and the hypertrophy signal is strongest.
- Low-volume programmes (single sets) may benefit more from going to failure than high-volume programmes (Without Fail trial; the Refalo 2023 non-linearity).

### Gaps
- RIR estimation accuracy in returning or untrained-in-a-lift contexts is not addressed.
- No trial specifically on failure training for trunk or back extensors.

## Training at long muscle lengths, lengthened partials and stretch emphasis

### Takeaway
PROMISING but CONTESTED overall. **Exercise-selection evidence is fairly consistent** that variants loading a muscle at longer lengths grow more for some muscles: seated vs prone leg curl (hamstrings), overhead vs neutral triceps extension, standing vs seated calf raise for the gastrocnemius, and deep vs half squat for the glutes and adductors. **Lengthened partials vs full ROM** is murkier. One 2026 meta-analysis (Strey et al.) found a small benefit for long-length partials over short-length partials (ES ≈ 0.28). A 2025 Bayesian meta-analysis (Varovic, Wolf, Schoenfeld, Steele, *Int J Sports Med*) found practically equivalent regional growth for longer vs shorter mean muscle length. Wolf 2023 found full ROM slightly better than partial ROM overall, with a non-significant hint that lengthened partials beat full ROM. Recent trials in trained lifters (Larsen 2024 preprints) suggest lengthened partials ≈ full ROM. Short-length partials are the clear loser.

### Cited Findings
- **Wolf et al. 2023** (*Int J Strength Cond*; meta-analysis of partial vs full ROM): trivial SMD favouring full ROM over partial ROM, 0.12 (95% CI −0.02 to 0.26). A subgroup suggested a possible benefit of partials at long muscle lengths over full ROM, −0.28 (95% CI −0.81 to 0.16; crosses zero). — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/99e86b317bec40f5abc2f0008358737e)
- **Kassiano et al. 2023**, "Which ROMs Lead to Rome?" (systematic review), addressed optimal ROM for hypertrophy, noting there were no consistent practical guidelines. Its detailed conclusions were not read. — [PubMed 36662126](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36662126/)
- **Varovic, Wolf, Schoenfeld, Steele 2025** (*Int J Sports Med*, published online 26 June 2025; Bayesian meta-analysis of 12 studies in young adults, ROM or exercise-selection manipulations): training at longer mean muscle lengths "does not seem to produce greater regional muscle hypertrophy" than shorter mean lengths. Example SMD 0.07 [95% QI −0.02, 0.15], with a slight trend at distal sites. The authors caution that the length contrasts in included studies were small; one summary says about a 22% length difference. — [Thieme abstract](https://www.thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/a-2615-4935); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/464); [Stronger By Science write-up](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/muscle-length-regional-hypertrophy/)
- **Strey et al. 2026** (*Sport Sciences for Health* 22:33, published 5 January 2026; partial reps at long vs short muscle length): longer-length partials gave significantly greater hypertrophy, ES = 0.283 (CI 0.04–0.52; p = 0.036). Distal and central regional hypertrophy both favoured the long-length condition. A blog reports it pooled 8 studies (unverified). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11332-025-01586-5); [Ulster University record](https://pure.ulster.ac.uk/en/publications/muscle-hypertrophy-from-partial-repetition-at-long-vs-short-muscl/)
- A **2025/2026 systematic review on longitudinal growth** (*Sports Medicine and Health Science*; Androulakis-Korakakis among the authors) concluded that longer-muscle-length RT is superior to shorter-length RT for hypertrophy. Evidence that it adds fascicle length (longitudinal growth) is equivocal. It noted greater hypertrophy with lengthened partials vs full ROM in some studies, and possibly greater increases in fascicle angle and radial hypertrophy. — [ScienceDirect S2666337625000332](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666337625000332); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/389630011_Does_longer-muscle_length_resistance_training_cause_greater_longitudinal_growth_in_humans_A_systematic_review)
- An August 2025 OSF registration describes a further meta-analysis of longer vs shorter muscle-length training. No results were found. — [OSF](https://osf.io/x86yg/overview)
- **Maeo et al. 2021** (*Med Sci Sports Exerc*; within-subject): whole-hamstring muscle-volume gains were greater with **seated (hip-flexed, longer) than prone leg curl, +14% vs +9%**. There was no significant difference for the monoarticular short head of the biceps femoris, consistent with the effect coming from biarticular lengthening at the hip. — [PubMed 33009197](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/); [PMC7969179](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7969179)
- **Maeo et al. 2023** (*Eur J Sport Sci*): triceps hypertrophy was "substantially greater" with overhead (shoulder-flexed) than neutral-arm elbow extensions, despite lighter absolute loads in the overhead condition. A "~40%" figure circulates, but the snippets did not confirm it. — [Taylor & Francis/Informa DOI page](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/17461391.2022.2100279)
- **Kinoshita/Maeo et al. 2023** (*Front Physiol*): "Triceps surae muscle hypertrophy is greater after standing versus seated calf-raise training." Standing calf raises put the gastrocnemius at a longer length. — [Frontiers in Physiology 10.3389/fphys.2023.1272106](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1272106/full)
- **Pedrosa et al. 2022** (untrained women, leg extension): a lengthened partial range gave greater distal quadriceps hypertrophy than a shortened partial range. Some sources also describe a full-ROM comparison. This comes from secondary summaries only. — [Overview article citing it (PMC12869050)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12869050)
- **Larsen et al. 2024** (SportRxiv preprints, not peer-reviewed at the time found): in 30 resistance-trained participants, upper limbs were randomised to lengthened partials vs full ROM. A second study compared lengthened partials performed past failure with full ROM, and that lengthened-partials group did more total volume load, which confounds the result. A secondary evidence summary characterises 2025–2026 trained-lifter trials as finding lengthened partials ≈ full ROM. — [SportRxiv preprint 455](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/455); [Evipedia summary (secondary)](https://evipedia.ai/lengthened_partials_er)
- Glutes and adductors: **Kubo et al. 2019** (*Eur J Appl Physiol*; 17 men, 10 weeks): full squat vs half squat increased adductor volume 6.2% vs 2.7% and gluteus maximus volume 6.7% vs 2.2%. The full squat gave significantly greater relative gains; quadriceps gains were similar, and the hamstrings did not change in either group. — [Springer DOI](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00421-019-04181-y); [Stronger By Science on squat depth](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/squat-depth/)
- **Plotkin et al. 2023** (*Front Physiol*; untrained; 9 weeks; hip thrust n = 18 vs back squat n = 16, set-volume matched, MRI): **similar gluteal hypertrophy**. The squat gave more thigh hypertrophy, strength gains were specific to the exercise trained, and deadlift strength gains were similar. Higher hip-thrust sEMG did not predict more glute growth. — [PMC10593473](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10593473/)

### Inferences
- Robust, low-risk applications: choose exercises that load the target muscle at long lengths, such as seated leg curl, overhead triceps work, standing calf raises, deep squats or deep leg press for the glutes and adductors, and RDLs for the hamstrings. Adding lengthened partials is optional. They are unlikely to hurt, may help a little, and are useful as a way to extend sets past full-ROM failure. Avoid short-length-only partials for hypertrophy.
- The best-supported muscles are the biarticular hamstrings, the long head of the triceps, the gastrocnemius, the quadriceps (distal region) and the glutes and adductors via squat depth. Evidence for trunk or back extensors is essentially absent.
- For a lifter with back pain, the long-length emphasis for the glutes and hamstrings can be had with lower spinal demand: seated leg curls; hip thrusts, which match squats for glute growth (Plotkin) and can be supported by a bench; deep leg press or hack squat; and 45° back extensions biased to the hips. RDLs place the hamstrings long but load the spine in shear and compression, so they can be reintroduced progressively rather than used as the primary hamstring builder.
- The effect is probably muscle- and architecture-specific. Studies with larger length contrasts (seated vs prone leg curl, overhead vs neutral) show bigger effects than ROM-tweak studies, which may explain the null in the Varovic meta-analysis.

### Gaps
- No full text was accessible for Strey 2026 or Varovic 2025, including heterogeneity, risk of bias and certainty grading.
- Peer-reviewed status and final results of the Larsen 2024 preprints are unconfirmed. The Pedrosa 2023 calf study was not located.
- No RCT measured RDL vs hip thrust for hamstring hypertrophy, or any long-length method for the erector spinae or multifidus.
- No study isolates "loaded stretch" holds versus lengthened partials in trained lifters (not found).

## Eccentric-focused, accentuated eccentric, flywheel and isometric training

### Takeaway
ESTABLISHED: eccentric and concentric actions both build muscle, with eccentrics slightly ahead on average (Schoenfeld 2017: ~10.0% vs 6.8%). Accentuated-eccentric loading (AEL) has not shown superiority over constant loads for hypertrophy or architecture in recent reviews (CONTESTED/THIN). Flywheel eccentric-overload training beat conventional training in a 2017 meta-analysis (PROMISING; dated). For isometrics, **long-muscle-length isometrics produce more hypertrophy than short-length isometrics of equal volume**, and high-intensity (≥70%) contractions are needed for tendon adaptation (Oranchuk 2019; PROMISING).

### Cited Findings
- Schoenfeld et al. 2017 meta-analysis (concentric vs eccentric): mean muscle growth favoured eccentric over concentric actions, **10.0% vs 6.8%**. The authors concluded both actions are useful in a hypertrophy programme. — [PubMed 28486337](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28486337/)
- A systematic review of RCTs (searched to early 2024) found eccentric-only training improves eccentric strength more than concentric-only training. Concentric and isometric strength gains were similar. This review covered strength, not hypertrophy. — [PMC11943567](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11943567) *(attribution of this snippet to this exact PMC ID is uncertain)*
- Amdi & King 2025 (*Sports Medicine – Open*; meta-analysis on eccentric-phase duration) note that eccentric-only actions can give similar or greater hypertrophy than concentric-only or combined actions. Their primary duration findings were not visible in the snippet. — [Springer s40798-025-00887-w](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-025-00887-w)
- An accentuated-eccentric-loading systematic review and meta-analysis (searched to July 2024) found AEL increases perceived exertion and metabolic stress, but did not show superior strength or muscle-architecture gains over constant-load training. A 2026 lower-body AEL systematic review declined to pool the studies because they were too heterogeneous. — [PMC12370621](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12370621/); [2026 AEL lower-body review (PMC12900726)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12900726/)
- A 2017 meta-analysis of flywheel eccentric-overload training found significant advantages over conventional resistance training in concentric and eccentric strength, power, **hypertrophy**, jump height and running speed. No 2023–2026 flywheel hypertrophy meta-analysis was found. — [PubMed 28385560](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28385560/)
- Oranchuk et al. 2019 (*Scand J Med Sci Sports* 29(4):484–503; systematic review of 26 outputs): isometric training at longer muscle lengths gave **0.86–1.69%/week hypertrophy (ES 0.03–0.09/week)**, greater than equal volumes at shorter lengths. Long-length isometrics transferred better to dynamic performance. **High-intensity (≥70%) contractions are required to improve tendon structure and function.** — [PubMed 30580468](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30580468/)
- A small study of short-term inertial (flywheel) squat training reported early functional and morphological adaptations. — [PMC6139363](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6139363/)

### Inferences
- For a lifter with back pain, isometrics offer controllable, low-velocity loading. Long-length isometrics (for example, a bottom-position split squat or a hamstring hold in a lengthened position) can contribute to hypertrophy. Heavy (≥70% MVC) isometrics are the evidence-based option for tendon. Spinal isometrics such as back-extension holds are a reasonable graded-exposure tool, though no hypertrophy data specific to the erectors were found.
- Eccentric emphasis (slower lowering, eccentric-only machine work) is a valid way to add hypertrophy stimulus at lower concentric loads. Heavy supramaximal eccentrics on spinally loaded lifts carry more soreness and damage risk and have no proven hypertrophy advantage (AEL evidence).
- Flywheel squats and RDLs still load the spine. Flywheel work that is not spinally loaded (for example, leg curl or knee extension devices) would be the low-spinal-load option.

### Gaps
- The Lum & Barbosa 2019 isometric review (believed to be *Int J Sports Med*, "Brief review: effects of isometric strength training on strength and dynamic performance") was **not found** in searches, so its content is unverified.
- "Yielding" vs "overcoming" isometrics: no comparative hypertrophy data were found.
- No 2023–2026 hypertrophy-specific meta-analysis of eccentric vs concentric training or of flywheel training was found. The Schoenfeld 2017 and flywheel 2017 meta-analyses may be outdated but were not superseded in what I found.

## Load range (low-load vs high-load equivalence; BFR as a low-spinal-load option)

### Takeaway
ESTABLISHED: when sets are taken close to failure, low loads (about ≥30% 1RM) produce hypertrophy similar to high loads. Strength, especially 1RM, favours heavy loads (Lopez 2021 network meta-analysis; Currier 2023). BFR is a plausible way to grow limb muscles with light loads and little spinal load. However, the only trial in recurrent low back pain found no extra benefit from low-load BFR on appendicular muscle size, and no transfer to the trunk extensors (THIN).

### Cited Findings
- Lopez et al. 2021, "Resistance Training Load Effects on Muscle Hypertrophy and Strength Gain: Systematic Review and Network Meta-analysis" (*Med Sci Sports Exerc* 53(6):1206–1216), compared low, moderate and high loads in healthy adults. Snippets did not show its effect sizes. As usually summarised, hypertrophy was similar across loads and strength favoured higher loads; this was not verified here. — [Rgu repository listing (secondary citation)](https://rgu-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/2344468)
- Currier et al. 2023 (*BJSM*; Bayesian network meta-analysis): "all prescriptions comparably promoted muscle hypertrophy". — [BJSM PDF](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/57/18/1211.full.pdf)
- In recurrent nonspecific low back pain, low-load BFR exercise of the appendicular muscles **did not cross-transfer to the trunk-extensor musculature**. A related trial found **no significant benefit** of low-load BFR on appendicular muscle size and function versus standard exercise. — [Trial protocol (Trials, PMC4751635)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4751635/); [results paper (PMC6697223)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6697223)
- The rationale given in that literature for trialling BFR in back pain is concern about high mechanical and compressive spinal loading from high-load exercise. — [PMC4751635](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4751635/)
- 2024 trial in male collegiate athletes with chronic nonspecific LBP: 4 weeks of low-load BFR significantly reduced pain and disability, with strength gains similar to high-load resistance training. Hypertrophy was not the outcome, and the sample was small. — [Frontiers in Public Health 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2024.1496482/full)
- A 2026 registered trial of low-intensity BFR for low back pain is ongoing. — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT07576101](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07576101)
- A protocol cites prior work that high-load trunk extension increased erector spinae volume by ~2.2%, whereas low-load trunk exercise did not cause hypertrophy. This is a secondary citation; the primary source was not identified. — [PMC4751635](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4751635/)

### Inferences
- The low-load equivalence means hypertrophy of the legs, glutes and upper body can continue with light, high-rep, close-to-failure work on supported machines (leg press, hack squat, belt squat, chest-supported rows) while the spine recovers. Heavy, specific work is still required to regain competition 1RM.
- BFR is optional rather than necessary. Light loads to near failure already grow muscle, and BFR mainly shortens time or cuts total reps. It does not train the back extensors.

### Gaps
- Lopez 2021 effect sizes and the minimum effective load threshold were not verified from the primary source.
- No BFR hypertrophy trial in trained strength athletes with back pain was found.

## Rest intervals and frequency

### Takeaway
PROMISING: rests longer than ~60 s give a small hypertrophy advantage, with no detectable gain beyond ~90 s (Singer 2024). Frequency matters little for hypertrophy once weekly volume is equated, but has identifiable effects on strength (Pelland 2026). Frequency is therefore best used as a scheduling tool: spreading volume across sessions limits per-session spinal fatigue.

### Cited Findings
- Singer et al. 2024, "Give it a rest" (*Front Sports Act Living*, 14 August 2024; Bayesian meta-analysis, 19 measurements from 9 studies): a small hypertrophy benefit for inter-set rests **>60 s**, possibly via smaller volume-load losses. There was no meaningful difference among rests **>90 s**. Training to failure or not did not modify the rest effect. Whole-body estimates were near zero and slightly favoured shorter rests; site-specific effects varied. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/full); [PubMed 39205815](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39205815/)
- Pelland et al. 2026: frequency has consistently identifiable effects on strength but not hypertrophy. — [SPONET record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460)
- The per-session plateau (~11 fractional sets per muscle per session for hypertrophy, press release) implies that frequency becomes relevant once weekly volume exceeds what one session can usefully hold. — [FAU news release](https://fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/build-muscle-gain-strength-study.php)

### Inferences
- Use about 2 min or more between sets of hard compounds and ≥60–90 s on isolation work. Supersetting non-competing low-spinal-load accessories is a reasonable time saver.
- A higher frequency of lower-volume exposures to the competition lifts suits strength (Pelland) and also spreads spinal loading.

### Gaps
- The Schoenfeld 2019 frequency meta-analysis was not returned by searches. From recall, Schoenfeld et al. found no meaningful frequency effect on hypertrophy when volume is equated, but this is unverified.
- Rest-interval data in trained lifters at high volumes are limited (9 studies).

## Regional hypertrophy, exercise selection, and does hypertrophy transfer to 1RM?

### Takeaway
ESTABLISHED/PROMISING: hypertrophy is regionally non-uniform and depends on the exercise. Different exercises for the same muscle grow different regions (squat vs hip thrust; overhead vs neutral triceps; seated vs prone leg curl). Strength gains are exercise-specific (Plotkin 2023). Strength also has steeper diminishing returns to volume than hypertrophy (Pelland). Extra muscle supports long-term strength, but short-term 1RM depends on specific practice.

### Cited Findings
- Plotkin 2023: squat and hip thrust gave similar glute growth, but strength gains favoured the trained exercise (squat 3RM to the squat group, hip-thrust 3RM to the hip-thrust group). Deadlift strength gains were similar. — [PMC10593473](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10593473/)
- Maeo 2021: the hamstring advantage of seated leg curls was confined to the biarticular heads. Muscle-specific results depend on joint configuration. — [PubMed 33009197](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/)
- Kubo 2019: squat depth changed glute and adductor growth but not quadriceps growth. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00421-019-04181-y)
- Varovic 2025: regional outcomes showed a slight distal trend with longer-length training, but overall equivalence. — [Thieme](https://www.thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/a-2615-4935)
- Pelland 2026: hypertrophy and strength have different dose-response curves, so they are partially dissociated outcomes. — [SPONET](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)
- Robinson 2024: strength was similar across wide RIR ranges while hypertrophy depended on proximity to failure, a further dissociation. — [PubMed 38393985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/)

### Inferences
- Use a range of exercises per muscle to cover regions, for example both hip-dominant and knee-dominant work for the glutes and quads, and seated leg curls plus hip-hinge work for the hamstrings.
- During return from back pain, low-spinal-load accessories can keep building muscle. The specific skill and 1RM of the deadlift, squat and strongman events then need deliberate, progressive re-exposure, because strength transfer is exercise-specific.

### Gaps
- No 2020–2026 primary study or meta-analysis quantifying how much hypertrophy contributes to 1RM gains in trained lifters was retrieved. Known work, for example on whether muscle growth causally contributes to strength, was not found in these searches, so this remains an inference.

## Lumbar extensor and multifidus hypertrophy specifically

### Takeaway
THIN/CONTESTED. Direct evidence that resistance training enlarges the lumbar erector spinae or multifidus is sparse, small and mixed. The one MRI study of dynamic isolated lumbar extension in chronic low back pain found **no significant multifidus CSA change** despite better function. Ultrasound-based trials, including a 2025 *Sci Rep* trial of isolated lumbar extension, report gains. A pooled analysis (per a search summary) suggested exercise increases multifidus size but not erector spinae CSA. **No study was found that measured lumbar-extensor hypertrophy from squat or deadlift training in trained lifters.** Acute MRI shows the multifidus and erector spinae are stressed more in stoop-type lifts than in squats.

### Cited Findings
- Willemink et al. 2012 (*Spine*; prospective single-arm, n = 16 men with chronic nonspecific LBP; MRI at 0, 12 and 24 weeks): dynamic isolated lumbar extensor training improved functional status "without influencing lumbar multifidus functional cross-sectional area" as determined by MRI. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/231613263_The_Effects_of_Dynamic_Isolated_Lumbar_Extensor_Training_on_Lumbar_Multifidus_Functional_Cross-Sectional_Area_and_Functional_Status_of_Patients_With_Chronic_Nonspecific_Low_Back_Pain); [Ovid/Spine abstract](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/spinejournal/abstract/10.1097/brs.0b013e318274fb2f~the-effects-of-dynamic-isolated-lumbar-extensor-training-on?redirectionsource=fulltextview)
- A 2025 *Scientific Reports* trial compared isolated lumbar extension (ILEX) alone with ILEX in a multimodal programme. It reported increases in lumbar extensor CSA, muscle thickness and strength, assessed by **ultrasound**. — [Nature Sci Rep s41598-025-22452-x](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-22452-x.pdf)
- 2019 *BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders* preliminary study (patients with low back pain, high-intensity resistance exercise): the change in lumbar extensor CSA was small and not statistically significant; the study also assessed fat fraction. — [BMC Musculoskelet Disord 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12891-019-2658-1)
- Acute MRI (T2 shift) in 13 healthy adults: the T2 increase in the multifidus and erector spinae was greater after stoop lifts than after squat lifts. This shows acute activation or loading, not hypertrophy. — [PubMed 22814283](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22814283/)
- Secondary citation: high-load trunk extension increased erector spinae volume by ~2.2%, while low-load trunk exercise did not. — [PMC4751635](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4751635/)
- Low-load limb BFR did not cross-transfer to trunk-extensor muscle in recurrent LBP. — [PMC6697223](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6697223)
- Ongoing work: a 2026 RCT used MRI to measure multifidus and erector spinae CSA and fatty infiltration after stabilisation and stretching exercise (results not visible in the snippet). ClinicalTrials NCT06890052 tests isolated lumbar extension with multifidus CSA by ultrasound at L4/L5 as its primary outcome. — [J Orthop Surg Res 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13018-026-06758-8); [NCT06890052](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06890052)
- An EMG-based argument (Brookbush Institute; secondary, opinion) holds that erector EMG is similar across most loaded lower-body compounds at matched relative loads. EMG is not hypertrophy. — [Brookbush Institute](https://brookbushinstitute.com/articles/stop-calling-deadlifts-a-back-exercise)

### Inferences
- In squats and deadlifts the lumbar erectors work mostly isometrically, holding a near-neutral spine. They are not taken through a large range at long lengths, so hypertrophy-oriented loading is limited. Under the "long length / dynamic range" logic and the fractional-set accounting, squats and deadlifts are probably weak direct hypertrophy stimuli for the lumbar extensors. This is an inference; no trained-lifter imaging data were found.
- If lumbar-extensor size or capacity is a goal, the best available evidence (still weak) points to dynamic, progressively loaded **direct lumbar extension**: pelvis-stabilised isolated lumbar extension machines, or 45°/horizontal back extensions with some spinal flexion and extension. Load must be high enough to matter (the high- vs low-load trunk-extension citation). These also fit graded-exposure rehabilitation for non-specific low back pain. Expect functional gains even if CSA changes are hard to detect by MRI.
- Muscle *quality* (fat infiltration) may respond, but this was not established in what was found.

### Gaps
- Fisher, Steele and colleagues' isolated lumbar extension studies (the MedX/ILEX literature, for example on pelvic restraint, and whether whole-body or deadlift training strengthens the lumbar extensors) were **not retrieved** in searches. Their conclusions, as I recall them (that pelvic restraint is needed to isolate the lumbar extensors, and that deadlifts or squats may not strengthen them), are unverified here.
- No MRI study of erector spinae or multifidus hypertrophy after squat or deadlift training in trained lifters was found.
- A search summary mentioned a meta-analysis in chronic LBP showing exercise increases multifidus thickness and CSA but not erector spinae or quadratus lumborum CSA. I could not pin its primary URL or authors, so it is excluded from cited findings.
- Results of the 2026 MRI RCT and of NCT06890052 are not available.
