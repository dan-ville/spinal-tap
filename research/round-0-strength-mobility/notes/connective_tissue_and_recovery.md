# Connective-Tissue Adaptation and Recovery for Strength Athletes (evidence to late 2026)

Applied lens: trained, self-coached adult lifter returning from non-specific low back pain (possible disc history), aiming at strongman/powerlifting.

Certainty labels used below: **ESTABLISHED** (multiple RCTs/meta-analyses, consistent), **PROMISING** (mechanistic + small/early human data), **CONTESTED** (conflicting data or methodological critique), **SPECULATIVE** (mostly in-vitro/animal or expert opinion).

Method note: research done via web search snippets (abstract-level); full texts were not opened. Numbers come from abstracts/search summaries and should be checked against the papers before being quoted as exact. 17 searches were run; some requested topics (bone, caffeine, massage/compression, energy deficit) were not searched and are listed under Gaps with background knowledge marked as such.

## Tendon/ligament adaptation: loading, time course, rest windows, isometrics, gelatin/collagen

### Takeaway
Tendon stiffness and modulus increase reliably with resistance training, and **high strain magnitude** is the main driver (ESTABLISHED). CSA gains are small. Tendon collagen turns over far more slowly than muscle protein, so connective tissue lags behind muscle. The "short bouts, ~6 h apart" refractory model is mainly from engineered-ligament/in-vitro work (PROMISING/SPECULATIVE in humans). Gelatin/collagen + vitamin C before loading rests on one small 2017 crossover trial plus small follow-ups and unpublished protocols (PROMISING, low certainty). Isometrics for tendinopathy analgesia: the original 2015 effect has **not held up as superior** to isotonic loading in later RCTs/meta-analyses (CONTESTED; older claim effectively superseded).

### Cited Findings
**Loading parameters**
- 2022 *Sports Medicine* systematic review/meta-analysis of healthy lower-limb tendons (61 articles, 763 participants): loading produced **moderate increases in stiffness, large increases in modulus, small increases in CSA**; the main mechanism of increased stiffness is increased modulus (material quality), not size. **High-strain protocols produced larger gains in modulus and stiffness than low-strain protocols (stiffness SMD ~1.04)**; resistance training produced greater modulus gains than other training types. Authors caution tendon strain is rarely measured directly in vivo. — [Sports Med 2022, PMC9474511](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9474511); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s40279-022-01695-y)
- Bohm, Mersmann & Arampatzis 2015 (*Sports Medicine – Open*) meta-analysis: SMD **0.70 for stiffness, 0.69 for Young's modulus, 0.24 for CSA**; **loading magnitude plays a key role for tendon adaptation, in contrast to muscle contraction type** (eccentric vs concentric not decisive). — [Bohm et al. 2015, PMC4532714](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4532714/)
- An earlier systematic review noted important between- and within-study variability in tendon property changes. — [PubMed 25563908](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25563908/)

**Time course / turnover**
- Radiocarbon (C-14) dating showed that in most samples the collagen in the **core of the Achilles tendon had not turned over since about age 17**; adult tendon adds/removes collagen mainly at the periphery ("grows like a tree"). Original data attributed to Heinemeier/Kalliokoski, summarised by Baar. — [GSSI Sports Science Exchange (Baar)](https://www.gssiweb.org/en/sports-science-exchange/Article/minimizing-injury-and-maximizing-return-to-play-research-lessons-from-engineered-ligaments)
- Human tendon collagen synthesis rates vary by method: some studies report ~**1%/24 h**, others ~**0.2%/24 h**. — [PMC review via search (PMC2075262 / related)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2075262)

**Rest windows (Baar refractory hypothesis)**
- Engineered sinews become refractory to the mechanical stimulus quickly; **short (<10 min) bouts of activity with relatively long (~6 h) rest** were best for collagen synthesis in these constructs. — [GSSI SSE #187 (Baar)](https://www.gssiweb.org/docs/default-source/sse-docs/barr_sse_187_v2.pdf?sfvrsn=2); [GSSI article](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/minimizing-injury-and-maximizing-return-to-play-research-lessons-from-engineered-ligaments)
- Baar's secondary write-up: ligament collagen synthesis maximised by **5–10 min of exercise separated by 6 h**; the supporting human (jump-rope) data are small. — [mysportscience (Baar, secondary)](https://www.mysportscience.com/amp/gelatin-and-injury)
- Certainty: in-vitro/engineered-tissue mechanism plus very small human data -> PROMISING/SPECULATIVE for human programming.

**Gelatin/collagen + vitamin C**
- Shaw et al. 2017 (*Am J Clin Nutr*): n=8 young men, crossover; **15 g vitamin-C-enriched gelatin ~1 h before 6 min of rope-skipping doubled blood PINP** (collagen synthesis marker) vs placebo; 5 g had smaller effect. Engineered ligaments treated with serum from the 15 g condition had higher collagen content/mechanics. — [PubMed 27852613](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27852613/); [PMC5183725](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5183725)
- Later study in resistance-trained young males: **30 g hydrolysed collagen** before high-intensity resistance exercise augmented **whole-body** collagen synthesis more than 15 g or 0 g (year not confirmed in snippet; not tendon-specific). — [PMC (pmid 38007183)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmid/38007183)
- RCT (2022, *IJSNEM*), 50 male athletes: **20 g hydrolysed collagen + 50 mg vitamin C** with strength training improved squat and CMJ **rate of force development** vs maltodextrin placebo — functional outcome, not direct tendon measure. — [PubMed 34808597](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34808597/)
- JUMPFOOD (patellar tendinopathy, collagen + vit C + progressive tendon loading) and COL-TEN (dietary collagen effect on human tendon protein synthesis, registered 2023, recruitment closed) — **only protocols found; no published results located**. — [ISRCTN72255101](https://www.isrctn.com/pdf/ISRCTN72255101); [PubMed 38017500](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38017500/)

**Isometrics for tendinopathy pain**
- Rio et al. 2015 (*BJSM*): in **6 volleyball players** with patellar tendinopathy, a single bout of heavy isometric quadriceps exercise (5 x 45 s at ~70% MVC) produced immediate analgesia lasting ~45 min and reduced cortical inhibition, greater than isotonic. (Note: one indexed record lists a different first author; verify author list.) — [PubMed 25979840](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25979840/); [Monash](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/isometric-exercise-induces-analgesia-and-reduces-inhibition-in-pa/)
- Holden et al. 2020 randomised crossover in patellar tendinopathy was designed as the larger replication (results not visible in snippet; widely reported as finding no difference between isometric and isotonic for immediate pain). — [Aalborg AM](https://vbn.aau.dk/ws/files/402719184/AM_Isometric_exercise_and_pain_in_patellar_tendinopathy._A_randomized_crossover.pdf); [PubMed 32818059](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32818059/)
- Immediate/short-term effects of short vs long isometric contractions in patellar tendinopathy (Pearson et al. 2018) — [PubMed 30095504](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30095504/)
- Clifford/Millar-type meta-analysis 2020: **isometric exercise not superior to isotonic** for pain in chronic tendinopathy; evidence limited. — [PMC7406028](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7406028/)
- Network meta-analysis (search to 2021): across 3 RCTs isometrics roughly equivalent to isotonic for immediate pain relief, CI spanning no difference. — [PMC8634001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8634001)

### Inferences
- For a lifter, heavy loading that produces high tendon strain (heavy slow resistance, heavy isometrics, normal heavy compound lifting) is the stimulus; the typical powerlifting/strongman programme already supplies it. Extra "tendon work" mainly matters for tissue not loaded heavily in the main lifts.
- Because tendon matrix adapts on a months-to-years scale while muscle strength can climb within weeks, ramp load progression conservatively after layoffs (the mismatch is the plausible injury mechanism; inferred, not directly tested).
- Isometrics remain a reasonable, tolerable loading option for irritable tendons, but should not be sold as uniquely analgesic.
- Gelatin/collagen 10–15 g + ~50 mg vitamin C, 30–60 min before a short loading bout, is low-risk and cheap; evidence certainty is low, so treat as optional.

### Gaps
- Bohm/Arampatzis specific dosing (commonly cited as ~85–90% MVC, ~3 s contractions, 4–5 x 4 reps, 3x/week, 12–14 weeks; and their 2015 J Appl Physiol finding that high strain magnitude with long ~3 s contraction duration beat short/repetitive contractions) — background knowledge, not verified this session.
- Magnusson & Kjaer's work on tendon collagen synthesis peaking ~24 h and remaining elevated ~72 h after exercise, and net collagen loss in the first 1–2 days post-exercise — background knowledge, not verified.
- Heavy slow resistance (Kongsgaard 2009; Beyer 2015) for patellar/Achilles tendinopathy — not searched this session.
- No published human tendon-biopsy results for collagen supplementation (COL-TEN) located; status of JUMPFOOD results unknown.

## Intervertebral disc adaptation to loading in humans

### Takeaway
Cross-sectional data (Belavý 2017) associate regular walking/running with better disc hydration/GAG; the first RCT (ASTEROID, 2025–2026) found 12 weeks of progressive run-walk **improved pain and disability** in chronic non-specific LBP, with a secondary analysis reporting **disc adaptations that appear level-specific**. Evidence that exercise improves disc health is PROMISING but small (n=40). An earlier 6-month Belavý RCT found **no** whole-disc T2 change. Loading thought to be disc-friendly: dynamic, axial, moderate magnitude and speed; static, very heavy, or high-impact loading falls outside the hypothesised "anabolic window" (SPECULATIVE, based on intradiscal-pressure mapping).

### Cited Findings
- Belavý et al. 2017, *Scientific Reports* ("Running exercise strengthens the intervertebral disc"), cross-sectional: **long-distance runners and joggers had higher lumbar disc T2 times (hydration/GAG) than non-athletes**. Using intradiscal pressure data, **walking and running fall within a loading window hypothesised as anabolic; lifting a 20 kg load or lying down did not; fast running and high-impact jumping were above it**. — [Nature srep45975](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep45975)
- Belavý et al. 2020 (*Eur Spine J*), 6-month RCT in chronic LBP with progressive loading exercise: **no group-by-time differences in T2 of the whole disc** (small sample). — [Springer s00586-020-06379-7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00586-020-06379-7)
- ASTEROID protocol (Tagliaferri et al., *BMJ Open SEM* 2023): 12-week digitally delivered progressive interval running, **3 x 30 min/week**, vs waitlist; adults 18–45 with chronic non-specific LBP. — [PMC9853241](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9853241/)
- ASTEROID primary results (Neason et al., *BJSM* 2025, "Running is acceptable and efficacious in adults with non-specific chronic low back pain"): **between-group improvements in pain and disability**. — [RheumNow summary (secondary)](https://rheumnow.com/news/run-walk-exercise-may-help-lower-back-pain)
- Safety: **9 of 20** in the running group had adverse effects considered likely study-related, mostly **knee/ankle pain, none serious**. — [RheumNow (secondary)](https://rheumnow.com/news/run-walk-exercise-may-help-lower-back-pain); ISSLS 2025 abstract reported **no attrition and no study-related serious adverse events**, and that running may improve disc health though **level-specific**. — [ISSLS 2025 abstract](https://issls-2025.p.asnevents.com.au/days/2025-05-15/abstract/115409)
- Pre-planned secondary analysis (*Eur Spine J* 2026, n=40): "Running is associated with intervertebral disc adaptations." — [Springer s00586-026-09759-7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00586-026-09759-7); [VU repository](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/50350/)
- 2024 *Sports Health* systematic review of running and the disc: **transient negative changes (height/water loss) right after running**, likely compression-driven; **cross-sectional studies suggest long-term running has a mild positive effect, not yet confirmed by high-quality longitudinal studies**. — [PubMed 38204324](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38204324/)
- Animal: running exercise alleviated pain and promoted cell proliferation in a rat model of disc degeneration. — [PMC4307353](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4307353/)

### Inferences
- For a lifter with possible disc history, adding progressive walking and run-walk intervals (e.g., 3 x 30 min/week, progressing) is a reasonable, evidence-aligned addition for both back pain and (possibly) disc health; monitor knees/ankles, which were the main side effects.
- The "20 kg lift is outside the anabolic window" point is from pressure mapping, not outcome data; it does **not** show heavy lifting harms discs. There is no human RCT on heavy barbell lifting and disc MRI outcomes in this search.
- Disc changes are level-specific and small; pain/disability improvement is the more robust outcome to target.

### Gaps
- No human trial found on disc outcomes from heavy resistance training, deadlifting, or strongman-type loading.
- The exact disc MRI effect sizes in the 2026 ASTEROID secondary analysis were not visible in snippets.
- Other 2024–2025 Belavý/Owen jumping-based trials (e.g., in women/adolescents) were not located this session.

## Sleep: restriction effects on strength, recovery and pain; targets

### Takeaway
Acute sleep loss reduces maximal strength only **modestly (~3%)**, with larger hits to strength-endurance and when deprivation is total or late-night (ESTABLISHED, Craven 2022). Poor sleep is a **prognostic factor for non-recovery from low back pain** (OR ~1.55, low-quality evidence). Sleep matters more for a returning-LBP lifter as a pain/recovery lever than as an acute 1RM factor.

### Cited Findings
- Craven et al. 2022, *Sports Medicine* meta-analysis of acute sleep loss: **strength overall -2.85% (95% CI -4.47 to -1.23; 66 outcomes)**; total deprivation ~-3.0%; early-night restriction ~-1.2% (not significant); **strength-endurance ~-9.85%** (only 9 outcomes). **Consistent negative effects only with deprivation and late-restriction protocols.** — [PMC9584849](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9584849/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s40279-022-01706-y)
- 2025 meta-analysis (Kong et al., *Front Physiol*, studies to Sept 2024) on sleep deprivation and sports performance/RPE in athletes and non-athletes. — [Front Physiol 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1544286/full)
- Review on sleep loss/deprivation and muscle strength (LJMU repository). — [LJMU](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/27054/1/Implications%20of%20sleep%20loss%20or%20sleep%20deprivation%20on%20muscle%20strength.pdf)
- 2024 *Sleep* systematic review/meta-analysis of prospective cohorts and RCT secondary analyses (14 studies, 19,170 participants; 13 at high risk of bias): **baseline poor sleep moderately associated with not improving in LBP long-term (OR 1.55, 95% CI 1.39–1.73)**; **no association with future disability in the short-to-moderate term**; evidence low to very low quality. — [UTS repository (likely source)](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/183418) (full text not accessed; attribution to this URL not fully confirmed)
- 2021 review of sleep and chronic spinal pain: weak-to-moderate evidence of association; low-to-moderate quality. — [PubMed 34501283](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34501283/)
- 2011 systematic review: CLBP consistently associated with greater sleep disturbance and reduced sleep duration/quality (cross-sectional). — [PubMed 20842008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20842008/)
- 2022 systematic review: low-quality evidence that self-reported sleep-quality improvements track improvements in CLBP disability. — [ResearchGate PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Jeremy-Chang-15/publication/357291287_Are_Changes_in_Sleep_QualityQuantity_or_Baseline_Sleep_Parameters_Related_to_Changes_in_Clinical_Outcomes_in_Patients_With_Nonspecific_Chronic_Low_Back_Pain_A_Systematic_Review/links/635259ea12cbac6a3eded642/Are-Changes-in-Sleep-Quality-Quantity-or-Baseline-Sleep-Parameters-Related-to-Changes-in-Clinical-Outcomes-in-Patients-With-Nonspecific-Chronic-Low-Back-Pain-A-Systematic-Review.pdf)

### Inferences
- One bad night is unlikely to wreck a heavy session (-3% strength); avoid testing maxima or very high-volume/strength-endurance work after major sleep loss.
- For back-pain recovery, protecting sleep quality is a plausible, low-cost lever, though causality is not established.

### Gaps
- Chronic partial restriction effects on hypertrophy/strength gains over weeks (e.g., Saner et al. 2021 MPS work; Knowles 2018) — not searched.
- Practical targets (7–9 h; athletes often advised >=8 h) — background guidance from sleep consensus bodies; not verified this session.

## Protein: daily dose, per-meal dose, energy deficit

### Takeaway
~**1.6 g/kg/day** is the break-point beyond which extra protein added no further fat-free-mass gain on average (ESTABLISHED, Morton 2018); the upper CI (~2.2 g/kg) is a sensible ceiling for those wanting to cover individual variance. Trommelen 2023 showed **100 g** of protein post-exercise produced a larger and longer (>12 h) anabolic response than 25 g, undermining the "~20–40 g per meal max" idea, though it does not mean meal distribution is irrelevant (PROMISING; debated).

### Cited Findings
- Morton et al. 2018 *BJSM* meta-analysis (49 RCTs, 1,863 participants): protein supplementation with resistance training increased **1RM by 2.49 kg (0.64–4.33)** and **FFM by 0.30 kg (0.09–0.52)**; **break-point at 1.62 g/kg/day** total protein beyond which no further FFM gains; effect weaker with age, stronger in resistance-trained individuals. The 1.62 threshold applies to FFM, not strength. — [PubMed 28698222](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/); [PMC5867436](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/)
- Trommelen et al. 2023, *Cell Reports Medicine*: after resistance exercise, 0 vs 25 vs 100 g protein (milk protein); **100 g gave a greater and more prolonged (>12 h) anabolic response**; still rising at 12 h; dietary AAs incorporated into muscle: **18% (4.5 g) of 25 g vs 13% (13 g) of 100 g** by 12 h. Critics note slow-digesting protein and no plateau test; authors published a 2024 reply. — [PMC10772463](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10772463/); [PubMed 38118410](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38118410/)

### Inferences
- Practical: 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day; per-meal amounts can be large without "waste"; 3–5 protein feedings of ~0.3–0.5 g/kg each remains a convenient default.
- In an energy deficit, aim toward the upper end (background; see Gaps).

### Gaps
- Energy deficit protein needs (e.g., Helms 2014: 2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM in lean athletes dieting; ISSN 2017 position stand 1.4–2.0 g/kg; Murphy 2022 meta-analysis on lean mass in deficit) and energy availability/RED-S thresholds — not searched this session; background only.

## Creatine and other supplements

### Takeaway
Creatine monohydrate (3–5 g/day) for strength/lean mass is ESTABLISHED (not re-verified this session). Cognitive benefit: small memory effect (SMD ~0.3), stronger in older adults, low-to-moderate certainty (PROMISING). No connective-tissue-specific evidence was found. Caffeine (3–6 mg/kg) is ESTABLISHED as ergogenic per ISSN (not re-verified this session).

### Cited Findings
- 2023 *Nutrition Reviews* meta-analysis: creatine improved **memory vs placebo (SMD 0.29, 95% CI 0.04–0.53; I^2 66%)**, benefit concentrated in **older adults**; dose (~2.2–20 g/day) and duration (5 days–24 weeks) did not moderate. — [PMC9999677](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9999677)
- 2024 *Frontiers in Nutrition* meta-analysis (Xu et al.; 16 RCTs, 492 participants aged 20.8–76.4): **improved processing speed; no significant effect on overall cognition or executive function**; certainty rated **low**. — [Front Nutr 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1424972/full)
- 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis (PROSPERO CRD42024533557): **memory SMD 0.31 (0.18–0.44)**, GRADE moderate; also a significant attention-time effect. — [PubMed 39070254](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39070254/); [PMC11275561](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11275561/)

### Inferences
- Creatine is the one supplement worth taking daily for this athlete; cognitive upside is a minor bonus.

### Gaps
- ISSN creatine position stand (Kreider 2017) and 2021 "common questions" paper; caffeine ISSN position stand (Guest 2021: 3–6 mg/kg ~60 min pre); weak-evidence supplements (BCAAs, glutamine, tart cherry, omega-3 for recovery, curcumin) — not searched; background only.
- No human evidence located on creatine and tendon/ligament/disc.

## Deloads: definitions, frequency, structure

### Takeaway
No agreed definition exists; consensus is a **short period (typically ~1 week) of reduced training stress** via lower effort, volume, duration or frequency, used to boost readiness. The only RCT (Coleman 2024) used **complete training cessation** for 1 week and found **slightly worse lower-body strength** vs continuous training, no difference in hypertrophy (CONTESTED as to value; the tested deload type is not the type most coaches use).

### Cited Findings
- Bell et al. 2023 international Delphi (*Sports Med Open*): strength/physique coaches over 3 rounds (34 -> 29 -> 21 completers); deloading described as a short-term period of reduced training stress; field lacks an agreed definition. — [PMC10511399](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10511399/)
- Bell et al. 2022 (*Front Sports Act Living*): most commonly reported deload duration **one week (microcycle)**, range **one session to two weeks**; strength athletes commonly taper pre-competition whereas physique athletes do not. — [Front Sports 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2022.1073223/epub)
- Bell et al. 2025 practical recommendations: deload implemented to enhance readiness, between or during training phases; typically reduce **intensity of effort, volume, duration, or frequency**. — [SHURA 35313](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/35313/); [DCU PDF](https://doras.dcu.ie/31501/1/a_practical_approach_to_deloading__recommendations.203%282%29.pdf)
- Rogerson, Nolan, Korakakis, Immonen, Wolf & Bell 2024, "Deloading Practices in Strength and Physique Sports: A Cross-sectional Survey," *Sports Med Open* 10 — findings not visible in snippets. — [SHURA (Bell group)](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/32417)
- Coleman et al. 2024 (*PeerJ*) RCT, 39 trained young adults (29 M, 10 F), 9-week programme, one group **stopped training for 1 week at midpoint**: continuous group had **greater isometric and dynamic lower-body strength gains**; **no differences in muscle size, local endurance or power**; continuous group slightly better readiness-to-train scores. — [PMC10809978](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809978/); [PeerJ 16777](https://peerj.com/articles/16777)

### Inferences
- For a self-coached lifter: prefer a **reduced-volume/reduced-effort** deload (e.g., cut sets ~30–50%, keep some heavy exposure) rather than full rest, and schedule it pragmatically (pre-planned every ~4–8 weeks or autoregulated when performance/readiness drop). The frequency range is coach practice, not trial-derived.

### Gaps
- Exact consensus frequencies from the 2023 Delphi and the 2024 survey (commonly reported as every ~4–6 weeks for ~1 week) not verified in this session.

## Fatigue/readiness monitoring

### Takeaway
No single validated readiness battery for strength athletes exists. Best-supported practical tools are **bar velocity (load-velocity profiles, velocity loss)** and **simple subjective wellness/RIR/RPE**; CMJ is useful where available; HRV evidence in strength athletes is mostly narrative/weak. ACWR has been heavily critiqued and should not be used for injury prediction (CONTESTED, leaning discredited).

### Cited Findings
- Systematic review on velocity-loss (VL) thresholds: higher within-set VL associated with **more hypertrophy but worse CMJ, sprint, and submaximal-velocity performance**; low-to-moderate VL viable to optimise those outcomes. — [BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil 2024 (s13102-024-00807-7)](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/s13102-024-00807-7)
- Autoregulated resistance training scoping review: **11 monitoring/regulation themes across 90 studies**, including jump height and barbell velocity. — [Sports Med (s40279-022-01754-4)](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s40279-022-01754-4)
- HRV in strength and conditioning: narrative review suggests HRV helpful to assess training status/recovery (narrative, not systematic). — [PMC (via search, PMC11204851)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11204851)
- CMJ: in volleyball, weekly CMJ FT:CT ratio, or average jump height, suggested for neuromuscular fatigue (sport-specific). — [PMC9807551](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9807551)
- 2026 *Sports Medicine* structured narrative review: wellness questionnaires, HR-derived markers, CMJ and wearables describe exposure and response; proposes an integrating framework. — [Sports Med 2026 (s40279-026-02417-4)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-026-02417-4)
- ACWR critique: Impellizzeri et al. 2020 (IJSPP): **no evidence supporting ACWR in load-management or injury-reduction recommendations**; Sports Med (Dec 2020) reanalysis replacing chronic load with fixed/random values produced **similar odds ratios** -> statistical artefact; authors recommend **dismissing ACWR**. Lolli et al. 2019 identified mathematical coupling. — [PMC7534938](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938); [Impellizzeri Sports Med 2021 PDF](https://iris.univr.it/retrieve/e34cfb98-e922-4c2f-aab5-18583ab7e31b/Impellizzeri_What%20Role%20Do%20Chronic%20Workloads%20Play_SportMed_2021.pdf); [PMC7485291](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7485291)

### Inferences
- Practical stack for a self-coached lifter: session RPE/RIR + a 3–5 item wellness check (sleep, soreness, back pain, mood/stress) + top-set bar speed or a fixed-load warm-up velocity (if a device is available). Grip/CMJ are optional extras. Track back-pain response 24 h after sessions as the most relevant individual signal.

### Gaps
- Validity data for grip strength as a readiness test in lifters, and for HRV-guided programming in strength athletes (RCTs), not found this session.
- No rebuttal from ACWR proponents located.

## Recovery modalities: cold water immersion, massage, compression

### Takeaway
Post-lifting cold water immersion **modestly blunts hypertrophy** (Piñero 2024) and strength gains (2023 meta-analysis) (ESTABLISHED direction, small magnitude). Avoid CWI right after hypertrophy/strength sessions; use it only when acute recovery before competition matters. Massage/compression were not searched this session.

### Cited Findings
- Piñero et al. 2024, *Eur J Sport Sci* 24(2):177–189 (with Schoenfeld): post-exercise CWI **reduced muscle-size gains vs resistance training alone; effect small**; 8 studies, ~116 participants (mostly young men); training status did not moderate. — [PMC11235606](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11235606/); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/301/603)
- 2023 meta-analysis (*Eur J Sport Sci*): CWI after resistance exercise **attenuates strength gains in males**; whole-body CWI subgroup showed no significant difference. — [PubMed 35068365](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35068365/)

### Inferences
- Heat, massage and compression can be used for comfort; none is likely to change long-term adaptation meaningfully (background).

### Gaps
- Massage (e.g., Dupuy 2018 meta-analysis: massage best for DOMS/perceived fatigue) and compression garment meta-analyses — not searched; background only.
