# Maximal-Strength Programming for Powerlifting and Strongman: Current Evidence (to late 2026)

Evidence labels used throughout: **[ESTABLISHED]** = consistent across multiple meta-analyses; **[PROMISING]** = supported by at least one meta-analysis or RCT but small, heterogeneous or not replicated; **[CONTESTED]** = meta-analyses disagree or the construct is disputed; **[CONVENTION]** = practitioner practice described by surveys, no outcome data.

Method note: Only WebSearch results (abstracts, PubMed/PMC records, repository pages, secondary summaries) were available. Direct page fetching failed (link.springer.com is not reachable from this session's network), so no full texts were read. Numbers are given as reported in abstracts or search excerpts. Where only a secondary source (blog, press release) carried a figure, it is flagged.

## 1. Periodization models: periodized vs non-periodized, and model vs model

### Takeaway
Periodized programs show a small-to-moderate 1RM advantage over non-periodized programs (ES ~0.31 when volume is equated) **[ESTABLISHED, small effect]**. Undulating may beat linear slightly, in trained lifters only **[CONTESTED]**. There is no meta-analytic evidence for block or conjugate/Westside specifically. The construct of "periodization" is itself criticised (Kiely, Afonso). In practice, the benefit may come from *variation plus progressive specificity*, not from any named model.

### Cited Findings
- **Moesgaard et al. 2022, Sports Medicine 52:1647–1666** (35 volume-equated studies). Periodized vs non-periodized: 1RM ES 0.31 (95% CI 0.04–0.57), favouring periodization. Hypertrophy: ES 0.13 (95% CI −0.10 to 0.36), not significant. The authors attribute the strength effect to neurophysiological adaptations rather than muscle growth. — [PubMed 35044672](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/); [Univ. Copenhagen press release](https://news.ku.dk/all_news/2022/04/varied-strength-training-programmes-increase-strength-but-not-muscle-mass)
- Moesgaard 2022, linear (LP) vs undulating (UP): the 1RM comparison favoured UP (ES 0.31), but only in trained, not untrained, participants. Hypertrophy did not differ between LP and UP (ES 0.05, 95% CI −0.20 to 0.29). — [PubMed 35044672](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/); secondary [UNC evidence table](https://dptcapstone.web.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/23235/2023/04/Evidence-Table.pdf). The full-text subgroup detail was not verified.
- **Williams et al. 2017, Sports Medicine** (18 studies, 81 effects, 1988–2015): periodized plans have a *moderate* effect on 1RM vs non-periodized. The authors suggest the advantage may be underestimated because longer studies show greater benefit. (The exact pooled ES was not visible in the excerpts.) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs40279-017-0734-y); [PubMed 28497285](https://www.pubmed.gov/28497285/)
- Williams 2017 is effectively **superseded in magnitude** by Moesgaard 2022. When volume is equated, the advantage shrinks to "small" (0.31). This is consistent with part of Williams' "moderate" effect reflecting volume differences.
- **Harries, Lubans & Callister 2015** meta-analysis: no difference between linear and undulating periodization for upper- or lower-body strength. — [PubMed 25268290](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25268290/). This conflicts with Moesgaard 2022's trained-only UP advantage.
- **Rhea et al. 2002** (n=20, volume- and intensity-equated): DUP > LP at 8 and 12 weeks. This is a small, often-cited single RCT. — [PubMed 11991778](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11991778/)
- **Critiques of periodization as a construct [CONTESTED]:**
  - Afonso et al. 2017, "Is empirical research on periodization trustworthy?" Studies conflate "periodization" with "variation". No studies test the theory's predictions about the direction, timing or magnitude of adaptation. More than 95% study strength. None last longer than 9 months. — [PMC5358028](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5358028/); [JSSM](https://jssm.org/16-1-27.p_d_f)
  - Kiely 2017/2018, "Periodization theory: confronting an inconvenient truth" (Sports Med 48:753–764): periodization rests on outdated stress-theory assumptions and persists culturally. Trials rarely use varied but non-periodized comparators. — cited via [Afonso 2019, Frontiers in Physiology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2019.01023/epub)
  - Afonso et al. 2019 (systematic review of meta-analyses): "variation does not imply periodization". Comparisons of periodized vs *varied* non-periodized programs are scarce. — [Frontiers in Physiology 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2019.01023/epub)
  - Afonso et al. 2020: "nonlinear periodization" often describes nonlinear *programming*, not periodization in the original sense. — [PMC7466683](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7466683)
- **Conjugate/Westside and block:** the searches found no meta-analysis or controlled trial of conjugate/Westside programming for 1RM, and no block-specific meta-analysis. — (negative search result; see Gaps)

### Inferences
- For a self-coached lifter, the practical difference between periodization *models* is small and uncertain. Some planned variation in load and volume, with intensity and specificity rising toward a competition, is supported. Strict allegiance to a named model is not.
- The Moesgaard finding that periodization helps strength but not hypertrophy fits a skill/neural explanation: periodized plans usually include phases of heavier, more competition-specific work.
- The Afonso/Kiely critique matters for *interpretation*: most "periodized vs non-periodized" trials compare against monotonous constant-load training, not against sensible but unplanned variation. So the 0.31 ES may mean "variation beats monotony", not "a pre-planned macrocycle beats responsive planning".

### Gaps
- No 2023–2026 periodization meta-analysis was found (two searches). Moesgaard 2022 remains the latest located synthesis.
- No empirical studies of conjugate/Westside (max-effort/dynamic-effort with rotating exercises) were found. Treat it as **[CONVENTION]**.
- No block-vs-DUP meta-analysis was found. Individual block-periodization RCTs were not retrieved.
- Reverse linear periodization: not searched successfully. No data are reported here.
- The Williams 2017 pooled ES value is not verified.

## 2. Autoregulation (RPE/RIR, VBT, APRE, flexible DUP)

### Takeaway
Autoregulated loading produces 1RM gains **similar to or slightly better than** fixed-percentage loading **[CONTESTED / PROMISING]**. The broadest meta-analysis (Hickmott 2022) found no significant difference (+2.07 kg, p=0.09). Athlete-only meta-analyses (Zhang 2021; a Thieme-published VBT meta-analysis) found advantages. RIR estimates are biased about one rep low on average and are more accurate close to failure and in low-rep sets. That favours using RIR/RPE with heavy, low-rep strength work.

### Cited Findings
- **Hickmott et al. 2022, Sports Medicine – Open** (15 studies): autoregulated vs standardized load prescription gave similar 1RM gains. MD 2.07 kg, p=0.09, not significant. Within VBT, velocity-loss thresholds ≤25% gave slightly greater 1RM than >25% (MD 2.32 kg, 95% CI 0.33–4.31, p=0.02). — [PubMed 35038063](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35038063/); [PMC8762534](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8762534)
- **Zhang et al. 2021, Frontiers in Physiology** (athletes, 166 subjects, 5–10-week interventions): autoregulation was more effective than fixed loading for maximal strength (ES 0.64, p<0.001, I²=0%). — [Frontiers 10.3389/fphys.2021.651112](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.651112/full)
- **Liao et al. 2021, PLoS ONE** (VBT vs percentage-based training): back-squat 1RM difference not significant (MD 3.03 kg, 95% CI −3.55 to 9.61, I²=0%). — [PMC8601436](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8601436/)
- **Thieme-published VBT meta-analysis (Int J Sports Med, ~2022):** VBT "might be more effective" than percentage-based training for maximal strength, but only significantly in athletes (ES 0.35, 95% CI 0.06–0.64, p=0.02, I²=0). — [Thieme 10.1055/a-1790-8546](https://www.thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/a-1790-8546?lang=en)
- **Helms et al. 2018, Frontiers in Physiology** (resistance-trained men aged 19–35; 8 weeks DUP, 3×/week; RPE n=10 vs %1RM n=11; sets and reps matched): both groups improved squat, bench and total 1RM, with no significant between-group difference. The authors say RPE loading "may provide a small 1RM strength advantage in a majority of individuals". — [PubMed 29628895](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29628895/); [PMC5877330](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5877330)
- **RIR accuracy – Halperin et al. 2022, Sports Medicine** (scoping review plus exploratory meta-analysis; 13 publications/12 studies, n=414): lifters *underpredict* reps to failure by about 0.95 reps (95% CI 0.17–1.73). Heterogeneity is very high (I²=97.9%). Predictions are more accurate closer to failure and in sets with fewer total reps to failure. Accuracy improves slightly in later sets. Training status did not clearly influence accuracy. — [PubMed 34542869](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/67)
- Halperin's training-status null result **conflicts with** Steele et al. 2017 (PeerJ), which found accuracy tended to improve with resistance-training experience. — [PMC5712461](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5712461/)

### Inferences
- For heavy powerlifting/strongman work (1–5 reps, RIR 0–3), RIR/RPE estimates fall in the zone where Halperin found the best accuracy. Autoregulating top-set load by RPE is defensible, and so is using RPE-based "load drops" or set caps.
- The modest, inconsistent advantage of autoregulation probably comes from adjusting to daily readiness. That may matter more for a lifter returning from low back pain, whose readiness may vary. Autoregulation also gives a built-in mechanism to back off on bad days without abandoning the plan. (This is an inference: no trials were found in a low-back-pain population.)
- VBT adds objective feedback, but there is no clear 1RM advantage over RPE. Velocity-loss caps of ≤25% look at least as good as higher caps for 1RM, while generating less fatigue.

### Gaps
- APRE (Mann) and "flexible DUP" (Colquhoun/Zourdos-type) were not specifically retrieved. No pooled data are reported here for them.
- The Zourdos et al. 2016 RIR-based RPE scale validation was not retrieved in this session.
- No 2023–2026 autoregulation or VBT meta-analysis was found. A Held et al. network meta-analysis (VBT vs 1RM-based) was noted, but its results were not retrieved.
- Graham & Cleather 2021 (RPE autoregulation in the squat) was searched but not found.

## 3. Dose-response for strength: volume, intensity, proximity to failure, minimum effective dose, frequency

### Takeaway
Strength rises with weekly volume, but with **steeper diminishing returns than hypertrophy** (Pelland et al. 2026). Frequency has an identifiable effect on strength even when volume is matched, unlike hypertrophy. Heavier loads beat light loads for strength (Lopez 2021) **[ESTABLISHED]**. Strength gains are fairly insensitive to proximity to failure across a wide RIR range, while hypertrophy benefits from training closer to failure (Robinson 2024) **[PROMISING; RIR values in the analysis were estimated]**. A minimum dose of about 3–6 heavy sets per lift per week can maintain or modestly build 1RM in powerlifters **[PROMISING; mixed-methods, small trials]**.

### Cited Findings
- **Pelland, Remmert, Robinson, Hinson & Zourdos 2026, Sports Medicine 56:481–505**, "The Resistance Training Dose Response" (Bayesian meta-regressions; 67 studies, 2,058 participants; preprint on SportRxiv 2024):
  - Weekly volume relates positively to both hypertrophy and strength. Strength shows "more pronounced diminishing returns" than hypertrophy.
  - Frequency on a volume-adjusted basis has a *negligible* effect on hypertrophy, but only strength "exhibits consistently identifiable effects" of frequency.
  - — [Springer 10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w); [SportRxiv preprint v2](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Resistance-Training-Dose-Response:-Exploring-of-Pelland-Remmert/09fe56f89e54a93dc0ab4260976825479282d973)
  - Secondary sources only (not verified against the paper): a 100% posterior probability of a positive volume slope for both outcomes; for hypertrophy, about 0.24% gain per added set (95% CrI 0.15–0.33) at a mean of 12.25 fractional sets/week. A claim of "significant diminishing returns at ~31 fractional sets/week" is **unverified**. — [vbtcoach chart summary](https://vbtcoach.com/charts/volume-dose-response-hypertrophy); [Root Strength blog](https://www.rootstrengthseattle.com/blog/training-volume-dose-response)
- **Robinson et al. 2024, Sports Medicine 54(9)**, "Exploring the dose-response relationship between estimated RT proximity to failure, strength gain, and muscle hypertrophy: a series of meta-regressions" (about 55 studies). Strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR. Hypertrophy improved as sets ended closer to failure. RIR was *estimated* from intervention descriptions, and the authors caution that the exact relationships remain unclear. — [PubMed 38393985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/); [Abertay record](https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-the-dose-response-relationship-between-estimated-resist/); [FAU press release](https://www.fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/muscle-growth-strength-study)
- A related trial (Robinson et al. 2024, J Sports Sci): stopping sets at 1–2 RIR produced quadriceps hypertrophy similar to training to momentary failure over 8 weeks. — summarised in the [Robinson 2024 search record](https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-the-dose-response-relationship-between-estimated-resist/). The primary record URL was not retrieved.
- **Load – Lopez et al. 2021, Med Sci Sports Exerc** (network meta-analysis; sets to volitional failure; low >15RM vs moderate 9–15RM vs high ≤8RM): hypertrophy is load-independent. Strength gains are superior with high-load and with moderate-load vs low-load training. — [PubMed 33433148](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433148/); [JYX record](https://jyx.jyu.fi/jyx/Record/jyx_123456789_75929)
- Schoenfeld et al. 2017 (earlier meta-analysis) reached the same conclusion: maximal strength is best served by heavy loads, while hypertrophy is similar across loads. — cited via the [Lopez search record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/)
- **Minimum effective dose – Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021, Frontiers in Sports and Active Living** (often cited as 2020). This mixed-methods work combined elite-coach interviews, surveys and two training interventions in intermediate-advanced powerlifters. It suggests about **3–6 working sets of 1–5 reps per lift per week**, across 1–3 sessions, at **>80% 1RM, RPE 7.5–9.5, for 6–12 weeks**. Autoregulated single sets at RPE 9–9.5 save time but are "less likely" to produce meaningful gains. — [PubMed 34527944](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34527944/); [PMC8435792](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8435792/)
- The same paper's earlier systematic review: a single set of 6–12 reps at 70–85% 1RM, 2–3×/week to failure, for 8–12 weeks gives "suboptimal yet statistically significant" squat and bench 1RM gains. — [PMC8435792](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8435792/)
- **Frequency – Grgic et al. 2018, Sports Medicine.** Strength ES increased with frequency: 0.74 (1×/week), 0.82 (2×), 0.93 (3×), 1.08 (4+×), p=0.003. When volume was equated, frequency had no significant effect, so the authors judged the frequency effect to be largely volume-mediated. — [PubMed/Springer 10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x); [VU repository](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/37695/)
- **Updated by** Pelland 2026, which finds a volume-adjusted frequency effect on strength. Grgic 2018's "no effect when volume-equated" conclusion is therefore **partly superseded** for strength. It still stands for hypertrophy.
- Swinton, Schoenfeld & Murphy 2024 (Sports Medicine) modelled dose-response across outcome domains. They describe Grgic's frequency effect as primarily volume-mediated. — [Springer PDF 10.1007/s40279-024-02006-3](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-024-02006-3.pdf). Its own results were not retrieved.

### Inferences
- For a strength-focused lifter, the cost-benefit curve favours **moderate weekly volume per lift**, spread over **more frequent sessions**, mostly at **>80% 1RM and 1–4 RIR**. Pushing volume or failure proximity mainly buys hypertrophy, not 1RM, and it adds fatigue and spinal loading.
- Because strength is insensitive to proximity to failure, a returning low-back-pain lifter can train the main barbell lifts at RPE 6–8 with little loss of strength stimulus. That reduces the risk of form breakdown near failure (an inference; no low-back-pain-specific data were found). Hypertrophy-oriented accessories, where failure matters more, can be taken closer to failure on spine-sparing machines.
- Minimum-dose data support maintenance blocks during back-pain flare-ups or deloads: about 3–6 heavy sets per lift per week.

### Gaps
- Refalo et al. 2023 (Sports Medicine; failure vs non-failure meta-analysis) was not retrieved. Robinson 2024 covers the same question with meta-regression.
- The exact Pelland 2026 numbers for strength (slope per set, point where returns flatten, frequency effect size) were not verified, because the full text was unreachable.
- Intensity thresholds below 80% 1RM for strength *in trained lifters specifically* were not quantified beyond the Lopez categories.

## 4. Specificity, practice of the 1RM, and neural vs hypertrophic contributions

### Takeaway
A large part of 1RM gain is **task-specific skill and neural adaptation** **[ESTABLISHED in principle; key trials in untrained subjects]**. In "practice the test" designs, performing only 1RM attempts gave 1RM gains equal to high-volume training without the hypertrophy. Heavy, specific practice is non-negotiable for a competitive 1RM. Hypertrophy work builds capacity but transfers incompletely and slowly.

### Cited Findings
- **Mattocks et al. 2017, Med Sci Sports Exerc**, "Practicing the test produces strength equivalent to higher volume training". Participants were randomised to high-volume hypertrophy training or to only performing the 1RM test. 1RM change (upper and lower body) did not differ between groups. Neither volume nor change in muscle size contributed to greater strength gains. — [PubMed 28463902](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28463902/)
- A 2017 Muscle & Nerve study (21 days of test familiarisation vs traditional training): 1RM rose similarly in both arms, but only the training arm gained muscle thickness. 1RM gains were attributed to specificity and non-hypertrophic mechanisms. — [PubMed 27875635](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27875635/)
- Buckner, Jessee, Mattocks et al. (Sports Medicine commentary, "Determining strength: a case for multiple methods"): when strength is measured with a test both groups are naive to, differences between training groups shrink. 1RM gains are heavily test-specific. — [Springer 10.1007/s40279-016-0580-3](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s40279-016-0580-3)
- Moesgaard 2022: periodization improves strength but not hypertrophy, which the authors attribute to neurophysiological adaptation. — [PubMed 35044672](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/)
- Amdi et al. 2025 surveyed 548 powerlifters on exercise specificity. Programming choices varied with sex, equipment use, age, weight class, relative strength and training status. This is descriptive and **[CONVENTION]**. — [PubMed 41166696](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41166696/)

### Inferences
- In practice: include regular heavy, competition-specific exposures (singles, doubles and triples at RPE 7–9 in the competition lifts and key strongman events) throughout the year, not only in the peak. These exposures train the skill component.
- The specificity data come mainly from untrained or recreational participants over a few weeks. In trained lifters, the relative contribution of hypertrophy to long-term 1RM progress is probably larger (muscle size sets the ceiling). Long-term career data were not found, so this remains an inference.
- For strongman, the specificity principle implies that implement and event practice (stones, yoke, log, farmers) is needed for event performance, regardless of barbell strength. It also carries injury risk (see section 8).

### Gaps
- No studies were found that quantify how much accessory or hypertrophy work transfers to 1RM in trained powerlifters, or the career-long split between hypertrophy and neural contributions.
- No RCTs of heavy-singles frequency in trained powerlifters were retrieved.

## 5. Rest intervals between sets

### Takeaway
For strength, **longer rest (about 3–5 min) between heavy sets** is supported by older reviews **[ESTABLISHED by convention plus older reviews; little new data]**. Singer et al. 2024 addresses hypertrophy only: it found a small benefit to resting more than 60 s and nothing further beyond 90 s. No recent strength-specific meta-analysis was located.

### Cited Findings
- **Singer et al. 2024, "Give it a rest", Frontiers in Sports and Active Living** (Bayesian meta-analysis; 19 measurements from 9 studies). Hypertrophy only. There was a small hypertrophic benefit to rest >60 s, possibly mediated by better volume load. No appreciable difference beyond 90 s. Training to failure did not modify the effect. The authors question NSCA's 30–90 s hypertrophy recommendation. — [PubMed 39205815](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39205815/); [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/full)
- **Willardson 2006** (review): for strength training with loads below 90% 1RM, 3–5 min rest allows greater strength gains by preserving training intensity. — [PubMed 17194236](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17194236/)
- **de Salles et al. 2009, Sports Medicine** (review): 3–5 min rest produced greater absolute-strength gains because of higher training intensities and volumes. About 1 min may suffice between *maximal test attempts*. — [PubMed 19691365](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19691365/)
- Grgic et al. 2017 (Eur J Sport Sci) reviewed short vs long rest for *hypertrophy*, not strength. — [PubMed 28641044](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28641044/)

### Inferences
- Rest 3–5 min between heavy compound sets, and longer between near-maximal strongman event runs. Shorter rest only on accessories.
- For a lifter with back pain, longer rest also helps keep the technique and bracing quality of every heavy set (an inference).

### Gaps
- The search found no 2020–2026 meta-analysis of rest interval *on maximal strength* in trained lifters. The commonly repeated "3+ min for strength" rests on reviews from 2006–2009 and on a few RCTs (e.g., Schoenfeld 2016), which were not retrieved here.

## 6. Concurrent training interference

### Takeaway
Aerobic training added to a strength program **does not meaningfully blunt maximal strength or whole-muscle hypertrophy** (Schumann 2022, 43 studies) **[ESTABLISHED for recreational/general populations]**. **Explosive strength is attenuated**, especially when both are done in the same session. Separating sessions by ≥3 h removes the significant effect. There may be modest blunting of fibre-level hypertrophy, more with running than with cycling.

### Cited Findings
- **Schumann et al. 2022, Sports Medicine**, "Compatibility of concurrent aerobic and strength training for skeletal muscle size and function" (43 studies). No significant interference with maximal strength or hypertrophy, regardless of aerobic modality, frequency, training status or age. Explosive strength gains were attenuated, more so in the same session (p=0.043). Separating sessions by ≥3 h made the effect non-significant. — [PubMed 34757594](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/); [JCU full-text PDF](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/71546/1/Schumann2021_Article_CompatibilityOfConcurrentAerob.pdf)
- **Lundberg, Feuerbacher, Sünkeler & Schumann 2022** (follow-up subgroup analysis): concurrent training may modestly blunt muscle *fibre* hypertrophy, possibly more with running than cycling (type I fibres). — [PMC9474354](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9474354)

### Inferences
- For strongman, conditioning (medleys, sled, carries, low-impact cycling) is compatible with maximal-strength goals. Prefer cycling or sled over high-volume running. Keep conditioning ≥3 h away from, or on separate days from, explosive or heavy work (log clean, stones, speed pulls).
- For a lifter with low back pain, low-impact aerobic work (walking, cycling) is a sensible general-health and recovery adjunct, with no evidence of harm to 1RM (an inference).

### Gaps
- Few concurrent-training studies involve highly trained strength athletes or strongman-style anaerobic conditioning (medleys, carries). The Schumann data mostly involve continuous or interval aerobic work in non-elite populations.
- No 2023–2026 updates to the Schumann analysis were retrieved.

## 7. Peaking and tapering (powerlifting and strongman)

### Takeaway
The experimental evidence is thin **[PROMISING / CONVENTION]**. Reviews recommend a taper of **about 1–2 weeks** with **volume-load cut by about 30–50%** while **intensity is maintained**. Surveys show lifters typically use a 7–10-day step taper that cuts volume-load by 41–50%. Most strongman athletes taper, usually with a step or linear taper.

### Cited Findings
- **Travis et al. 2020, Sports**, "Tapering and peaking maximal strength for powerlifting performance: a review". There is no settled evidence-based protocol, and practitioners rely on trial and error. The optimal taper may be ≤2 weeks, with volume-load roughly halved and intensity maintained or reduced. — [ETSU record](https://dc.etsu.edu/etsu-works-2/370); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/886093729f784abda2365f6ae95125f9)
- **Travis, Pritchard et al. 2021** (survey of 364 US/Canadian raw powerlifters): the most common taper was a 7–10-day step taper with a 41–50% reduction in volume-load. Intensity changes varied widely (−30% to +10%). — [ETSU record](https://dc.etsu.edu/etsu-works-2/366)
- **Pritchard et al. 2016, JSCR** (tapering in New Zealand's elite raw powerlifters): a descriptive survey. — [PubMed 26670988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26670988/)
- **Winwood, Pritchard et al. 2018** (tapering practices of strongman athletes, n=454): the first empirical description of strongman tapering. Most athletes tapered before major competitions, with step and linear tapers the most common. — [Bond University record](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/tapering-practices-of-strongman-athletes/)
- **Rogerson et al. 2024, Sports Medicine – Open** (deloading survey, 246 competitive strength and physique athletes, including strongman): all athletes deloaded, mainly for energy and fatigue management. In strength sports, the most common deload cut volume while maintaining or reducing intensity. — [Springer 10.1186/s40798-024-00691-y](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-024-00691-y); [SHU PDF](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/33446/1/s40798-024-00691-y.pdf)

### Inferences
- Practical default: a final heavy exposure (openers or near-openers) about 7–10 days out, then reduce volume about 40–60% while keeping some heavy, low-rep work. For strongman, reduce event volume and drop high-fatigue implements (stones, heavy carries) 7–14 days out. Most of this is **[CONVENTION]** informed by surveys.

### Gaps
- No RCTs comparing taper lengths or structures in powerlifters were found in this session, beyond the reviews' note that "only a couple" exist.
- Strongman-specific peaking data are limited to surveys.

## 8. Strongman-specific evidence: implement training, injury epidemiology, programming practices

### Takeaway
Strongman evidence is **mostly descriptive** (surveys from 2011–2018) **[CONVENTION]**. Injury risk is real: 82% of athletes reported an injury, at about 5.5 injuries per 1,000 training hours. The lower back and shoulder are the most affected sites. **Exposure-adjusted risk was about 1.9× higher during implement training** than during traditional gym lifts. This is directly relevant for a lifter returning from low back pain.

### Cited Findings
- **Winwood, Hume, Cronin & Keogh 2014, JSCR** (retrospective injury epidemiology, n=213): 82% reported injuries; about 1.6 training injuries per lifter per year; 5.5 per 1,000 training hours. Most were to the lower back and shoulder, mainly muscle or tendon strains and tears, mostly acute and of moderate severity. Most injuries occurred in traditional lifts, but exposure-adjusted risk was **1.9× higher in strongman implement training**. The data are self-reported 1-year recall. — [ECU record](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/298); [Bond record](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/retrospective-injury-epidemiology-of-strongman-athletes/); [SRKnowledge summary](https://srknowledge.org.nz/research-completed/retrospective-injury-epidemiology-of-strongman-athletes)
- **Winwood, Keogh & Harris 2011, JSCR** (strength and conditioning practices of 167 strongman competitors; 65-item questionnaire): 80% used some form of periodization. 97% included maximal-strength training, 90% power training, 74% hypertrophy training, and 88% Olympic lifts or derivatives. — [Bond record](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/the-strength-and-conditioning-practices-of-strongman-competitors/); [SRKnowledge](https://www.srknowledge.org.nz/?p=2603)
- **Winwood 2015 PhD thesis (AUT; Cronin & Keogh)**: 88% of coaches (n=193) used strongman implements. Sleds, ropes, kettlebells, tyres, sandbags and farmers bars were the top six. — [AUT open repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/handle/10292/8437)
- No dedicated strongman programming survey from 2023–2025 was found. The 2024 deloading survey (Rogerson) is the most recent source that includes strongman athletes. — [Springer 10.1186/s40798-024-00691-y](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-024-00691-y)

### Inferences
- The lower back is the top injury site, and implements carry about twice the per-hour risk. A returning low-back-pain lifter should introduce implements gradually: low load, short exposures, perfect setup. Start with the more forgiving events (sled, farmers at moderate load, yoke at moderate load) before stones, max-load deadlift variants and keg or sandbag loading.
- Gym-based barbell strength can carry most of the strength development. Implement work should be dosed as *skill practice plus event specificity*, not volume.

### Gaps
- There are no prospective strongman injury cohorts and no RCTs of strongman programming for 1RM or event performance in this search. Data from 2018–2026 beyond deloading were not found.
- Keogh's later strongman reviews (e.g., implements for general S&C) were not retrieved in detail.

## 9. Managing spinal load for a lifter returning from non-specific low back pain

### Takeaway
High-load lifting (the deadlift) is a viable rehabilitation exercise for mechanical low back pain, but it is **not superior** to low-load motor-control exercise. It works best for those with **lower baseline pain and disability and better back-extensor endurance** **[PROMISING; small RCT]**. Combined with section 3, this supports a graded return: lower RPE, moderate volume, high frequency and autoregulation, with progressive exposure to heavy spinal loading.

### Cited Findings
- **Aasa, Berglund, Michaelson et al. 2015, JOSPT** (RCT, n=70; high-load lifting (deadlift) plus education vs individualised low-load motor control plus education, in mechanical/nociceptive low back pain): the high-load arm started with an unloaded bar and a neutral spine. The groups' outcomes were broadly similar. — [PubMed 25641309](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25641309/); [ClinicalTrials NCT01061632](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01061632)
- **Berglund et al. 2015, secondary analysis** (35 deadlift participants): those with less disability, lower pain intensity and higher Biering-Sørensen back-extensor endurance at baseline benefited from deadlift training. The authors recommend screening for these factors. — [PubMed 25559899](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25559899/)
- 24-month follow-up of the same trial (J Rehabil Med 2016). — [Medical Journals Sweden](https://www.medicaljournals.se/jrm/content/html/10.2340/16501977-2091); [PubMed 27870804](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27870804/)
- 2021 clinical review (3 studies): programs including deadlifts improve pain and function, but are not more beneficial than low-load motor control. — [PubMed 33626500](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33626500/)

### Inferences
- The evidence supports returning to heavy spinal loading rather than avoiding it, provided pain and disability are low and back-extensor endurance is reasonable.
- Programming levers with evidence of little cost to 1RM:
  1. Stop sets further from failure (Robinson 2024).
  2. Use minimum-dose volume during flare-ups (Androulakis-Korakakis 2021).
  3. Train more frequently with fewer sets per session (Pelland 2026 frequency effect).
  4. Autoregulate daily load with RPE/RIR or velocity (Hickmott 2022: no loss vs fixed loading).
  5. Rest longer between sets to preserve technique.

  Hypertrophy for the spinal erectors and hips can come from lower-load or machine variations, because hypertrophy is load-independent (Lopez 2021).
- Strongman implements carry about twice the per-hour injury risk, with the lower back the main site (Winwood 2014). Phase them in after barbell tolerance is re-established.

### Gaps
- No studies were found on returning *competitive* strength athletes to heavy lifting after non-specific low back pain, or on load-progression rates. The guidance above is extrapolated from rehabilitation trials in general mechanical low back pain populations, plus dose-response data in healthy lifters.
- Data on lifting belts and intra-abdominal pressure for spinal load management were not searched.
